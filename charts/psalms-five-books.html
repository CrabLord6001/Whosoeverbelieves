The Psalms belong to Act 5, the Age of Israel under the Law. Most were written in the days of David and the kings, and the collection was finished after the return from exile. Many look ahead to Act 7, when David’s Son will rule the nations (Psalm 2:7–8; Psalm 72:8).

At a Glance

WhoDavid wrote 73 psalms by their titles; Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman and Ethan wrote others; 50 name no author
WhatIsrael’s songbook and prayer book: 150 songs of lament, praise, thanks, trust and wisdom
WhereSung in Israel’s worship at the tabernacle and temple in Jerusalem; a few come from exile137:1
WhenAbout a thousand years, from Moses to the return from BabylonPs 90
WhyTo teach God’s people to bring every feeling to Him, and to look ahead to the Messiah, David’s Son, who will reign110:1
HowFive books, each closing in praise, moving from lament toward the final “Praise Yah!”150:6

Notes & Sources

  1. Types follow common categories used by evangelical commentators. Many psalms mix types (Psalm 22 moves from lament to praise), so each square shows the main type, and the tooltip lists any other.
  2. Author counts are taken from the psalm titles as printed in the World English Bible. The titles are part of the Hebrew text.
  3. Book II ends with “This ends the prayers by David, the son of Jesse” (Psalm 72:20), though more psalms by David appear later. The note likely marks the close of an earlier collection.
  4. Jewish tradition compares the five books of Psalms to the five books of Moses (Midrash Tehillim on Psalm 1).
  5. Psalms 42–83 are often called the “Elohistic Psalter” because they mostly use the name God (Elohim) where the rest of the Psalter uses Yahweh.
