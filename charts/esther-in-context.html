Esther belongs to Act 5, the Age of Israel from Moses to Messiah, in the scene of Israel’s captivity and return. Most Jews stayed in Persia after the exile, and the book shows God keeping His promises to them (Genesis 12:3) even while His name goes unmentioned.

Background

The Greek Additions

The Hebrew book of Esther never mentions God. A Greek translation made a few centuries later added six passages, 107 verses in all, that bring God’s name, prayers and a prophetic dream into the story. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include them. Protestant and Jewish Bibles follow the Hebrew.

Where they came from

A translation from Egypt

A note at the end of the Greek book says it was brought to Egypt “in the fourth year of the reign of Ptolemeus and Cleopatra,” and that it was translated by Lysimachus in Jerusalem. The two decrees (B and E) are written in polished Greek rhetoric, quite unlike the Hebrew book. The table below shows which Ptolemy the note may mean.

Jerome set them apart

Translating the Latin Vulgate from the Hebrew around AD 400, Jerome moved the additions to the end of the book and noted that they were not in the Hebrew. That is why older Bibles number them Esther 10:4–16:24.

Trent made them canonical

In 1546 the Council of Trent accepted the books of the Vulgate “entire with all their parts,” additions included. The King James Version of 1611 placed them in its Apocrypha as “the rest of the chapters of the Book of Esther, which are found neither in the Hebrew nor in the Chaldee.”

The case for them

Catholic and Orthodox Christians receive them because the early church read the Old Testament largely in Greek, and the Septuagint included them. Josephus retells several of them in the first century (see below). Many readers value the prayers for their devotion.

Several Ptolemies ruled alongside a Cleopatra, so the note fits more than one king. On any of them, the Greek was made three to four centuries after the events of 483–473 BC.

What Josephus shows

The Jewish historian Josephus retells Esther around AD 93 in his Antiquities of the Jews (book 11, chapter 6). His account is the earliest outside witness to the additions, and it cuts both ways.

  • He knew some of them. He weaves in the two decrees, the prayers of Mordecai and Esther, and Esther fainting before the king (B–E), so those were in circulation by the first century.
  • He left others out. Mordecai’s dream and its interpretation (A and F) are missing, and he retells freely in his own words. He treated the Greek material as sources for a history, not as a fixed sacred text.
  • He follows the Greek on the king. The Greek calls the king Artaxerxes, and Josephus makes him Xerxes’ son, “Cyrus, whom the Greeks called Artaxerxes.” That would move the story after Ezra’s return in 458 BC. The Hebrew name Ahasuerus is the Hebrew form of Xerxes, which fits the dates in Esther and the Persian records (see the timeline above).
  • His own canon excludes them. In Against Apion (1.8) Josephus counts 22 sacred books written from Moses to the reign of Artaxerxes, and says that later writings were not held in equal esteem because the succession of prophets had ended. By his own standard, passages written in Greek in Egypt centuries later do not qualify.

Why they are not part of Scripture

  • They are not in the Hebrew Bible. God entrusted His word to Israel (Romans 3:2), and the Hebrew Esther has no trace of these passages. Jesus spoke of the Hebrew Scriptures as “the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms” (Luke 24:44). See How Jesus Bookended the Old Testament.
  • They came centuries later, in another language. The events took place in 483–473 BC. The additions were written in Greek in the second or first century BC.
  • They contradict the book they were added to. The table above shows the conflicts. Addition A even disagrees with the Greek translation it was placed in front of.
  • The text was still growing. There are two Greek versions of Esther, the Septuagint and the so-called Alpha text, and they differ widely. That is the mark of a retelling being expanded, not a fixed text being copied.
  • They miss the point of the book. The Hebrew Esther shows God at work without naming Him: a sleepless night, a chronicle read at the right moment (Esther 6:1), and a queen in the right place “for such a time as this” (Esther 4:14). The additions spell out what the author chose to leave unspoken, and the book’s quiet witness to God’s providence is lost.

Notes & Sources

  1. Regnal years are counted from Nisan (spring). Xerxes’ 3rd year is 483 BC, his 7th is 479/478, and his 12th is 474/473. The 13th of Adar in his 12th year falls in early 473 BC.
  2. Persian reign dates follow the standard chronology built from Babylonian records (Parker & Dubberstein, Babylonian Chronology).
  3. “Ahasuerus” is the Hebrew form of the Persian name the Greeks wrote as Xerxes.
  4. The temple was completed on Adar 3 in Darius’s 6th year (Ezra 6:15), which most chronologies place in early 515 BC. Some reckon it as 516.
  5. Nehemiah’s return and the decree to rebuild Jerusalem are dated to 445 BC, the starting point of Daniel’s seventy weeks (Daniel 9:25; Nehemiah 2:1–8). See Daniel’s Seventy Weeks.
  6. The Greek battles and Xerxes’ death come from the Greek historians, chiefly Herodotus. Esther does not mention the war.
  7. On the date of the Greek translation, see Elias Bickerman, “The Colophon of the Greek Book of Esther,” Journal of Biblical Literature 63 (1944), which argued for 78/77 BC. Josephus’s retelling is in Antiquities 11.6; his count of the sacred books is in Against Apion 1.8.
  8. The Greek additions are labeled A–F, as in most modern editions, with the Vulgate and King James Apocrypha chapter numbers given for reference. Quotations from them follow the World English Bible’s Greek Esther.
  9. Most interpreters read “Agagite” as a descendant of Agag the Amalekite; Jewish tradition and Josephus take it that way.
