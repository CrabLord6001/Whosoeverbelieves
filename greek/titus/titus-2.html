Introduction to Titus 2

Titus 2 answers chapter 1's problem with a household. Against teachers who overturn whole houses (1:11), Paul sets out what a healthy one looks like, group by group — older men, older women, younger women, younger men, servants (vv. 1–10) — and then grounds the whole list in the appearing of grace (vv. 11–14). Verses 11–14 are the most contested paragraph in the letter, because παιδεύουσα in v. 12 is routinely offered as proof that grace guarantees a transformed life in everyone who receives it. The grammar repays slowing down. Grace trains ἡμᾶς, those who already have it, and v. 14 states the purpose of the atonement rather than its measurable result in each person. Verse 13 carries a separate weight of its own as one of the clearest grammatical statements of Christ's deity in the New Testament. The Greek text follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with NA28 divergences noted where they occur.

The emphatic Σὺ δέ opens the chapter by setting Titus against the men of 1:10–16. The pronoun is unnecessary for the grammar, so it carries contrast: they teach what they ought not, you teach this.

λάλει is a present imperative, which makes the charge a standing practice rather than a single address. The relative ἀ is neuter plural and the subject of πρέπει, a verb of fitness, so what Titus says must suit τῇ ὑγιαινούσῃ διδασκαλίᾳ. The participle is the same medical word used in 1:9 and 1:13, and Wilkin notes that sound teaching is what makes a local church healthy, much of it reaching the congregation through the elders rather than directly.

The accusative πρεσβύτας with the infinitive εἶναι continues the content of λάλει in v. 1, an indirect command. Wilkin counts four groups in vv. 2–10, and this is the first.

The Byzantine νηφαλέους against NA28's νηφαλίους is a spelling difference only. The word is literally wineless and had come to mean clear-headed. σεμνός is dignified, σώφρων the same adjective as 1:8. Then the participle ὑγιαίνοντας governs three datives of reference, τῇ πίστει, τῇ ἀγάπῃ, τῇ ὑπομονῇ, each article repeated for weight. Wilkin notes that ὑπομονή stands where Paul's familiar triad has hope, and he reads the endurance as itself hopeful, since it is sustained by the expectation of v. 13.

ὡσαύτως carries the construction of v. 2 across to the older women without repeating the infinitive. ἱεροπρεπεῖς compounds the temple with what is fitting, so the bearing in view is the kind that would suit a sacred setting, and Wilkin notes it is a different word from the σεμνός applied to the men. κατάστημα is demeanor, the whole visible bearing rather than a single act.

Two prohibitions follow. διαβόλους is the word used in the singular of the devil, and Wilkin draws the point that the plural here describes using the tongue in a devilish way rather than naming a class of being. The perfect passive participle δεδουλωμένας is strong, describing a settled state of enslavement to wine rather than an occasional lapse. The positive counterpart, καλοδιδασκάλους, occurs only here in the New Testament and is spelled out in vv. 4–5.

The purpose clause uses σωφρονίζωσιν, a causative built on the σώφρων root, so the older women are to bring the younger to sound-mindedness. The subject of the verb is the older women of v. 3, which is the structural point Wilkin presses: Titus is not told to instruct the young women himself, and neither are the elders. The teaching here runs woman to woman.

τὰς νέας is substantival, the young ones, and the two compounds that follow are the content of the training. φιλάνδρους and φιλοτέκνους are both formed on φιλέω, and both occur only here in the New Testament. That the affection has to be taught is itself the assumption of the verse.

Five more accusatives continue the list from v. 4. οἰκουρούς is the chapter's one textually consequential divergence. The Byzantine text has οἰκουρός, a keeper of the house, where NA28 reads οἰκουργός, a worker at home, and the two differ by a single letter. Wilkin, working from the Majority Text, glosses the word as homemaker and allows worker at home as an alternative. The reading also matters for the audio on this page, which follows the Westcott–Hort text and so pronounces the NA28 form rather than the Byzantine one printed above.

ὑποτασσομένας is a present passive participle, and Wilkin observes that loving one's husband in v. 4 and submitting to him here are not treated as the same thing, since Paul lists them separately. The clause closes with the purpose, ἵνα μὴ ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ βλασφημῆται. The subject of the blaspheming is left unstated, which is the point: outsiders are watching.

τοὺς νεωτέρους is a comparative form used substantivally, the younger men as against the older of v. 2. Wilkin places the dividing line around forty, with the younger group running from about twenty.

The verse is one clause and one command, παρακάλει σωφρονεῖν, and the brevity is conspicuous. Seven things are urged on the younger women across vv. 4–5 and one on the younger men. Wilkin notes the imbalance and declines to explain it, which is the right restraint, since Paul gives no reason.

The imperative force carries over into the middle participle παρεχόμενος, and the middle voice matters, since Titus is to furnish himself as the exhibit. τύπος is a stamp or mold, the pattern from which other impressions are taken.

The three accusatives at the end of the verse carry two textual questions, and Wilkin flags both from the Majority Text side. The Byzantine ἀδιαφθορίαν stands where NA28 reads ἀφθορίαν, and the third noun ἀφθαρσίαν is absent from NA28 altogether. The words are close in sense, covering incorruption and soundness, and nothing in the paragraph turns on which is read.

λόγον ὑγιῆ ἀκατάγνωστον continues the accusatives of v. 7. ἀκατάγνωστος is a legal word, not open to a charge being brought down against it, and it occurs only here in the New Testament.

ὁ ἐξ ἐναντίας is an articular prepositional phrase used substantivally, the one on the opposing side. ἐντραπῇ is a second aorist passive subjunctive meaning to be put to shame, and the shame is the intended effect of sound speech rather than of argument. The closing phrase deserves a note Wilkin supplies from the manuscripts: both the Majority Text and the critical text read περὶ ἡμῶν, about us, where some English versions following the Textus Receptus read about you. That divergence is not between the Byzantine text and NA28, which agree here, and so it does not appear in the apparatus below. The Byzantine word order περὶ ἡμῶν λέγειν against NA28's λέγειν περὶ ἡμῶν carries no difference in meaning.

Δούλους is fronted, and the infinitive ὑποτάσσεσθαι again depends on λάλει from v. 1 as indirect command. The middle voice places the ordering of oneself under another as something the servant does rather than something done to him.

ἰδίοις δεσπόταις uses the harsher word for master, from which the English despot comes, and ἴδιος limits the submission to one's own master rather than to masters generally. Wilkin cautions against collapsing the ancient institution into modern employment, since these were people held as property. εὐαρέστους is well-pleasing, and the negated participle μὴ ἀντιλέγοντας uses the same verb that described the opponents of 1:9, which quietly links the two.

The purpose clause supplies the verse's memorable image. κοσμῶσιν is the verb behind the English cosmetic, meaning to arrange or adorn, and its object is not the servant's own reputation but τὴν διδασκαλίαν τοῦ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν θεοῦ. Wilkin's observation is that doctrine lived out is more attractive to an unbelieving master than doctrine argued. The Byzantine text reads τοῦ where NA28 has τὴν τοῦ, which only adjusts the article.

Ἐπεφάνη is a second aorist passive of ἐπιφαίνω, a verb used of a light breaking into view, and it is the same root as ἐπιφάνειαν in v. 13. Grace appeared once and will appear again. The γάρ grounds the whole household code of vv. 1–10 in that appearing.

The verse carries a real divergence. The Byzantine text reads ἡ χάρις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ σωτήριος with the article before σωτήριος, which makes the adjective attributive, the saving grace of God. NA28 drops the article, leaving σωτήριος predicative and yielding the familiar rendering that grace appeared bringing salvation to all men, which is the line the WEB follows above. The difference is real but modest, since both make grace the saving thing. Wilkin takes the salvation here to be the cleansing that accompanies regeneration rather than regeneration itself, reading it alongside 3:5, and he takes πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις to mean that grace is available to all rather than that all have heard of it.

This is the verse most often set against a Free Grace reading, and the grammar repays attention. παιδεύουσα is a present active participle, feminine nominative singular, agreeing with ἡ χάρις in v. 11. Grace is the trainer. The verb covers the whole range of bringing up a child, instruction and correction together, so training renders it better than either teaching or chastening alone.

Two things in the construction bear on the argument. The object is ἡμᾶς, us, so the training is directed at people who already have the grace rather than offered as the test of whether they have it. And the result is expressed by ἵνα … ζήσωμεν, a purpose clause with an aorist subjunctive, which states what the training is for and not what it infallibly produces in each case. The aorist participle ἀρνησάμενοι is antecedent, so the denying precedes the living. Three adverbs then cover the whole field, σωφρόνως toward oneself, δικαίως toward others and εὐσεβῶς toward God. Wilkin reads the whole as addressed to believers, set against exactly what the false teachers were encouraging, and he contrasts the orientation with Demas, who loved this present age.

προσδεχόμενοι is a present participle of continuous expectation, and Wilkin takes it as a daily looking rather than an occasional one. μακαρίαν attributes blessedness to the hope itself.

The closing phrase is among the strongest grammatical arguments for the deity of Christ in the New Testament. τοῦ μεγάλου θεοῦ καὶ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ places a single article before two singular personal nouns joined by καί, which is the construction Granville Sharp described and Wallace has defended at length. In that construction the two nouns refer to one person, so the great God and our Savior are both Jesus Christ. Two further details support it here. ἡμῶν is placed after the second noun rather than between them, and Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ stands in apposition to the whole. The appearing in view is also consistently Christ's elsewhere in Paul, which makes a reading that splits the phrase between two persons harder to sustain.

ὃς ἔδωκεν ἑαυτὸν states the atonement in the plainest terms, and ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν is the substitutionary preposition, on behalf of and in place of. Wilkin names the doctrine directly here.

Two aorist subjunctives then give the purpose. λυτρώσηται is the ransom word and its object is ἀπὸ πάσης ἀνομίας, every lawless deed, which is a redemption from the practice of sin rather than from its penalty alone. καθαρίσῃ follows with λαὸν περιούσιον, a phrase taken from the Greek Old Testament for a people belonging to God as his own possession. The force of the sentence for a Free Grace reader lies in what ἵνα does. These are the ends for which Christ gave himself, not a measurement applied to individuals afterward. Wilkin reads the second purpose as progressive sanctification, and he pairs it with v. 13, so that believers resist the false teachers by looking back to the cross and forward to the appearing.

Overall Exegesis: Titus 2

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The chapter has two halves joined by a single conjunction. Verses 1–10 are a household code arranged by group, older men, older women, younger women, younger men and servants, and the grammar runs on one verb. λάλει in v. 1 governs a chain of accusatives with infinitives all the way to v. 10, so nothing in that list is a free-standing command. Verses 11–14 are then a single sentence introduced by γάρ, which makes the appearing of grace the ground of everything just required. Verse 15 closes with three imperatives that gather the whole.

The construction matters for reading vv. 11–14. They are not a new topic but the reason the household code is possible, and the participles of vv. 12–13 hang on ἡ χάρις in v. 11 rather than on the believers.

II. Key Theological Themes

The chapter turns on two appearings. ἐπεφάνη in v. 11 and ἐπιφάνειαν in v. 13 share a root, so the believer's present life is bracketed by a grace that has already appeared and a glory that has not yet. Verse 12's ἐν τῷ νῦν αἰῶνι sits between them.

The disputed word is παιδεύουσα. Read as proof that grace infallibly produces godliness, it would make v. 12 a test of whether grace has been received. Three features of the grammar resist that. The participle agrees with grace and not with the believer, so grace is the trainer rather than the evidence. Its object is ἡμᾶς, people who already have what is training them. And the outcome is carried by ἵνα with an aorist subjunctive, which states an aim. The same restraint applies to v. 14, where the two ἵνα clauses name what Christ gave himself for. A purpose clause describes intent, not an audit.

Verse 13 carries a weight of its own. The single article governing μεγάλου θεοῦ and σωτῆρος is the Granville Sharp construction, and it identifies the great God and our Savior as one person, Jesus Christ.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

Household codes of this kind appear elsewhere in Paul, at Ephesians 5–6 and Colossians 3–4, and the Cretan version is distinguished by how much of the teaching runs sideways rather than down. The older women train the younger directly, and Titus is not placed between them. The instructions to servants in vv. 9–10 address people held as property rather than employees, and the point Paul makes of them is the striking one, that the least powerful people in the household are the ones who κοσμῶσιν, adorn, the teaching about God.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 11 — ἡ σωτήριος. The chapter's one divergence with a translational consequence. The Byzantine article makes the adjective attributive, the saving grace of God; NA28 omits it, leaving the adjective predicative and yielding the common rendering that grace appeared bringing salvation to all. Both make grace the saving thing, so the difference is of emphasis.

v. 5 — οἰκουρούς. Byzantine οἰκουρός, a keeper of the house, against NA28's οἰκουργός, a worker at home, differing by one letter. This is the one place in the chapter where the audio and the printed text diverge audibly, since the reading follows Westcott–Hort.

v. 7 — ἀδιαφθορίαν and ἀφθαρσίαν. NA28 reads ἀφθορίαν for the first and omits the third noun altogether. All three words cover incorruption and soundness.

vv. 2, 8, 10 — minor variations. In v. 2 the Byzantine νηφαλέους and NA28's νηφαλίους are two spellings of one word. In v. 8 the Byzantine word order περὶ ἡμῶν λέγειν is reversed in NA28, and in v. 10 Byzantine πίστιν πᾶσαν is reversed and the article before διδασκαλίαν differs. None affects the sense.

Nothing in this chapter's apparatus touches vv. 11–14 except the article in v. 11, and that article does not change who is being trained or the purpose of the training.
