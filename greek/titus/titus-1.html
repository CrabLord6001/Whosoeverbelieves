Introduction to Titus 1

Titus 1 does two things. Paul first grounds his own commission in the faith of God's chosen and the hope of eternal life (vv. 1–4), then turns to the work he left Titus on Crete to do, which is appointing qualified elders city by city (vv. 5–9) and answering the teachers who were overturning households (vv. 10–16). The chapter's weight for a Free Grace reader falls in two places. In v. 2 the ἐλπίς ζωῆς αἰωνίου is a hope, which is to say something certain but still future, even though the believer possesses eternal life already. And in v. 16 the false teachers deny God by their works, which is a statement about their conduct rather than a test by which a reader may measure whether anyone is regenerate. The Greek text follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with NA28 divergences noted where they occur.

Paul stacks two nouns in apposition before the first verb arrives. He is δοῦλος θεοῦ, a slave belonging to God, and then ἀπόστολος, one sent with authority. The postpositive δέ is mildly contrastive and sets the second title against the first, so that the exalted office stands beside the servant's status rather than replacing it.

The two κατά phrases are the interpretive question of the verse. With the accusative κατά most often marks a standard, which would make Paul's apostleship conform to the faith of God's chosen. Wilkin instead takes it as expressing what the apostleship was designed to produce, so that Paul's commission aims at producing faith and at producing knowledge of the truth. Either reading leaves ἐκλεκτῶν θεοῦ to be defined. Wilkin notes that the phrase appears in Paul only here and at Romans 8:33 and Colossians 3:12, and he reads it against the Old Testament description of Israel as a chosen people rather than as a pretemporal selection of individuals to eternal life. ἐπίγνωσις is the compounded form of γνῶσις and carries the sense of knowledge that is full rather than partial.

ἐπ’ ἐλπίδι uses ἐπί with the dative to name the basis on which the preceding ministry rests. The noun ἐλπίς in the New Testament does not carry the English sense of wishing for an uncertain outcome. It names something certain that has not yet arrived.

That distinction governs the verse. Wilkin points out that the believer holds eternal life now as a present possession, citing John 6:47, while what is in view here is the full experience of that life in a glorified body in the kingdom, which awaits Christ's return. The hope therefore ties the godliness of v. 1 to the second coming. ἀψευδής occurs only here in the New Testament and is formed by alpha privative on ψεύδος, so God is the one in whom falsehood has no place. The aorist middle ἐπηγγείλατο places the promise at a point before χρόνων αἰωνίων, times long past, which is the same adjective used of the life promised.

The aorist ἐφανέρωσεν answers the promise of v. 2. What was pledged before long ages was made visible at a point in time, and καιροῖς ἰδίοις is a dative of time naming the seasons that belong to God rather than to the preacher.

The striking form is ἐπιστεύθην, an aorist passive of πιστεύω. The verb ordinarily means to believe; in the passive with a personal subject it means to be entrusted with something, so Paul says that the proclamation was committed to him. The emphatic ἐγώ follows, unnecessary for the grammar and therefore pointed. Wilkin takes the λόγος to be the good news about Christ, covering both justification by faith alone and the sanctification that follows it. ἐπιταγή is a command rather than a suggestion, and the one who issues it is here called σωτῆρος ἡμῶν θεοῦ, a title Paul applies in this letter both to the Father and, in 2:13, to Christ.

γνησίῳ τέκνῳ is a dative of address. γνήσιος describes a legitimate child rather than an affectionate one, so the phrase asserts a real relationship, not a fond name. κοινήν πίστιν is the ground they share. Wilkin draws the obvious conclusion, which is that Paul's faith was not of a higher order than Titus's or than any other believer's.

The greeting itself carries two textual questions. The Byzantine text reads the three-part χάρις, ἔλεος, εἰρήνη where NA28 reads χάρις καὶ εἰρήνη without ἔλεος, and the Byzantine word order κυρίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ stands against NA28's Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ. Wilkin, writing from the Majority Text, observes that mercy appears in all three Pastoral greetings, which is the internal argument for the longer reading here.

Τούτου χάριν is an improper preposition governing a preceding genitive, and it points forward to the two purpose clauses rather than back to the greeting. The Byzantine κατέλιπόν and NA28's ἀπέλιπον differ only in the prefixed preposition and carry the same sense of leaving someone behind.

Two aorist subjunctives follow ἵνα, and they divide Titus's assignment in two. ἐπιδιορθώσῃ is built on ᾍρθός, straight, and Wilkin notes that the word describes putting right what is out of place, as a physician sets a broken bone. The object is τὰ λείποντα, a neuter plural participle used substantivally for the things still lacking. The second task is καταστήσῃς κατὰ πόλιν πρεσβυτέρους, where κατά with the accusative is distributive, meaning city by city rather than over the cities. Wilkin reads the stay as short-term work, pointing to 3:12.

The verse is a first-class condition, εἰ with the indicative ἐστιν, which assumes the case for the sake of the argument rather than doubting it. ἀνέγκλητος is a legal word, alpha privative on a root meaning to call in, and it describes a man against whom no charge can be brought and made to stick. The subject is the indefinite τίς, and Wilkin observes that the Greek says only "anyone" where some English versions supply "a man", though he takes the masculine rendering to be justified by what follows.

μιᾶς γυναικὸς ἀνήρ is a genitive of relationship with the numeral in first position for emphasis, literally a one-woman man. Wilkin reads it of a man married once. τέκνα ἔχων πιστά turns on πιστός, which can mean either faithful or believing; Wilkin allows both and prefers believing children. The negative μή then governs the participial construction rather than an indicative, and ἀνυπότακτα is neuter plural in agreement with τέκνα, not with the father.

The word a Free Grace reader should slow down on is ἀπίστοις. It is standardly rendered unbelieving, but Wilkin argues for unfaithful here, noting that its positive counterpart πιστός appears three times in this short letter at 1:6, 1:9 and 3:8, each time of faithfulness rather than of initial belief. On his reading the verse characterizes these men's unfaithfulness and does not settle whether they are regenerate. The perfect forms press the same point about condition rather than category. μεμιασμένοις is a perfect passive participle and μεμίανται a perfect passive indicative, so both describe a standing state produced by past defilement. The correlative καὶ … καί then names two faculties together, mind and conscience. Wilkin takes the first to mean they do not think biblically and the second to mean sinful action no longer troubles them.

Θεὸν stands first in the sentence, ahead of its verb, which throws the weight onto the object of the profession. ὁμολογοῦσιν εἰδέναι pairs a present indicative with a perfect infinitive of οἶδα, a verb whose perfect carries present force, so the claim is to a settled knowledge of God. Against it stands τοῖς ἔργοις, a dative of means, with the deponent ἀρνοῦνται.

This is the verse in the chapter most often turned into a test of whether a professing person is saved, and Wilkin declines that use of it. He takes the profession to concern knowing God in experience, so that what the works disprove is experiential knowledge rather than regeneration. The closing word carries the argument. ἀδόκιμοι stands last in the Greek sentence for emphasis, and Wilkin points out that the word and its antonym δόκιμος are used across the New Testament of believers being disapproved or approved, naming among others 1 Corinthians 9:27, 2 Corinthians 13:5–7 and James 1:12. Since disobedient believers are disapproved and unbelievers are as well, the term by itself settles nothing about whether these particular men were regenerate. Wilkin's conclusion is deliberately limited: they were repugnant, and they were to be opposed. βδελυκτοί occurs only here in the New Testament, and ἀπειθεῖς denies the obedience that πείθω would supply.

Overall Exegesis: Titus 1

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The chapter divides cleanly in two. Verses 1–4 are a single long salutation in which the main clause never arrives; Paul piles nominatives and prepositional phrases on one another until the greeting of v. 4 closes it. Verses 5–16 are the commission, and they are held together by a chain of grounding conjunctions. Verse 7 opens with γάρ to ground the qualifications of v. 6, and v. 10 opens with γάρ again to ground the whole standard in the situation on Crete. Paul's logic runs backward from the problem: there are men overturning households, therefore elders must hold fast the faithful word, therefore here is the kind of man Titus should appoint.

The qualification list itself is arranged as a negative series and a positive one. Five μή clauses in v. 7 are answered by six positive accusatives in v. 8 after a single ἀλλά, and the pivot is the whole force of that conjunction.

II. Key Theological Themes

Two words govern the chapter for a Free Grace reader. The first is ἐλπίς in v. 2. Eternal life is a present possession, yet it is here the content of a hope, which means something certain and still future. The resolution is the distinction between possessing the life now and experiencing it fully at Christ's return, and 3:7 makes the same move with the same vocabulary.

The second is ἀδόκιμος in v. 16, standing last in its clause for emphasis. Verse 16 is frequently read as a test by which a professing person's regeneration may be assessed, and the Greek does not support that weight. The profession in view concerns knowing God in experience, and ἀδόκιμος is used elsewhere in the New Testament of believers who fail a test, most plainly at 1 Corinthians 9:27. What the verse establishes is that these men's works contradict their claim; it does not establish what they are. The same restraint applies to ἀπίστοις in v. 15, which may be read as unfaithful rather than unbelieving, and the letter's three uses of πιστός at 1:6, 1:9 and 3:8 all concern faithfulness.

A third theme is medical. ὑγιαίνω and its participle appear in vv. 9 and 13 for teaching and for the believers who receive it, and ἐπιδιορθόω in v. 5 describes setting what is out of joint. Paul is not describing a purge but a course of treatment, which bears directly on who is being rebuked in v. 13.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

Crete had no apostolic founder named in the letter or in Acts, and Paul does not claim to have planted the churches there. Titus is left as a short-term representative rather than a resident pastor, which is why the assignment is to appoint elders rather than to shepherd directly. The quotation in v. 12 from a Cretan poet is one of three places where Paul cites a pagan author, and he endorses the line as applied to the false teachers without extending it to every Cretan believer.

The circumcision party of v. 10 invites comparison with Galatians, and the comparison should be made carefully. Titus raises no dispute about how a person receives eternal life. Its false teaching is characterized as myths, human commandments and material greed, which is a different problem from the one Paul answers in Galatians, however similar the parties may look.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 4 — ἔλεος. The Byzantine text reads the three-part χάρις, ἔλεος, εἰρήνη; NA28 reads χάρις καὶ εἰρήνη without mercy. The internal argument for the longer reading is that mercy appears in the greeting of all three Pastoral Epistles. The same verse has a word-order divergence, Byzantine κυρίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ against NA28's Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ, which carries no difference in sense.

v. 5 — κατέλιπόν. NA28 reads ἀπέλιπον. The two compounds differ only in prefix and both mean to leave behind. No exegetical consequence.

v. 10 — καί and the article. The Byzantine text reads πολλοὶ καὶ ἀνυπότακτοι where NA28 brackets the καί as doubtful, and reads anarthrous ἐκ περιτομῆς where NA28 has τῆς περιτομῆς. Neither changes the sense.

v. 15 — μέν and μεμιασμένοις. NA28 omits the μέν, leaving the δέ to carry the contrast alone; the Byzantine reading makes the balance explicit. NA28 also spells the participle μεμιαμμένοις, which is orthographic.

No divergence in this chapter touches the argument. The one worth a reader's attention is ἔλεος in v. 4, and even there the question is whether Paul wished Titus mercy, not what he taught.
