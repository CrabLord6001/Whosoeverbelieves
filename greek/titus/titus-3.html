Introduction to Titus 3

Titus 3 moves outward. Chapter 2 ordered the household; this chapter turns to how believers behave toward outsiders (vv. 1–2), and then grounds that behavior in what they themselves once were and what God did about it (vv. 3–7). Verses 4–7 are a single Greek sentence and the doctrinal center of the letter. They are also the paragraph a Free Grace reader will want to read most slowly, since ἔσωσεν in v. 5 and δικαιωθέντες in v. 7 are not synonyms, and λουτροῦ παλιγγενεσίας in v. 5 is the phrase on which discussions of baptismal regeneration turn. The chapter closes with the practical instructions and greetings (vv. 8–15). The Greek text follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with NA28 divergences noted where they occur.

᾽Υπομίμνῃσκε is a present imperative of a compound meaning to put in mind again, so Titus is to keep reminding rather than to inform. What follows is what believers already know.

Three infinitives carry the content. ὑποτάσσεσθαι is middle, as at 2:9, and governs the datives ἀρχαῖς καὶ ἐξουσίαις, which name offices rather than individuals. πειθαρχεῖν compounds obedience with rule. The Byzantine text reads the καί joining the two datives where NA28 omits it, which only affects whether rulers and authorities are read as two groups or one pairing. The third infinitive is the one Paul returns to, πρὸς πᾶν ἔργον ἀγαθὸν ἑτοίμους εῖναι, and Wilkin notes that good works here probably reach as far as the good that governments themselves do.

The infinitives continue. μηδένα βλασφημεῖν uses the verb of 2:5, and here the object is a person rather than the word of God. Wilkin is careful with this one. He observes that the Lord and his apostles said sharp things about people, so the prohibition is against slander rather than against plain speech.

ἀμάχους is alpha privative on the word for battle, so uncontentious rather than merely peaceable, and ἐπιεικεῖς names the quality of not insisting on the letter of one's rights. The participle ἐνδεικνυμένους repeats the verb of 2:10, so the showing is again outward and visible. The Byzantine πρᾷοτητα and NA28's πραΰτητα are two spellings of one word. The scope at the end is deliberately wide, πρὸς πάντας ἀνθρώπους, which Wilkin underlines as reaching past fellow believers.

Ἑμεν γάρ grounds the gentleness of v. 2 in memory. The imperfect places the whole description in a settled past condition, and the emphatic καὶ ἡμεῖς includes Paul himself, which is the force of the verse. Wilkin's point is that the recollection is what makes a believer understanding toward people still in that state, and that a person who has forgotten it loses sympathy.

Seven descriptions follow, moving from inward to outward. ἀνόητοι and ἀπειθεῖς are adjectives; the latter is the same word applied to the false teachers at 1:16, which quietly places the writer and his readers in their former company. Then four participles. πλανώμενοι is passive, being led astray rather than wandering off. δουλεύοντες takes the datives ἐπιθυμίαις and ἡδοναῖς, so the desires were the masters. διάγοντες is to spend one's life, and μισοῦντες ἀλλήλους closes the list with the reciprocal pronoun, hating one another.

The ὅτε δέ opens the sentence that runs to the end of v. 7, and the delay is rhetorical. The main verb does not arrive until ἔσωσεν in v. 5.

ἐπεφάνη is the same second aorist passive as 2:11, which ties the two appearings together: grace appeared there, kindness and love for mankind appear here, and both describe the one coming of Christ. χρηστότης is kindness in action. φιλανθρωπία is a compound of love and humanity, used only here and at Acts 28:2, and it is the Greek word behind English philanthropy. The singular verb with two feminine subjects treats the pair as one thing. τοῦ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν θεοῦ is held to the very end of the clause, far from the nouns it governs.

The negative comes first and is emphatic. οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων τῶν ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ places the source of salvation outside anything done, and the added ὃν ἐποιήσαμεν ἡμεῖς with its unnecessary pronoun shuts the door on the obvious objection. Wilkin is careful not to overread it. Paul is not denying that unbelievers perform righteous acts, only that such acts are the means of this salvation. The Byzantine ῗν is a genitive by attraction to ἔργων, where NA28's Ἄ leaves the relative in its own accusative case as object of the verb; the syntax differs, the sense does not. NA28 also reads the neuter τὸ αὐτοῦ ἔλεος for the Byzantine masculine τὸν αὐτοῦ ἔλεον, which is a difference of declension only.

Then the phrase that carries the argument. διὰ λουτροῦ παλιγγενεσίας uses λουτρόν, a washing or the vessel in which one washes, with παλιγγενεσία, a compound of again and birth that appears elsewhere in the New Testament only at Matthew 19:28. The genitive is most naturally read as appositional or descriptive, so the washing is the regeneration rather than a rite accompanying it, and nothing in the clause names water or baptism. Wilkin takes a further step worth stating plainly as his, since it is not the common reading. He distinguishes the salvation of this verse from regeneration itself, arguing that the cleansing that happened at regeneration produced this saving, and that what Paul means by ἔσωσεν here is deliverance from bondage to sin. On that reading the verse fits the letter's concern with godliness in experience, which is what Titus was sent to produce. Whether or not one follows him that far, the clause grounds the whole thing in τὸν αὐτοῦ ἔλεον, his mercy, and not in the works the verse has just excluded.

The relative οὗ is genitive, and its case is the exegetical question of the verse. Its antecedent is πνεύματος ἁγίου at the end of v. 5, and the genitive is best explained as attraction to that antecedent, since the verb ἐξέχεεν would otherwise take an accusative. The Spirit, not the washing, is what was poured out.

ἐκχέω is the verb used at Pentecost in Acts 2, which sets this outpouring in that line. πλουσίως is richly or abundantly, and the measure matters for v. 7, since what the Spirit is poured out for is heirship. διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ names the channel, and τοῦ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν is applied to Christ here where the same title was applied to the Father in v. 4, which is the letter's habit.

The purpose clause closes the long sentence. δικαιωθέντες is an aorist passive participle and therefore antecedent to the main verb, so justification precedes what follows rather than resulting from it. τῇ ἐκείνου χάριτι is a dative of means with the demonstrative in attributive position, that one's grace.

Wilkin presses a distinction here that organizes the whole paragraph. Justification is not the same as the salvation of v. 5, nor the washing, nor the renewing. It is the act by which God declares the believer righteous in his position, and it is the ground on which the other ministries stand. What the purpose clause then names is not justification but κληρονόμοι, heirs. The Byzantine γενώμεθα is a second aorist middle subjunctive where NA28 reads the passive γενηθῶμεν, a difference of voice that leaves the sense intact. The phrase κατ᾽ ἐλπίδα ζωῆς αἰωνίου repeats 1:2 word for word and closes the letter's frame. Eternal life is possessed now and hoped for still, and Wilkin takes the aim of the Spirit's renewing to be exactly this heirship, which is to say reigning with Christ in the age to come.

Overall Exegesis: Titus 3

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The chapter moves in four steps. Verses 1–2 instruct believers toward outsiders. Verse 3 recalls what they themselves were, introduced by γάρ so that the memory is the reason for the gentleness. Verses 4–7 are one Greek sentence, and its main verb ἔσωσεν is held back until v. 5, so that everything in v. 4 is suspended until God acts. Verses 8–11 give the practical charge and its negative counterpart, and vv. 12–15 close with travel plans and greetings.

The sentence in vv. 4–7 is the most carefully built in the letter. The negative comes first (οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων), the positive second (κατὰ τὸν αὐτοῦ ἔλεον), the means third (διὰ λουτροῦ), and the purpose last. Verse 8 then calls that whole sentence ὁ λόγος, the word that is faithful.

II. Key Theological Themes

Three terms in vv. 5–7 must be kept apart. ἔσωσεν is the main verb, λουτροῦ παλιγγενεσίας is its means, and δικαιωθέντες in v. 7 is an aorist passive participle antecedent to the subjunctive it serves. Wilkin's reading, which deserves naming as his, distinguishes them sharply. Justification is the positional declaration and the ground of the rest; the washing is the cleansing that occurred at regeneration; and the saving of v. 5 is deliverance from bondage to sin, which is what the letter's concern with godliness requires. On any reading the sentence makes salvation flow from mercy and not from works, and the aim it finally names is heirship rather than justification.

The phrase λουτροῦ παλιγγενεσίας is the usual starting point for arguments that baptism effects regeneration. Three things in the Greek weigh against that use of it. No word for water or baptism appears in the clause. The genitive is most naturally appositional, which makes the washing the regeneration rather than a rite attending it. And the verse has just excluded works done in righteousness as the ground of the saving, which would be a strange thing to say immediately before making a performed rite the instrument of it.

Verse 8 then places good works where the letter has placed them throughout. Their subject is οἱ πεπιστευκότες θεῷ, a perfect participle describing people who have already believed. Good works are urged on believers, not required of those who would become them.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

Verse 12 carries the letter's clearest historical marker. Paul is choosing where to spend the winter, which is not the situation of a man in custody, so Titus belongs after the release from the first Roman imprisonment rather than alongside 2 Timothy. Zenas and Apollos in v. 13 were most likely the carriers of the letter, and πρόπεμψον is the technical term for provisioning travelers rather than merely bidding them farewell, which is why v. 14 can treat helping them as the concrete case of maintaining good works.

The πιστὸς ὁ λόγος formula of v. 8 appears five times in the Pastoral Epistles and nowhere else in the New Testament, which is one of the features that binds the three letters together.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 5 — ῗν and ἔλεον. The Byzantine ῗν is a relative attracted into the genitive of its antecedent ἔργων; NA28's Ἄ leaves it in the accusative as the object of ἐποιήσαμεν. Attraction is ordinary Greek and the meaning is identical. The Byzantine τὸν αὐτοῦ ἔλεον treats the noun as a masculine of the second declension where NA28's τὸ αὐτοῦ ἔλεος treats it as a neuter of the third. Both were current, and the word is the same.

v. 7 — γενώμεθα. NA28 reads the aorist passive γενηθῶμεν where the Byzantine text has the second aorist middle. With a verb of becoming the two voices overlap, so nothing turns on it.

vv. 1, 2, 8, 13, 15 — minor variations. In v. 1 NA28 omits the καί joining rulers and authorities. In v. 2 πρᾷοτητα and πραΰτητα are two spellings of one word. In v. 8 NA28 omits the article τά. In v. 13 the Byzantine contracted Ἀπολλὼ stands for NA28's Ἀπολλῶν, the same name. In v. 15 the Byzantine text closes with Ἀμήν, which NA28 omits.

No divergence in this chapter bears on the doctrine of vv. 4–7. The two readings in v. 5 are a matter of syntax and declension, and neither touches what the verse excludes or what it affirms.
