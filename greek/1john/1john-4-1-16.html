Introduction to 1 John 4:1–16

The page has two halves and they are not obviously about the same thing. Verses 1–6 are a test applied to teachers, picking up the men who went out in 2:19 and giving the readers a criterion: what a spirit confesses about Jesus Christ come in the flesh. Verses 7–16 are about love, and they contain the letter's two great statements that God is love together with its fullest account of what that love did. The join is at v. 6, where the two spirits are named, and at v. 13, where the Spirit is given as the ground of assurance. For a Free Grace reader the verses to watch are 4:8, which says that the one not loving did not come to know God, and 4:15, which attaches God's abiding to a confession. Both are read here as the letter has read such statements since 2:3, as concerning an experienced relation rather than the possession of eternal life. The page also carries the second of three places where the recording and the printed text differ in length, at 4:3, and here the difference runs the other way from 2:23: the Byzantine text is the longer.

ἐν τούτῳ points forward. The Byzantine text reads the passive γινώσκεται, the Spirit of God is known by this, where NA28 reads the active γινώσκετε, you know by this. The forms differ by one letter and the sense is close either way.

The test itself is a confession with a precise object. ὁμολογεῖ takes Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν as its object and ἐν σαρκὶ ἐληλυθότα as a predicate participle agreeing with it. The participle is perfect, so what is confessed is not merely that he came but that he came and the coming stands: the incarnation is a permanent condition rather than a past episode. That is the whole weight of the test, and it is aimed at teaching that would allow the Christ to have visited flesh without permanently taking it.

John leaves both unnamed, which keeps the contrast on the plane of power rather than of identity.

The criterion is apostolic doctrine, which is what the whole test of vv. 1–6 has been about. ἐκ τούτου at the end is unusual, a masculine or neuter genitive where the letter elsewhere writes ἐν τούτῳ, and it points back rather than forward. The two spirits are then named: τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς ἀληθείας and τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς πλάνης, the second from the verb used at 1:8, 2:26 and 3:7 of being led astray.

ἐν τούτῳ points forward to the ὅτι clause, and ἐν ἡμῖν is most naturally among us rather than in us: the manifestation was public.

ἐφανερώθη repays a note of its own, because the letter uses this verb nine times and always in the aorist passive, yet not always of the same event. Three senses run through it. It is used of the first coming: the life was manifested at 1:2, Christ was manifested to take away sins at 3:5 and to undo the devil's works at 3:8, and here God's love is manifested among us. It is used of the second: ὅταν φανερωθῇ at 2:28 and ἐὰν φανερωθῇ at 3:2 are the appearing still to come, and 3:2 sets the two side by side, since what we shall be has not yet been manifested. And it is used of people being shown for what they are, at 2:19 of the men who went out and at 3:10 in its adjectival form, where the two sets of children are φανερά.

The third sense is the one this chapter completes. John does not use the verb at v. 12, but the thought is there and the opening clause of that verse is what calls for it: no one has ever beheld God, and yet if we love one another he abides in us and his love is brought to its end in us. What was manifested once in the sending of the Son is manifested now in the love of his people, so the same God who cannot be seen is made visible twice over. Reading v. 9 and v. 12 together is what keeps either from being taken as a condition of life rather than a disclosure of love.

τὸν μονογενῆ is the word of John 1:14 and 3:16, one of a kind rather than merely first-born, and ἀπέσταλκεν is a perfect: the sending stands. The purpose clause is the verse's point for a Free Grace reader. ἵνα ζήσωμεν δι᾽ αὐτοῦ uses an aorist subjunctive of ζάω: that we might live, through him. Life is the stated purpose of the mission and it comes through a person, which is the same thought as 5:11–12.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 4:1–16

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

Two movements, joined by the Spirit. Verses 1–6 are a test applied to teachers and close by naming the two spirits; vv. 7–16 are about love and close with the two statements that God is love. Verse 13 is the pivot between them, returning to the Spirit as the ground of assurance exactly as 3:24 did.

The first half is held together by one preposition. ἐκ occurs nine times in six verses — of the spirits to be tested, of the false prophets, of the readers, of the teachers, of their speech, and finally of the two spirits recognized. Chapter 3 established the usage and chapter 4 applies it to a practical question: what a man says and who listens to him both disclose his source.

The second half is built on repetition rather than argument. ἀγάπη and its cognates appear fifteen times in vv. 7–16, and the same statement is made twice, at v. 8 and v. 16. Between them vv. 9–10 supply the content: love is defined by what God did, not by what the readers feel, and the two verses are the fullest account of the mission in the letter.

II. Key Theological Themes

A test with a precise object. What vv. 2–3 require is not a general confession of Christ but the confession of Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν ἐν σαρκὶ ἐληλυθότα, with the participle in the perfect: he came in flesh and the coming stands. The test is aimed at teaching that would let the Christ visit flesh without permanently taking it.

Love defined by an act, and its origin named. Verse 10 denies that the love in question began with us and then names two aorists of God, loving and sending, with ἱλασμόν in apposition. That word has occurred once before, at 2:2, where its scope was stated to be unlimited. The two verses belong together: 2:2 gives the extent of the propitiation and 4:10 gives its origin.

Life as the purpose of the mission. Verse 9 closes with ἵνα ζήσωμεν δι᾽ αὐτοῦ, that we might live through him. The purpose is stated as life and the means as a person, which is the same construction the letter will use at 5:11–12.

What manifestation means, in three senses. φανερόω occurs nine times in the letter and always in the aorist passive, but not always of the same event. It is used of the first coming (1:2, 3:5, 3:8 and 4:9), of the appearing still to come (2:28 and 3:2, which sets the two side by side), and of people being shown for what they are (2:19, and the adjective at 3:10). The third sense is completed in this chapter without the verb: v. 12 denies that anyone has ever beheld God and then says that if we love one another he abides in us and his love is brought to its end in us. What was manifested once in the sending of the Son is manifested now in the love of his people.

The unseen God made visible. Verse 12 opens by denying that anyone has ever beheld God and then supplies the resolution: if we love one another, he abides in us and his love is brought to its intended end in us. The periphrastic perfect is the one from 2:5. What is promised is not the giving of life but the maturing of a love already given.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

Verse 8 is settled by the letter's usage, not by its tense. γινώσκω has meant acquaintance since 2:3, where knowing God is tested by present obedience, and at 2:4 the man who claims it while not keeping the commandments is called a liar with no verdict passed on his standing. Verse 7 has just used the present γινώσκει of the one who loves. In that company οὐκ ἔγνω concerns the acquaintance rather than the new birth, and the verse says of one man what 2:4 said of the other. The aorist suits that reading and is worth noting, but an aorist can be constative or gnomic as readily as punctiliar, so it supports the reading rather than establishing it.

Verse 15 attaches abiding to confession, not life to confession. What follows ὃς ἄν ὁμολογήσῃ is ὁ θεὸς ἐν αὐτῷ μένει, the mutual abiding of v. 13. The letter makes believing the condition of eternal life at 5:13 and confessing the condition of this abiding, and μένω has described continuance in a relation rather than entry into one since 2:6.

Verse 7 runs from the practice to the birth, as 2:29 did. πᾶς ὁ ἀγαπῶν ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ γεγέννηται states where loving comes from. It does not supply a test by which a man settles whether he has been born of God, and reading it that way sets it against 5:13 in the same way 2:29 would be.

Verse 13 grounds the assurance in a gift that stands. δέδωκεν is a perfect where 3:24 had the aorist, and ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος is partitive, a share given. What the readers are told to rest on is something God has done and continues to hold, not their performance of the love just commanded.

Verse 14 keeps the scope unrestricted. σωτῆρα τοῦ κόσμου is a predicate accusative without the article, and κόσμος is no more restricted here than it was at 2:2.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Six divergences fall in these sixteen verses, and one of them is substantial.

4:3, the second of three length divergences in the letter. The Byzantine text reads ὃ μὴ ὁμολογεῖ Ἰησοῦν Χριστὸν ἐν σαρκὶ ἐληλυθότα, keeping v. 3 symmetrical with v. 2. NA28 reads only ὁ μὴ ὁμολογεῖ τὸν Ἰησοῦν. The critical reading is the harder one and can be explained as original on that ground, with the longer form arising by assimilation to v. 2; the Byzantine reading has symmetry in its favor. Note that the difference here runs opposite to 2:23: there the recording was the longer, here the printed page is, so the word highlighting drifts the other way through this verse.

4:2. The Byzantine text reads the passive γινώσκεται, the Spirit of God is known by this; NA28 reads the active γινώσκετε, you know by this. One letter divides them.

The remaining four are minor. NA28 reads the perfect ἠγαπήκαμεν for the aorist in v. 10; it orders the final clause of v. 12 differently; it reads ἐάν for ἄν in v. 15; and in v. 16 part of the tradition leaves the final μένει to be understood where the Byzantine text supplies it.
