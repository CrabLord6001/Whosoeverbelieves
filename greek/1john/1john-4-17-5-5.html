Introduction to 1 John 4:17–5:5

The page carries the letter's two strongest statements for a Free Grace reader, and they sit at opposite ends of it. Verses 17–18 take up the confidence of 2:28 and name the occasion: ἡ ἡμέρα τῆς κρίσεως, the day of judgment, where what is at stake is a believer's freedom of speech rather than his presence there. Then 5:1 states the condition of the new birth in one line, with an articular present participle of believing and a perfect indicative of being born. Between them vv. 19–21 and 5:2–3 close out the argument about loving the brother that has run since 3:11, and 5:4–5 answer it with the victory and name what wins it. The page also crosses a chapter, so the audio bar carries both chapter buttons and the passage plays straight through.

ἐν τούτῳ most naturally points back to the abiding of v. 16, and τετελείωται is the perfect of 2:5 and 4:12, brought to the end intended and standing there. The construction μεθ᾽ ἡμῶν is unusual for this verb, love perfected with us rather than in us, which keeps it a shared thing rather than a private attainment.

The ἵνα clause gives the purpose and the result together. παρρησίαν ἔχωμεν is the noun and verb of 2:28 and 3:21, outspokenness, and the occasion is ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῆς κρίσεως. Hodges takes this as the believer's appearing to have his life assessed rather than a judgment that determines destiny, and the grammar supports him twice over: John writes ἔχωμεν in the first person plural, including himself, and the ground clause assumes the readers are already in the relevant position — καθὼς ἐκεῖνός ἐστιν, καὶ ἡμεῖς ἐσμὲν ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ τούτῳ, as he is, so are we. The correspondence is stated as a present fact.

The verse explains what confidence excludes. Φόβος is anarthrous and fronted, so the statement is qualitative: fear as such has no place in love. ἡ τελεία ἀγάπη is the adjective cognate with the verb of v. 17, mature or complete love rather than flawless love, and ἔξω βάλλει is a vivid present: throws fear outside.

The reason is ὅτι ὁ φόβος κόλασιν ἔχει. κόλασις occurs in the New Testament only here and at Matthew 25:46, and it means punishment, so what fear carries with it is the anticipation of being punished. The closing clause is the one that is sometimes read as a test: ὁ δὲ φοβούμενος οὐ τετελείωται ἐν τῇ ἀγάπῃ. The verb is the perfect of v. 17 again, and what it denies is completion, not possession. A believer who is afraid is one in whom love has not yet been brought to its end, which is exactly what the letter has been working toward since 2:5. Nothing here says such a man is unregenerate.

The Byzantine text reads ἡμεῖς ἀγαπῶμεν αὐτόν, we love him, where NA28 omits the object and leaves the verb absolute, we love. The difference is small but not nothing: the Byzantine reading makes the verse about love returned to God, the critical reading about love as such, which suits the move to loving the brother in v. 20.

The causal clause carries the theology. αὐτὸς πρῶτος ἠγάπησεν ἡμᾶς has an emphatic subject, an adverbial πρῶτος and an aorist. The priority is God's and it is stated as a completed act. The verse is the letter's shortest statement of the order that has governed it since 4:10: not that we loved, but that he loved, and our loving is the consequence rather than the cause.

The construction is the one used of claims throughout the letter, now as a third-class condition: ἐάν τις εἴπῃ with the quoted claim Ἀγαπῶ τὸν θεόν, and the conduct that contradicts it in a second subjunctive, μισῇ. The verdict is the familiar ψεύστης ἐστίν, as at 1:10, 2:4 and 2:22.

The argument that follows turns on what is seen. ὃν ἑώρακεν is a perfect of the brother and ὃν οὐχ ἑώρακεν a perfect of God, which takes up 4:12 directly: God has not been beheld, so the brother who has been is the available object. πῶς δύναται ἀγαπᾶν makes it a rhetorical question in the Byzantine text; NA28 reads οὐ, turning it into a flat denial. The question is the stronger rhetoric and the denial the plainer statement, and the sense is the same.

This is the verse a Free Grace reader will turn to first, and it should be read slowly. Πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων is an articular present participle, everyone believing, and the content believed is stated exactly: ὅτι Ἰησοῦς ἐστὶν ὁ Χριστός. Of such a person John says ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ γεγέννηται, a perfect passive: he has been born of God and stands born.

The construction is the same as 2:29 and 4:7, where the activity was practicing righteousness and loving, and it is worth being exact about what it does and does not settle. A long-standing reading takes the perfect as logically prior: because the birth stands in the perfect and the believing in the present, regeneration is held to precede and produce the faith. The right answer to that is not that the grammar tells against it but that the grammar is indeterminate here, and tells neither way.

A perfect alongside a present does not order two events. The perfect names a state now obtaining as the result of a completed action; it does not locate that action in a sequence relative to anything else in the sentence. The articular present participle, for its part, is generic: πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων identifies a class by its characteristic activity and does not time an occasion. Put together they assert that both are true of the same person, and they are perfectly capable of describing two things that obtain together. So the sentence will not yield a causal chain to anyone, and a Free Grace reader should no more claim the reverse order from this construction than a Reformed reader should claim his.

What settles it is the rest of John. The Fourth Gospel states its purpose at John 20:31 in these same terms, that believing may issue in life; John 1:12 gives the right to become children of God to those who received him, to those believing on his name, and only then speaks of being born of God; and this letter will say at 5:13 that the knowledge of having eternal life rests on believing. Read with those, 5:1 identifies the believer as one already born rather than making the birth the cause of his believing. The second half then repeats the point in the family's own terms: πᾶς ὁ ἀγαπῶν τὸν γεννήσαντα, the one who begot, loves also τὸν γεγεννημένον ἐξ αὐτοῦ, the one begotten of him, which is the argument of 4:20–21 stated as a family resemblance.

John's denial is not that the commandments are easy but that they weigh on a believer as a burden does, and v. 4 supplies the reason.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 4:17–5:5

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The page closes two arguments at once. Verses 17–18 finish the account of love begun at 4:7 by naming what mature love produces, confidence at the day of judgment; vv. 19–21 finish the argument about loving the brother that has run since 3:11, stating the commandment for the third time after 2:7–8 and 3:23. Then 5:1–5 opens the letter's closing movement by naming the condition of the new birth and the thing that overcomes the world.

The chapter division falls inside the argument rather than between arguments, which is why this page carries verses from both. Verse 4:21 binds love of God to love of the brother; 5:1 then grounds that binding in the family: the one who loves the father loves the child begotten of him. The two verses are a single thought and the chapter break obscures it.

Verses 1, 4 and 5 form a tight ring. The one believing has been born of God; what is born of God overcomes the world; the overcomer is the one believing. Each step is stated with an articular participle and the ring closes on the word it opened with.

II. Key Theological Themes

Confidence at the judgment, and what fear means. Verse 17 names the occasion as ἡ ἡμέρα τῆς κρίσεως and the thing sought as παρρησία, the noun of 2:28 and 3:21. Verse 18 then explains what confidence excludes: fear carries κόλασις, a word the New Testament uses only here and at Matthew 25:46, and mature love throws it outside.

Perfected love as maturity, not flawlessness. τελειόω and its adjective τέλειος appear three times on this page, and the family of words means brought to its intended end. The letter has used the verb at 2:5 and 4:12 of love reaching its goal in a believer, never of a believer reaching sinlessness.

The order of love. Verse 19 states it in the shortest form the letter manages: αὐτὸς πρῶτος ἠγάπησεν ἡμᾶς, with an emphatic subject and an aorist. Our loving is the consequence, never the cause, which is the argument of 4:10 compressed into a clause.

Commandments that do not weigh. Verse 3 denies that God's commandments are βαρεῖαι, the adjective used of the loads bound on men's shoulders at Matthew 23:4. The denial is not that obedience is easy but that it presses on a believer as a burden does, and v. 4 supplies the reason in the victory already won.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

5:1 states the condition of the new birth, and the grammar needs handling honestly. Πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων is an articular present participle and γεγέννηται a perfect passive, and the same construction appears at 2:29 and 4:7. It asserts that both are true of the same person; it does not order them. A long-standing reading takes the perfect as logically prior, so that regeneration precedes and produces the faith, and the point to make against it is not that the grammar tells the other way but that the grammar is indeterminate and tells neither. A perfect names a state now obtaining as the result of a completed action without locating that action in a sequence, and an articular present participle identifies a class by its characteristic activity without timing an occasion; the two together are as capable of describing contemporaneous states as successive ones. Neither side can derive its order from the construction. What settles the order is the rest of John: the Fourth Gospel states its purpose at John 20:31 in terms of believing issuing in life, John 1:12 gives the right to become children of God to those who received him and believed on his name, and this letter says at 5:13 that the knowledge of having eternal life rests on believing. Read with those, 5:1 identifies the believer as one already born rather than deriving his faith from the birth.

5:4 names faith as the victory. The subject is neuter, πᾶν τὸ γεγεννημένον, everything born of God rather than everyone, which keeps the statement about a kind of thing as 3:9 did with the seed. Then the present νικᾷ gives way to the aorist νικήσασα for the decisive act, and the victory is named: ἡ πίστις ἡμῶν.

4:18 denies completion, not possession. ὁ δὲ φοβούμενος οὐ τετελείωται uses the perfect of v. 17. A believer who is afraid is one in whom love has not yet been brought to its end, which is the condition the letter has been addressing since 2:5. The verse does not say such a man is unregenerate, and reading it that way would make fear a mark of lostness in a letter that tells its readers their sins are forgiven.

4:17's ground is stated as a present fact. καθὼς ἐκεῖνός ἐστιν, καὶ ἡμεῖς ἐσμὲν ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ τούτῳ is an indicative, not a goal. The confidence at the judgment rests on a correspondence that already holds.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Five divergences fall in these ten verses and none is large.

4:19. The Byzantine text reads ἀγαπῶμεν αὐτόν, we love him; NA28 omits the object and leaves the verb absolute. The Byzantine reading makes the verse about love returned to God, the critical reading about love as such, which eases the transition to v. 20.

4:20. The Byzantine text ends with the rhetorical question πῶς δύναται ἀγαπᾷν; NA28 reads οὐ and makes it a flat denial. The question is the stronger rhetoric, the denial the plainer statement, and the sense is the same.

5:2. The Byzantine text reads τηρῶμεν, we keep; NA28 reads ποιῶμεν, we do. The Byzantine verb is the one used at 2:3, 3:22 and 3:24 of keeping commandments, which is in its favor here.

5:4. The Byzantine tradition is itself divided between ἡ πίστις ἡμῶν and ὑμῶν, our faith or your faith. Robinson–Pierpont prints the first.

5:1, 5:5. NA28 brackets καί in 5:1 and adds a bracketed δέ in 5:5. Neither affects the argument.
