Introduction to 1 John 3:11–24

The love of the brother, announced at 3:10 without explanation, now becomes the subject. Verse 11 grounds it in the message heard from the beginning, v. 12 gives the negative example, and vv. 14–18 work out what loving and not loving amount to. Then the passage turns inward: vv. 19–22 deal with a believer whose own heart accuses him, and vv. 23–24 close with the commandment stated in two parts. Two verses here are regularly pressed into service as tests of whether a person has eternal life. Verse 14 makes loving the brethren the reason John's readers know they have passed from death to life, and v. 15 says that no murderer has eternal life abiding in him. Both repay close attention to what is actually said: v. 15 does not say that such a man lacks eternal life but that he lacks it μένουσαν, abiding in him, and v. 14 is addressed to people John has already told their sins are forgiven. Verse 23 is the plainest statement in the letter of what God commands, and it names believing first.

That is the hinge into v. 13: hatred of the righteous by those whose works are evil is the pattern, and the world's hatred of the readers is the same pattern repeated.

This verse is regularly offered as a test by which a person may determine whether he has eternal life, and the grammar repays care. ἡμεῖς stands first and emphatic, against ὁ κόσμος of v. 13. μεταβεβήκαμεν is a perfect: we have crossed over and stand on the far side, a completed and permanent transfer from ἐκ τοῦ θανάτου to εἰς τὴν ζωήν.

The second ὅτι is where readings part. ὅτι ἀγαπῶμεν τοὺς ἀδελφούς can be causal, giving the reason the knowledge is held, or it can be the content of an inference drawn from experience. Either way the verse states a reason for a knowledge rather than a condition for a transfer: the crossing stands in the perfect and is treated as accomplished, and what the loving supplies is awareness of it.

The register of that awareness is set by the verse's own second half, and this is the key to the whole pair of verses. μένει ἐν τῷ θανάτῳ uses the letter's abiding verb of a sphere a man remains in, which is plainly a matter of where he is living rather than of a sentence passed on him. Read consistently, the death and life of the first half belong to the same register. ζωή in the Johannine writings is not only a status held but a quality enjoyed — it is the word of John 10:10, where Christ comes that his own may have life περισσόν, in abundance — and the passage from death to life is the passage into that realm and its experience. So the loving of the brethren is not a credential produced to prove a transfer took place; it is the sign that eternal life is governing the man who has it, which is Hodges' fellowship reading stated from the positive side.

The vocabulary is borrowed and the borrowing should be acknowledged. μεταβέβηκεν ἐκ τοῦ θανάτου εἰς τὴν ζωήν is John 5:24 almost word for word, where the passage follows believing and nothing else. John is not redefining that here; he is applying its language to what his readers may know of a life already received. Verse 15 then says the same thing from the other side, and the participle there is what makes the pair cohere. NA28 omits τὸν ἀδελφόν, leaving the participle without an object.

ἀνθρωποκτόνος is a man-killer, and John applies it to hatred on the principle Christ stated in Matthew 5:21–22: the settled attitude is the act in its root. μισῶν is again a present participle, as in 2:9 and 2:11.

The second clause is the one that matters, and the participle at the end of it is easy to read past. οὐκ ἔχει ζωὴν αἰώνιον ἐν ἑαυτῷ μένουσαν does not say that such a man has no eternal life. It says he does not have it μένουσαν, abiding, in him. μένουσαν is a present active participle in the accusative feminine, agreeing with ζωήν, and it is the letter's own abiding verb, used here of eternal life as an operative reality rather than as a possession. Dropping the participle turns the verse into a statement about who has life at all, which is not what John wrote, and sets the sentence against 5:13.

Taken with v. 14 the two verses are one thought seen from both sides. Loving the brethren is where the abiding control of eternal life shows itself in a man; man-killing shows that eternal life is not abiding in him. Neither verse is measuring whether he possesses it. Both are describing whether it is governing him, which is the question the letter has been asking since 1:6.

The point also holds whichever way the subject is taken. If the murderer in view is an unbeliever, the clause is true of him in both senses and nothing is lost; if John is warning believers, as the οἴδατε addressed to them suggests, the participle is exactly what keeps the warning from contradicting the letter's own assurances. NA28 reads αὐτῷ for the Byzantine reflexive ἑαυτῷ.

ἐν τούτῳ points forward to the ὅτι clause, and ἐγνώκαμεν is the perfect of acquaintance used since 2:3. What is known is τὴν ἀγάπην itself, with the article: love as such has been defined by an act.

καταγινώσκῃ is to know something against someone, hence to condemn, and the present subjunctive with ἐάν makes the self-accusation a live and recurring possibility for the readers. μείζων with the genitive τῆς καρδίας is a comparison, and the clause that follows explains the comparison: καὶ γινώσκει πάντα, and he knows all things.

Whether that is a comfort or a threat depends on how the greatness is taken, and both readings have had defenders. If God's superior knowledge means he sees more grounds for condemnation than the heart does, the verse is a warning; if it means he sees the whole case where the heart sees only the accusation, it is a reassurance. The context decides it: v. 19 is about quieting the heart and vv. 21–22 are about confidence, so the sentence is moving toward assurance rather than away from it. Hodges reads it that way, and the structure of the paragraph is the strongest argument for doing so.

The verse closes the chapter and opens the test that chapter 4 will apply. ὁ τηρῶν τὰς ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ is the articular participle again, and the result is mutual: ἐν αὐτῷ μένει, καὶ αὐτὸς ἐν αὐτῷ, with the abiding verb carried over to both.

The second half introduces the Spirit for the first time in the letter. ἐν τούτῳ γινώσκομεν points forward to the closing phrase, and what is known is ὅτι μένει ἐν ἡμῖν, that he abides in us. The ground is ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος, ἐκ once more of source, and the relative οὗ is genitive by attraction to its antecedent rather than accusative as its own clause would require. ἔδωκεν is aorist: a giving already done. The assurance offered at the chapter's end rests on something God has given rather than on something the readers must sustain.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 3:11–24

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The section answers the clause left hanging at 3:10. Verse 11 states the message, v. 12 gives the negative example in Cain, v. 13 draws the consequence for the readers, and vv. 14–18 work out what loving and failing to love come to. Then the passage turns inward at v. 19 and stays there through v. 22, dealing with a believer whose own heart accuses him, before vv. 23–24 close with the commandment in two parts and the first mention of the Spirit.

Cain is the structural hinge rather than an illustration added for color. His hatred is traced ἐκ τοῦ πονηροῦ, the same construction as 3:8, and the reason given is a contrast of works. That pattern — the evil hating the righteous — is then applied to the readers in v. 13, so the world's hatred is presented as the Cain pattern repeated rather than as a new difficulty.

The movement from v. 19 is a change of subject that is easy to miss. Up to v. 18 the question is what love looks like; from v. 19 the question is what to do when a believer's heart will not let him rest in it. That is a pastoral problem, not a forensic one, and the vocabulary follows: πείθω of quieting the heart, καταγινώσκω of its accusation, and παρρησία of the freedom that follows.

II. Key Theological Themes

Love as the content of the message. ἀγγελία occurs only twice in the letter, at 1:5 and here. The first announcement is that God is light; the second is that we should love one another. The two are the letter's poles, and vv. 16–18 define the second by an act rather than by a disposition: Christ laid down his life, and the obligation that follows is stated with ὀφείλομεν, the same verb as 2:6.

Love tested by a case, not by a feeling. Verse 17 reduces the whole matter to a man with means, a brother in need, and a closed heart. The word for the means is βίος, deliberately the same word as 2:16, so what the world offers a man to boast in is what he is now asked to spend.

A heart that accuses, and a God who is greater. Verses 19–22 are the letter's only sustained treatment of a believer's self-doubt. The construction in v. 20 is the hardest in the epistle and its force depends on how the greatness is taken: if God's fuller knowledge means he sees more to condemn, the verse warns; if it means he sees the whole case where the heart sees only the charge, it reassures. The paragraph is moving toward παρρησία in v. 21 and answered prayer in v. 22, which settles the direction.

One commandment, in two tenses. Verse 23 is the letter's own summary of what God commands, and ἡ ἐντολὴ αὐτοῦ is singular with two clauses under it. The first verb is aorist, πιστεύσωμεν, a thing done; the second is present, ἀγαπῶμεν, a thing kept doing.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

Verse 15 denies the abiding, not the having. This is the single most important observation on the page and it rests on one participle. οὐκ ἔχει ζωὴν αἰώνιον ἐν ἑαυτῷ μένουσαν has μένουσαν as a present active participle in the accusative feminine, agreeing with ζωήν. John does not write that such a man has no eternal life; he writes that he does not have it abiding in him, which is the letter's own vocabulary for an operative rather than a merely possessed reality. Most English versions keep the participle, and readings that treat the verse as a bare denial of possession have quietly dropped it.

Verses 14 and 15 are one thought from two sides. μεταβεβήκαμεν is a perfect, so the crossing is complete and the readers stand on the far side; the second ὅτι supplies why they know it. What kind of knowing is in view is fixed by the verse's own second half, where μένει ἐν τῷ θανάτῳ describes a sphere a man remains in rather than a sentence passed on him. Read consistently, the death and life of the first half belong to the same register, and ζωή in John is a quality enjoyed as well as a status held — the word of John 10:10, where Christ comes that his own may have it περισσόν. So loving the brethren is where the abiding control of eternal life shows in a man, and the man-killing of v. 15 shows that eternal life is not abiding in him. Neither verse measures whether he possesses it; both describe whether it is governing him. Treating v. 14 instead as a test by which a man determines whether he has crossed reverses the order of the sentence and sets it against 5:13, where that knowledge rests on believing.

Verse 22 promises answered prayer, not eternal life. Keeping commandments appears here as the ground of confidence in asking, which is what the paragraph has been about since v. 19. It functions as it did at 2:3, as the condition of an experienced relation rather than of a standing one.

Verse 23 puts believing first and makes it an act. The aorist πιστεύσωμεν against the present ἀγαπῶμεν is the letter stating its own order. A reading that makes continuing love a condition of eternal life has to reverse the tenses John chose.

Verse 24 grounds the assurance in a gift. The chapter ends with ἐκ τοῦ πνεύματος οὗ ἡμῖν ἔδωκεν, with an aorist: a giving already done. What the readers are told to rest on is something God has given rather than something they must sustain.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Nine divergences fall in these fourteen verses, and two of them touch the sense.

3:19. The Byzantine text reads the present γινώσκομεν, by this we know; NA28 reads the future γνωσόμεθα, we shall know, and brackets the opening καί. The Byzantine reading states a present assurance where the critical text promises a future one. NA28 also reads the singular τὴν καρδίαν for the Byzantine plural.

3:21. The Byzantine text repeats ἡμῶν on both sides of the verb, our heart does not condemn us; NA28 brackets the first and omits the second, leaving the verb without an expressed object.

The remaining seven are minor. NA28 prefixes a bracketed καί in v. 13 and omits μου there and in v. 18; it omits τὸν ἀδελφόν in v. 14, leaving the participle without an object; it reads αὐτῷ for the Byzantine reflexive ἑαυτῷ in v. 15; it reads the aorist θεῖναι for the present τιθέναι in v. 16, where the present better suits a standing obligation; it reads ἀπ᾽ for παρ᾽ in v. 22; and it adds ἡμῖν to the closing clause of v. 23.
