Introduction to 1 John 3:1–10

This is the hardest page in the letter for any reader, and the hardest for a Free Grace reader in particular, because 3:6 and 3:9 appear to say flatly that a believer does not and cannot sin — while 1:8 has already said that anyone claiming to be without sin deceives himself. The two statements are in the same letter, eighteen verses apart, and no reading that ignores either is worth having. Three features of the Greek carry most of the weight here: the tenses, which are present throughout and describe conduct in progress rather than a settled fact about a person; the preposition ἐκ, which runs through vv. 8–10 marking the source of conduct exactly as it did at 2:16 and 2:21; and μένω in v. 6, which is still the abiding verb the letter has used since 2:6. Hodges reads the section as describing two sources from which conduct comes rather than two classes of people distinguished by destiny, and that reading is what the page sets out. Verses 1–3 come first, and they are the ground: what the readers already are, what they will be, and what that hope does in them now.

Ἰδετε is an aorist imperative, a summons to look at something rather than to consider it, and ποταπήν asks what sort or what country a thing comes from, so the love is being marveled at for its kind rather than measured for its size. δέδωκεν is a perfect: the Father has given it and it stands given.

The ἵνα clause states what the love produced rather than what it aimed at. τέκνα θεοῦ κληθῶμεν uses an aorist passive subjunctive of the verb to call or name, and the name is not a courtesy: v. 2 will say ἐσμέν, we are. The Byzantine text ends the sentence there. NA28 adds καὶ ἐσμέν, and we are, which makes explicit what the Byzantine text leaves v. 2 to say. The second half explains the world's incomprehension by an aorist, οὐκ ἔγνω αὐτόν: it did not come to know him, so it does not recognize his children. The Byzantine text reads ὑμᾶς, you, where NA28 reads ἡμᾶς, us.

Ἀγαπητοί is the third address of the letter, and the verse is built on a contrast of times. νῦν τέκνα θεοῦ ἐσμέν is a present indicative with νῦν fronted: the sonship is a present fact, not a prospect. Against it stands οὔπω ἐφανερώθη, not yet made manifest, of what they will be.

What follows is stated as knowledge, not as hope. οἴδαμεν is the perfect of settled knowing, and the content is ὅμοιοι αὐτῷ ἐσόμεθα, we shall be like him, with a future indicative rather than a subjunctive. The ground given is ὅτι ὀψόμεθα αὐτὸν καθώς ἐστιν, because we shall see him as he is. Nothing here is conditional on the readers' conduct, which is worth marking before vv. 4–10 arrive: the certainty John builds the next paragraph's exhortation on is a certainty he has just stated unconditionally. NA28 omits the connective δέ.

The verse draws the practical consequence, and καὶ ties it to what precedes. πᾶς ὁ ἔχων is the first of seven such articular participles in this section, a construction that names a type by its characteristic activity rather than counting individuals.

ἀγνίζει ἑαυτόν is present and reflexive: the purifying is the man's own ongoing act, which is precisely what vv. 6 and 9 will be accused of denying. The standard is καθὼς ἐκεῖνος ἁγνός ἐστιν, with the remote demonstrative John reserves for Christ, and the hope is set ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ, on him, so the motive is the expectation of v. 2 rather than fear of loss.

A definition, and the article is what makes it one. ὁ ποιῶν τὴν ἁμαρτίαν has the article on the object, so the activity is sin in the general sense rather than a particular sin, and καὶ τὴν ἀνομίαν ποιεῖ says the same of lawlessness.

The second clause is the one to weigh. ἡ ἁμαρτία ἐστὶν ἡ ἀνομία puts the article on both the subject and the predicate, which in Greek makes the two terms convertible: not merely that sin involves lawlessness but that the two are the same thing. That matters for the whole section, because it rules out narrowing the sin of vv. 6 and 9 to some special or deliberate class of offense. Whatever John means by not sinning, he means it of sin as such, defined here as broadly as it can be defined.

οἴδατε again assumes the readers' knowledge, and ἐκεῖνος is Christ. ἐφανερώθη is the aorist passive used of the incarnation at 1:2, and the purpose clause ἵνα τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν ἄρῃ uses an aorist subjunctive of αἴρω, to lift and carry away. NA28 omits ἡμῶν, which widens the statement from our sins to sins as such.

The second clause supplies the premise vv. 6–9 will reason from: ἁμαρτία ἐν αὐτῷ οὐκ ἔστιν, anarthrous and therefore qualitative. There is no sin in him at all. The argument of the paragraph runs from that: since sin has no place in Christ, it has no place in what is drawn from Christ, and conduct that is sinful is therefore drawn from somewhere else. That is the thread v. 8 picks up with ἐκ.

Everything turns on the tenses, and all four verbs of the first half are present. ὁ ἐν αὐτῷ μένων is the abiding participle the letter has used since 2:6, and οὐχ ἁμαρτάνει is a present indicative. What the present tense settles on its own, however, is less than is often claimed for it. The tense grammaticalizes imperfective aspect, which is how the writer chooses to portray an action rather than what kind of action it was; whether the sense is progressive, customary, iterative or gnomic is supplied by context, by the lexeme and by the construction. A gnomic present, stating a timeless generality, would in fact suit the reading this page declines. So the tense opens the question rather than settling it. What settles it here is the surrounding argument: 1:8 forbids any claim to be without sin, 2:1 tells believers what to do when they have sinned, and v. 9 supplies its own explanation in the seed clause. Within those limits the clause says that abiding and sinning do not occupy the same moment: so far as a man is abiding, he is not sinning, and the two are alternatives rather than a test and its result.

The second half is harder and is where the Free Grace reading has to be careful. πᾶς ὁ ἁμαρτάνων is again a present participle, but the two verbs that follow are perfects: οὐχ ἑώρακεν αὐτόν, οὐδὲ ἔγνωκεν αὐτόν. Taken as a statement about a man's history, this would mean that anyone sinning has never seen or known Christ, which would empty 1:8 of sense and would convict John himself by his own ἐὰν εἴπωμεν. Hodges takes the perfects as experiential rather than biographical: the seeing and knowing that the letter has been describing since 2:3, the acquaintance a believer draws on while he walks in the light, is not what the man is operating out of in the act of sinning. Whatever else he has seen and known, he is not seeing or knowing in that moment.

Two things keep this from being special pleading. The first is that the letter has already set the terms: 2:3–6 made knowing God a matter of acquaintance tested by present obedience, and this verse uses the same two verbs. The second is 1:8, which stands in the way of every reading that makes v. 6 a statement about who sins at all. The question is not whether a believer sins — John has settled that — but what is true of him while he does.

The warning is placed exactly where a reader of v. 6 would need it, which is itself a clue to how v. 6 was expected to be taken. μηδεὶς πλανάτω is a third-person present imperative with the strong negative: let nobody be deceiving you. The same verb appeared at 1:8 of self-deception and at 2:26 of the men who went out.

The deception in view is evidently the claim that conduct and character can be held apart, which is what the false teachers of chapter 2 were urging. John's answer is not that conduct determines standing but that conduct reveals its own source. The Byzantine text addresses Τεκνία; NA28 reads Παιδία.

ἐκ τοῦ διαβόλου is the phrase that decides the paragraph, and the letter has already taught the reader how to take it. At 2:16 what is in the world is ἐκ τοῦ κόσμου, and at 2:21 no lie is ἐκ τῆς ἀληθείας. In both places ἐκ traces something to its source rather than assigning a person to a parentage. The subject here is likewise an articular present participle, ὁ ποιῶν τὴν ἁμαρτίαν: the one whose practice is sin draws that practice from the devil.

The causal clause supports it the same way. ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς ὁ διάβολος ἁμαρτάνει is present, a continuing activity from the beginning, so the devil is named as the origin of that kind of conduct. Christ was manifested against the works, which is what the paragraph has been classifying all along.

The verse states twice that the one born of God does not sin, and a Free Grace reading has to take both halves as they stand rather than soften them. πᾶς ὁ γεγεννημένος ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ is a perfect passive participle: the birth is complete and permanent, which is itself an assurance statement before it is anything else. ἁμαρτίαν οὐ ποιεῖ is present, as in v. 6.

The reason given is the key: σπέρμα αὐτοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ μένει, his seed remains in him. σπέρμα is what is sown and what carries the nature of its source, and μένει is the letter's abiding verb used of the thing God has placed in the believer. Hodges takes the seed to be the divinely begotten element in the regenerate person, and the second clause then follows: οὐ δύναται ἁμαρτάνειν, with a present infinitive, is said of what has been born of God rather than of the believer considered in every respect. What is born of God does not sin and cannot; the believer, who is more than that element while he is in the body, both can and does, which is why 1:8 stands and why this same letter tells believers what to do when they have sinned.

Read that way the verse is an assurance rather than a threat. The causal clause that closes it, ὅτι ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ γεγέννηται, is a second perfect, and it grounds the impossibility in the completed birth rather than in the believer's performance. The alternative readings each cost something. Making the present tenses mean habitual practice turns the verse into a measurement a believer must take of his own life, which is the kind of assurance John elsewhere refuses to build; making it describe every regenerate person without qualification contradicts 1:8 outright. The seed clause is John's own explanation, and it points to a source rather than to a record.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 3:1–10

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The page divides at v. 4. Verses 1–3 are about what the readers are and will be, and every verb in them is indicative or future except the opening imperative: the Father has given, we are, we shall be, we shall see. Verses 4–10 then classify conduct, and the argument there is built almost entirely out of one construction. Seven times John writes an articular present participle — ὁ ἔχων, ὁ ποιῶν three times, ὁ μένων, ὁ ἁμαρτάνων, ὁ μὴ ἀγαπῶν — and each names a type by what it characteristically does rather than counting individuals.

The order is not incidental. John establishes the standing first and classifies the conduct afterwards, which means the exhortation of vv. 4–10 rests on an assurance that has already been stated unconditionally in vv. 1–2. Verse 3 is the hinge: the one who has this hope purifies himself, so the motive supplied for the whole section is expectation rather than anxiety.

Three terms recur and carry the logic. ποιέω runs through vv. 4, 7, 8, 9 and 10 of what a man does; ἐκ runs through vv. 8, 9 and 10 of where it comes from; and μένω appears in vv. 6 and 9, in the second case of the seed rather than of the believer.

II. Key Theological Themes

Sonship stated before conduct is examined. Verse 2 says νῦν τέκνα θεοῦ ἐσμέν with a present indicative, and the likeness to come is a future indicative resting on οἴδαμεν. Nothing in either verse is conditioned on the readers. Whatever vv. 6 and 9 mean, they cannot mean something that unsettles what vv. 1–2 have just settled.

Sin defined as widely as possible. Verse 4 puts the article on both ἡ ἁμαρτία and ἡ ἀνομία, making them convertible terms rather than overlapping ones. That closes off the escape route of reading the sin of vv. 6 and 9 as some special or deliberate class of offense. John means sin as such.

Conduct traced to a source. The function of ἐκ in vv. 8–10 is the same as at 2:16, where what is in the world is ἐκ τοῦ κόσμου, and at 2:21, where no lie is ἐκ τῆς ἀληθείας. In each case the preposition traces something to its origin. Read consistently, vv. 8–10 classify conduct by where it comes from rather than assigning persons to a parentage and therefore to a destiny.

What is born of God, and the believer. Verse 9 gives its own reason: σπέρμα αὐτοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ μένει. Hodges takes the seed to be the divinely begotten element in the regenerate person, and the impossibility of sinning is predicated of that rather than of the believer considered in every respect. The believer is more than that element while he is in the body, which is why 1:8 forbids him to claim he has no sin and why 2:1 tells him what to do when he has sinned.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

Verses 6 and 9 must be read with 1:8, not around it. The same letter says that anyone claiming to be without sin deceives himself and that the one born of God does not sin. Any reading that quietly drops one of these is not reading the letter. The two can only stand together if v. 9 is saying something other than that a regenerate man never commits an act of sin, and the verse itself supplies the qualification in the seed clause.

The tenses are present throughout, but the tense alone proves nothing. It is worth being exact here, because the argument is often made badly on both sides. The Greek present grammaticalizes imperfective aspect, which is how a writer chooses to portray an action; it does not by itself tell you what kind of action is meant. Progressive, customary, iterative and gnomic uses are all available, and which one is in play is settled by context, by the lexeme and by the construction. That cuts against the habitual reading, which treats the present as if it meant customary practice by definition, and it cuts equally against resting this page's reading on the tense alone, since a gnomic present stating a timeless generality would serve the other side. What the tenses do establish is that John is portraying conduct in progress rather than recording a verdict; what decides how to take that portrayal is 1:8, 2:1 and the seed clause of v. 9.

The perfects in v. 6 are experiential, not biographical. οὐχ ἑώρακεν and οὐδὲ ἔγνωκεν use the two verbs the letter has been using of acquaintance since 2:3. Taken as a claim about a man's history they would convict John himself, who wrote ἐὰν εἴπωμεν of sin in 1:6–10. Taken of the knowledge a believer is drawing on, they say what is not operating in him while he sins.

Verse 10 says the difference shows, not that it can be measured. φανερά ἐστιν means made visible. John does not offer a test by which a man may determine his own standing, which is what he says plainly at 5:13 rests on believing, and the verse's second half immediately narrows the whole discussion to one relation, loving the brother, which 3:11 takes up as the message heard from the beginning.

What the alternatives cost. Reading the present tenses as habitual practice — the regenerate man does not habitually sin — turns the verse into a measurement a believer must take of his own life, and the measurement has no threshold John supplies. Reading it absolutely, of every regenerate person in every respect, contradicts 1:8 in the same letter. The seed clause is John's own explanation of what he means, and it points to a source rather than to a record.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Five divergences fall in these ten verses, and two of them touch the sense.

3:1. The Byzantine text ends the first sentence at κληθῶμεν; NA28 adds καὶ ἐσμέν, and we are. The addition makes explicit what the Byzantine text leaves v. 2 to state in its own words, so the theology is the same either way. Later in the same verse the Byzantine text reads ὑμᾶς, the world does not know you, where NA28 reads ἡμᾶς, us.

3:5. NA28 omits ἡμῶν, so the purpose of the manifestation is to take away sins rather than specifically our sins. The Byzantine reading is the narrower and the more personal.

3:2, 3:7. NA28 omits the connective δέ in v. 2 and addresses Παιδία in v. 7 where the Byzantine text reads Τεκνία. Neither affects the argument.
