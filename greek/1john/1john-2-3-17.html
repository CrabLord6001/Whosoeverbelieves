Introduction to 1 John 2:3–17

The section takes up the question 1:5–2:2 left standing. If fellowship with a God who is light is the thing in view, how does a believer know he is in it? Verse 3 answers with ἐν τούτῳ γινώσκομεν, a formula John uses repeatedly from here on, and the test he gives is obedience. That makes the passage a natural place to go wrong. Read as a test of whether a person has eternal life, vv. 3–6 and 9–11 produce exactly the works-based assurance the letter's own prologue excluded; read as Hodges reads them, they test the believer's present walk, which is the subject John has been on since 1:6. Verse 12 settles the question of audience beyond argument, since the sins of the people addressed are stated as already forgiven and given as the reason for writing rather than as something to be secured. Verses 7–11 turn on the commandment to love a brother, and vv. 15–17 on the one rival affection the letter names. The Greek text follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, which differs from NA28 at three points here, one of them substantive.

The verse pairs two tenses of the same verb and the pairing carries the argument. γινώσκομεν is present, an ongoing awareness, while ἐγνώκαμεν is perfect: we have come to know him and stand in that knowledge. γινώσκω in the Johannine writings regularly carries the sense of acquaintance deepened by experience rather than bare information.

What the test is a test of decides the theology of the whole paragraph. ἐὰν τὰς ἐντολὰς αὐτοῦ τηρῶμεν is a third-class condition with a present subjunctive, so the keeping is habitual. Hodges takes the knowledge in view to be the experiential acquaintance a believer has with God while walking in his light, not the fact of regeneration, and the context supports that reading rather than the other. The formula ἐν τούτῳ points forward to the condition, and the whole sentence sits inside a discussion that began at 1:6 with fellowship and has not changed subject. Read as a test of eternal life it would also contradict 5:13, where John says the knowledge of having eternal life rests on believing.

The construction is the same one used of claims in 1:6, 1:8 and 1:10, now in the third person. ὁ λέγων is an articular present participle, which names a type of person by what he characteristically says, and the participle μὴ τηρῶν under the same article makes the saying and the not-keeping one description.

ψεύστης ἐστίν repeats the charge of 1:6 and 1:10. The closing clause is the same as 1:8 with one word changed: ἡ ἀλήθεια οὐκ ἔστιν, now ἐν τούτῳ rather than ἐν ἡμῖν, and the same reading holds: a truth that is not operating, rather than a person who was never given it. The point of the verse is that the claim and the conduct contradict each other, and John says so without pronouncing on the man's destiny. NA28 adds ὅτι before the quoted claim, marking it as reported speech; the Byzantine text sets it out directly.

ὃς δ’ ἀὴν τηρῇ is an indefinite relative clause, relative plus ἄν plus present subjunctive, so it describes whoever is doing the keeping rather than a known person. τὸν λόγον stands where v. 3 had the plural commandments, which widens the test from particular instructions to the whole word.

τετελείωται is a perfect passive of τελειόω, a verb meaning to bring to its intended end rather than to make faultless. The love of God is not improved by the believer's obedience; it reaches in him the end it was aimed at. ἀληθῶς stands first in its clause for emphasis. The second ἐν τούτῳ opens a fresh sentence that runs into v. 6, and the thing known this time is ὅτι ἐν αὐτῷ ἐσμέν, being in him, which is the letter's language for abiding rather than for the new birth.

This is the verse that most clearly shows what kind of writing the paragraph is. ὀφείλει is the verb of owing, used of a moral debt, and it governs the infinitive περιπατεῖν. John does not say that the one who abides in Christ will walk as Christ walked. He says he ought to, which is the language of obligation and presupposes that the thing can fail to happen.

That matters for how vv. 3–6 are used. An obligation laid on a believer is not a diagnostic by which his regeneration is measured; it is a duty he may meet or neglect, and the letter spends its energy urging him to meet it. καθὼς ἐκεῖνος uses the remote demonstrative, which John reserves for Christ, and περιεπάτησεν is aorist, taking the earthly life as a completed whole. NA28 brackets οὕτως as doubtful; the Byzantine text prints it, and it makes the correspondence explicit.

Πάλιν turns the denial of v. 7 around without withdrawing it. The commandment is old in origin and new in the situation now obtaining, and the ὅ clause says where it is found true: ἐν αὐτῷ καὶ ἐν ὑμῖν, in Christ and in the readers both.

The ὅτι clause gives the ground. παράγεται is present, so the darkness is in the act of passing rather than gone, and the same verb returns at v. 17 of the world. Against it τὸ φῶς τὸ ἀληθινὸν with the article repeated makes the adjective emphatic, and ἠδη φαίνει places the shining already in the present. The verse is a statement about the age the readers are living in, not a command, and it supplies the reason the commandment can be called new.

This verse decides the audience of the letter, and with it the reading of 1:9. ὅτι here is causal rather than content: John writes because of what is already true of them. What is already true is ἀφέωνται, a perfect passive of ἀφίημι, so their sins have been forgiven and stand forgiven.

The grammar leaves no room for forgiveness as something still to be secured. It is the premise of the letter, not its goal, and it is stated of τεκνία, the same address as 2:1. The ground given is διὰ τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, on account of his name, which locates the cause in Christ rather than in anything the readers did or confessed.

That raises the obvious question, and it is better answered here than left to the reader. If these sins already stand forgiven, what is being asked for in 1:9, and what is being cleansed in 1:7? Hodges answers that the two are different transactions rather than two attempts at one, and three features of the text support him. The first is the frame: John states his purpose as κοινωνία in 1:3, before any of this, so everything from 1:5 to 2:2 sits inside a discussion about maintaining a shared walk rather than about how a person passes from death to life.

The second is the tenses, which John did not write the same way. ἀφέωνται here is a perfect, a settled condition given as the ground of writing, while ἀφῇ in 1:9 is an aorist subjunctive, a particular act of forgiving in answer to a particular confession. The same split runs through the cleansing, where 1:7 has καθαρίζει in the present, continuous and concurrent with the walking, against the once-for-all aorist Paul uses of the believer's washing at 1 Corinthians 6:11. A standing state and a repeated act are not the same thing.

The third is that John's own Gospel supplies the picture. At John 13:10 Jesus tells Peter that the one who has bathed needs only to wash his feet. λελουμένος is perfect, the bath complete and not repeated, while νίψασθαι is what the walk keeps requiring. The bath is this verse; the feet are 1:9. The distinction is made by John rather than imported into him.

This also explains why the forgiveness of 1:9 costs God nothing. He forgives because he is πιστὸς καὶ δίκαιος, and δίκαιος is righteous rather than merely fair. Forgiving a confessed sin compromises nothing, because the blood named in 1:7 has already satisfied the claim. The judicial work this verse reports as finished is what makes the family forgiveness of 1:9 possible, so the two are not in tension: the second rests on the first. The debt was paid once and is not repaid, and what confession restores is a relationship a son has disrupted rather than the standing he was given.

Two things can be said in fairness. The distinction between judicial and family forgiveness is drawn from the fellowship frame rather than stated in so many words, and ἀφίημι is ordinarily a judicial word, so a reader may fairly ask whether splitting it does real work. And Hodges reaches his reading of 1:9 by taking this verse as the fixed point, where a reader who reverses the order gets a different letter. What carries the most weight is that John nowhere makes confession a condition for receiving eternal life, while he makes believing that condition repeatedly, 5:13 included.

The verse defines τὸ ἐν τῷ κόσμῳ from v. 15 with three articular nouns in apposition, and the three are a classification rather than a list of examples. ἐπιθυμία is simply strong desire, taking its moral color from its object, and the two genitives after it are most naturally subjective: the desire the flesh has, the desire the eyes have.

ἀλαζονεία is the third, a word for boastful pretension, and τοῦ βίου is life in the sense of means and circumstances rather than ζωή, the life John has been writing about since 1:1. The distinction is deliberate: what the world offers is a display built on βίος, while what was manifested in the prologue is ζωή. The closing contrast οὐκ ἔστιν ἐκ τοῦ πατρός, ἀλλ’ ἐκ τοῦ κόσμου uses ἐκ of origin, so the three are being traced to their source rather than merely disapproved.

παράγεται returns from v. 8, where it was the darkness that was passing; here it is the world and ἡ ἐπιθυμία αὐτοῦ, its desire. The present tense again puts the passing in progress, which is the argument against loving it: the thing is going even as it is being loved.

The contrast is the verse's point and it is easy to overread. ὁ δὲ ποιῶν τὸ θέλημα τοῦ θεοῦ is set against the world, not against another class of men, and μένει εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα uses the letter's abiding verb, which describes continuance rather than entry. Hodges would not take this as a test distinguishing the saved from the lost, and the grammar does not press that way either: the one doing God's will abides, where the world does not, and the comparison is between two things with different futures rather than between two destinies for two kinds of people. Eternal life in this letter is received by believing, as 5:13 says in so many words.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 2:3–17

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The section has four movements and they are not of one kind. Verses 3–6 test the claim to know God by obedience; vv. 7–11 restate the test as the commandment to love a brother; vv. 12–14 break off into direct address and state what is already true of the readers; vv. 15–17 name the rival affection. The third of those is the one to read first, because it fixes to whom the other three are addressed.

Verses 3–11 are built on a repeated figure. Three times John writes ὁ λέγων, an articular participle naming a man by what he says, and each time a second participle under the same article supplies the conduct that contradicts it: not keeping the commandments (v. 4), hating a brother (v. 9). The construction is the third-person form of the ἐὰν εἴπωμεν conditions of 1:6, 1:8 and 1:10, so the whole run from 1:6 to 2:11 is one sustained argument about claims a believer may make and the conduct that tests them.

Verses 12–14 change the register completely. Six clauses, each a vocative with a causal ὅτι, and in every one the reason given is something the readers already have: sins forgiven, knowledge of the one from the beginning, victory over the evil one, strength, the word abiding. Every verb in those reasons is a perfect or a present indicative. Nothing in the paragraph is asked of them; it is all reported to them.

II. Key Theological Themes

Knowing God as acquaintance. The verb of vv. 3–5 is γινώσκω, which in John regularly describes knowledge gained and deepened by living with someone rather than knowledge of a fact. The perfect ἐγνώκαμεν in v. 3 and ἐγνώκατε in vv. 13 and 14 describe that acquaintance as a standing possession. What vv. 3–6 test is not whether it exists but whether the man claiming it is living consistently with it.

Obligation rather than guarantee. Verse 6 is the hinge of the paragraph and is often read past. John writes ὀφείλει, he owes it, with an infinitive. That is the vocabulary of duty, and duty presupposes that the thing can be left undone. Had John meant that abiding in Christ produces the corresponding walk automatically, the verse would be a prediction rather than an obligation and the rest of the letter's exhortation would be unnecessary.

Light, darkness and the brother. Verses 9–11 take the light-and-darkness language of 1:5–7 and attach it to one relation. The man who hates a brother is ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ, which is where 1:6 located the believer who claims fellowship while walking in darkness, and the consequence John names is disorientation rather than condemnation: he does not know where he is going.

The world as a rival affection. Verses 15–17 do not ask the readers to leave the world but to stop loving it, and the ground given in v. 16 is one of origin rather than of morality. The three terms are a classification of everything the world holds, and the last of them, ἡ ἀλαζονεία τοῦ βίου, turns on a word John has carefully not used of the life he announced in the prologue. What the world offers is a display built on βίος; what was manifested in 1:1–2 is ζωή.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

Verse 12 settles the audience, and 1:9 with it. The ὅτι of v. 12 is causal, and what it reports is ἀφέωνται, a perfect passive: their sins have been forgiven and stand forgiven, on account of his name. That is the premise of the letter rather than its goal. Any reading of 1:9 that makes confession a condition for obtaining eternal life has to explain how the same people, eleven verses later, are told their sins are already forgiven and are given that as the reason John is writing to them at all.

Verses 3–6 are not a test of regeneration. Three things tell against that use of them. The knowledge in view is acquaintance rather than the fact of the new birth; v. 5 states the result as ἐν αὐτῷ ἐσμέν, the letter's abiding language; and v. 6 frames the corresponding walk as an obligation. Read as a test of eternal life the paragraph also collides with 5:13, where knowing that one has eternal life rests on believing in the name of the Son of God.

Verses 9–11 concern a believer's sight, not his destiny. The man is blinded by ἡ σκοτία itself, not by God or the devil, and what he loses is the knowledge of where he is going. The parallel with 1:6 is exact, and in 1:6 John puts himself inside the condition with ἐὰν εἴπωμεν. A reading that makes the hater an unbeliever has to break that parallel and supply a verdict the text withholds.

Verse 17 is a contrast of futures, not of destinies. ὁ δὲ ποιῶν τὸ θέλημα τοῦ θεοῦ is set against the passing world, not against a second class of men, and the verb is μένει, which the letter uses of continuing rather than of entering. The verse says that what is done in God's will lasts where the world does not. It is not offering abiding as the evidence by which a man may conclude he is saved.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Four divergences fall in this section, one of them structural.

2:4. NA28 prefixes ὅτι to the quoted claim, which marks it as reported speech. The Byzantine text sets the claim out directly. No difference in sense.

2:6. NA28 brackets οὕτως as doubtful; the Byzantine text prints it, making the correspondence between the believer's walk and Christ's explicit rather than implied.

2:7. Two differences in one verse. The Byzantine text addresses Ἀδελφοί where NA28 reads Ἀγαπητοί, and the Byzantine text repeats ἀπ’ ἀρχῆς at the end of the verse where NA28 omits it. The repetition closes the sentence on the phrase it opened with, which suits the verse's argument that the commandment is old.

2:13. The one divergence here that changes the shape of a paragraph. The Byzantine text reads Γράφω in the third address of v. 13 where NA28 reads Ἑγραψα. On the Byzantine text vv. 13–14 give three presents answered by three aorists, a clean two-panel structure; on NA28 they give two presents and four aorists, and the paragraph divides after the second clause of v. 13 instead. The content of what is said to each group is unaffected either way.
