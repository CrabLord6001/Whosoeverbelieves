Introduction to 1 John 5:6–21

The letter ends by stating why it was written, and 5:13 is the sentence the whole series has been working toward: John writes to those believing on the name of the Son of God ἵνα εἰδῆτε ὅτι ζωὴν αἰώνιον ἔχετε, that you may know that you have eternal life. The verb is a present indicative and the knowledge is the point of the book. Verses 6–12 build to it by setting out the witness, and 5:11–12 state the gift in possession language that 5:13 then makes the reader's own. The rest of the page is the letter's closing: confidence in prayer at vv. 14–15, the sin leading to death at vv. 16–17, three things the readers know at vv. 18–20, and a final word at v. 21. Two textual matters belong here. Verses 7–8 are where the Comma Johanneum stands in the Textus Receptus, the Greek text behind the King James and the Modern English Version, and the Byzantine Majority Text does not contain it; and 5:13 is the third and last place where the recording and the printed text differ in length.

οὗτός points back to the Son of God named in v. 5, and ὁ ἐλθὼν δι᾽ ὕδατος καὶ αἴματος is an aorist participle with διά and the genitive, which here marks attendant circumstance rather than agency: he came by way of water and blood.

What the water is has been read two ways, and both are defensible. On the first, it is the baptism, where the Father spoke and the Spirit descended, so that the Spirit of the second half of this verse was a witness at that event alongside the Father. On the second, it is the water that came from the pierced side at the crucifixion, which John reports at John 19:34 and attaches his own testimony to in the next verse; on that reading the water and the blood are joined because they came out together, and both belong to the death rather than one to the baptism and one to the cross. The second has the advantage that this writer has already put the two side by side once, in his Gospel, in that order. The first has the advantage of explaining the denial that follows, since a teaching that divided the Christ from Jesus at the cross would have been happy to let him come ἐν τῷ ὕδατι μόνον, in the water only.

The denial that follows shows what the statement is aimed at. οὐκ ἐν τῷ ὕδατι μόνον excludes a teaching that would allow the Christ to come at the baptism and depart before the cross, which is the error the test of 4:2–3 was framed against. NA28 repeats the preposition in the second member. Then the Spirit is introduced as witness: τὸ πνεῦμά ἐστιν τὸ μαρτυροῦν, an articular participle making the witnessing his defining activity, and the ground is that the Spirit is ἡ ἀλήθεια with the article, the truth itself.

In the Byzantine Majority Text the verse is short and contains no Trinitarian formula: ὅτι τρεῖς εἰσὶν οἱ μαρτυροῦντες, for there are three who bear witness. The three are named in v. 8.

This is the place where the Textus Receptus adds the words commonly called the Comma Johanneum, naming the Father, the Word and the Holy Spirit in heaven. The Textus Receptus is the Greek text behind the King James and, among modern versions, the Modern English Version, which is why the clause is familiar to many readers who have never met it in a Greek manuscript. The Byzantine Majority Text does not contain them, which is worth stating plainly because the Majority Text and the Textus Receptus are often assumed to agree here and they do not. The external evidence is the reason: the clause is absent from every Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century, appearing then only in a handful whose readings follow the Latin; it is absent from the ancient versions apart from the Latin; and it is not quoted by the Greek fathers in the Trinitarian controversies, where a verse naming three heavenly witnesses would have been decisive. The doctrine of the Trinity does not rest on it, and this series prints the Byzantine text as it stands.

The three are named with the article on each: τὸ πνεῦμα, τὸ ὕδωρ and τὸ αἷμα, all three neuter, and they take up the water and blood of v. 6 with the Spirit added. εἰς τὸ ἕ εἰσιν is idiomatic for agreeing: the three tend toward one thing, which is to say their testimony converges.

Which three they are depends on how the water of v. 6 was taken, and the two readings give two coherent sets. If the water is the baptism, the three are spread across the ministry: the Spirit, who descended and remained; the water, where the Father's voice was heard; and the blood, the death the baptism pointed to. On that reading the Spirit and the Father are joined as witnesses at the one event, which is why the Spirit is named first here and introduced as the witness at the end of v. 6. If the water is what came from the pierced side, the three converge on the cross instead: the blood and the water came out together at John 19:34, so they are naturally paired, and the Spirit is added as the continuing witness to what they attest. Either way the convergence is the point John is making, and the set is complete without any addition.

The grammatical point that is usually raised here deserves a direct answer. οἱ τρεῖς is masculine while the three nouns are neuter, and defenders of the Comma argue that the masculine requires masculine antecedents, which only the added clause supplies. The construction does not require it. Greek regularly uses a masculine with neuter nouns where the nouns are treated as persons or as witnesses acting, which is precisely what οἱ μαρτυροῦντες in v. 7 already does: that participle is masculine in the short text, before any of the three has been named. The agreement is therefore with the sense rather than with the grammatical gender of the nouns, and the masculine in v. 8 follows the masculine already standing in v. 7. The argument from gender proves nothing the short text cannot account for.

A first-class condition taking the premise as granted: εἰ τὴν μαρτυρίαν τῶν ἀνθρώπων λαμβάνομεν, since we do accept human testimony. The genitive is of source, testimony that men give.

The apodosis argues from the lesser to the greater: ἡ μαρτυρία τοῦ θεοῦ μείζων ἐστίν. Then the content is given, and the Byzantine text supplies a relative: ἣν μεμαρτύρηκεν περὶ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ, which he has testified concerning his Son, where NA28 reads ὅτι and makes the following clause the content of the testimony rather than its description. μεμαρτύρηκεν is a perfect: the testimony has been given and stands.

Two participles divide the verse, and the prepositions after them differ in a way worth marking. ὁ πιστεύων εἰς τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ takes εἰς with the accusative, believing into the Son, which is the Fourth Gospel's characteristic construction for saving faith; the negative half has ὁ μὴ πιστεύων τῷ θεῷ with the plain dative, not believing God, that is, not crediting his testimony.

The result of believing is ἔχει τὴν μαρτυρίαν ἐν αὐτῷ, has the testimony in himself, where NA27 reads the reflexive ἑαυτῷ. The result of not believing is put as strongly as the letter ever puts anything: ψεύστην πεποίηκεν αὐτόν, a double accusative with a perfect, he has made God a liar and the verdict stands. The reason is a second perfect, οὐ πεπίστευκεν. Unbelief is treated here not as a deficiency but as a judgment passed on God's own word.

Every element of the Free Grace reading of this letter is in that clause. The knowledge is the stated purpose of the book, so a reading that makes assurance unattainable or provisional defeats the author's own aim. The Byzantine text is the longer here, at both ends, and this is the third and last of the three places where the recording and the printed page differ in length. On the Byzantine reading the letter has two aims, assurance and continuance in faith, and the second is stated in the tense of continuing. Word-level highlighting drifts through this verse, and of the three places where it does, this is the one a reader is most likely to be studying closely.

The first of three sentences beginning οἴδαμεν, and it returns to 3:9 in nearly the same words: πᾶς ὁ γεγεννημένος ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ οὐχ ἁμαρτάνει. What is added here is the second clause, and it supplies a different explanation from the one 3:9 gave.

The participle changes tense, which is easy to miss. ὁ γεννηθεὶς ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ is aorist where the first was perfect, and the two editions then part on the object. The Byzantine text reads τηρεῖ ἑαυτόν, keeps himself, making the believer the subject throughout; NA27 reads αὐτόν, keeps him, which takes the aorist participle as Christ, the one begotten of God, who keeps the believer. The difference is a rough breathing and it changes who does the keeping. The Byzantine reading fits the letter's practice of telling believers what to do, and v. 21 ends with a command of exactly that kind. The closing clause is the same either way: ὁ πονηρὸς οὐχ ἁπτεται αὐτοῦ, with a verb meaning to lay hold of, and the middle voice with the genitive.

What the evil one cannot lay hold of is worth being exact about, because the verse is often read as though it promised a believer immunity from attack, which it plainly does not. What it says is that there is something in him the evil one does not touch. Read with 3:9, that something is what has been born of God, the σπέρμα that remains in him; and read with 3:5, where ἁμαρτία ἐν αὐτῷ οὐκ ἔστιν, the reason it is untouchable is that sin has no place in what comes from God. The begotten element gives the evil one nothing to take hold of.

Two consequences follow, and they are the practical use of the verse. A believer's salvation is not something Satan or his agents can take from him, because the part of him that is born of God is outside their reach altogether; and the same clause tells against the idea that a believer may be possessed and made to act against his own will, since what is in him from God is precisely what cannot be laid hold of. The verse is not a promise that a Christian is immune to demonic oppression.

It is also the last of the letter's many uses of κόσμος, and it confirms the sense the word has carried throughout. The world here is the ordered system under the evil one, as it was at 2:15–17, and it is stated without restriction: the whole of it lies there. A reading that narrows κόσμος at 2:2 to a select group has to let it mean the whole race here, in the same letter, by the same writer, in the same unqualified construction.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 5:6–21

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The close has four movements. Verses 6–10 establish the witness; vv. 11–13 state what the witness amounts to and why the letter was written; vv. 14–17 take up prayer, including the hardest case; and vv. 18–21 end with three things the readers know and a final command.

Verse 13 is the structural center of the whole letter and not merely of this page. John has spent five chapters applying tests, and he now says in one sentence what the tests were for: that those believing into the name of the Son of God may know that they have eternal life. The Fourth Gospel states its purpose in the same shape at John 20:31, and the two statements should be read together.

The ending is built on repetition. οἴδαμεν opens vv. 18, 19 and 20 in succession, each introducing something settled, and the letter which began with what the apostles had heard and seen ends with what the readers know.

II. Key Theological Themes

The witness, and what refusing it amounts to. Verses 9–10 argue from the lesser to the greater and then state the alternative in the strongest terms the letter has used: ψεύστην πεποίηκεν αὐτόν, a perfect, he has made God a liar and it stands. Unbelief is treated as a verdict passed on God rather than as a deficiency.

Eternal life as a gift already given and located in a person. Verse 11 uses an aorist, ἔδωκεν, and then places the life ἐν τῷ υἱῷ αὐτοῦ. Verse 12 draws the consequence with one verb used four times: having the Son is having the life. No degrees, no evidence, no growth — the division is on possession of a person.

Prayer, and a sin that is not prayed about. Verses 14–15 make the condition κατὰ τὸ θέλημα αὐτοῦ and move from hearing to having. Verses 16–17 then give the exception, and v. 17's second clause is what keeps it bounded: not every sin leads to death.

Three certainties and a command. The readers know that the one born of God does not sin, that they are of God while the whole world lies in the evil one, and that the Son of God has come and given understanding. Then one aorist imperative: keep yourselves from idols.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

5:13 is the letter's stated purpose and the series' anchor. ἵνα εἰδῆτε is the perfect subjunctive of settled knowledge and ἔχετε a present indicative: that you may know that you have eternal life, now. Three things follow. Assurance is the author's aim, so a reading that makes it unattainable or provisional defeats the book. The thing known is a present possession rather than a future hope. And the addressees are identified by believing and by nothing else, which is why the letter's tests have been read throughout this series as concerning fellowship and the operation of life rather than its possession.

5:16 concerns a brother, and the life given is not the life he has. τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ is the word used of a fellow believer throughout the letter. Since the man is already a brother, the ζωή promised in answer to intercession cannot be the eternal life he already possesses. Hodges takes it as the preservation of physical life, and 1 Corinthians 11:30 shows the New Testament treating bodily death as something a believer's sin can bring on. Readings that make the sin leading to death a loss of salvation have to give ἀδελφός a sense it carries nowhere else here.

5:18 depends on a variant that changes who does the keeping. The Byzantine text reads τηρεῖ ἑαυτόν, the believer keeps himself; NA27 reads αὐτόν, so that the one begotten of God — Christ — keeps him. A rough breathing divides them. The Byzantine reading suits a letter that ends by telling its readers to guard themselves, and the change of participle from perfect to aorist is accounted for either way.

5:20's final identification most likely names the Son. οὗτός can refer to the Father, just called ὁ ἀληθινός, or to the Son, who is named immediately before and is the nearer antecedent. The second predicate decides it for this series: ζωὴ αἰώνιος is what 5:11–12 has just said is in the Son, and what 1:2 announced at the letter's opening. Read so, the letter ends where it began.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

5:7–8, the Comma Johanneum. The words naming the Father, the Word and the Holy Spirit as three heavenly witnesses stand in the Textus Receptus — the Greek text behind the King James and the Modern English Version — and are not in the Byzantine Majority Text. That is worth saying plainly, because the two are often assumed to agree here. The clause is absent from every Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century and appears then only in a few whose readings follow the Latin; it is absent from the ancient versions apart from the Latin; and the Greek fathers do not quote it in the Trinitarian controversies, where three named heavenly witnesses would have settled the argument. The grammatical case sometimes made for it — that the masculine οἱ τρεῖς in v. 8 needs masculine antecedents — does not hold, because οἱ μαρτυροῦντες in v. 7 is already masculine in the short text, before any of the three neuter nouns has been named. The agreement is with the sense, as Greek regularly allows where things are treated as witnesses acting.

5:13, the third and last length divergence. The Byzantine text is the longer at both ends: it names the addressees before the purpose clause and then adds a second purpose, καὶ ἵνα πιστεύητε, with a present subjunctive. NA28 reads simply ἔγραψα ὑμῖν, places the participial phrase after the purpose clause, and has no second ἵνα. On the Byzantine reading the letter has two stated aims, assurance and continuance in believing. The recording is the shorter, so word-level highlighting drifts here, and of the three verses where it does this is the one a reader is most likely to be studying closely.

The remaining divergences are small. NA28 repeats the preposition in v. 6 and reads ὅτι for ἣν in v. 9; NA27 reads the reflexive ἑαυτῷ in v. 10 and αὐτόν in v. 18; NA28 reads ἀπ᾽ for παρ᾽ in v. 15, as at 3:22; the Byzantine tradition is divided on the articles in the last phrase of v. 20 and between ἑαυτά and ἑαυτούς in v. 21; and NA28 omits the closing Ἀμήν.
