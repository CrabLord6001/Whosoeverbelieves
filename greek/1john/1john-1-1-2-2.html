Introduction to 1 John 1:1–2:2

The letter opens without a main verb. Four relative clauses pile up in v. 1, the parenthesis of v. 2 interrupts them, and the sentence does not land until ἀπαγγέλλομεν in v. 3, where Hodges locates the purpose of the whole epistle. That purpose is κοινωνία, fellowship, and it governs how the rest of this page reads. Verses 5–10 then set out the terms of walking in God's light, and 2:1–2 answer the obvious objection. The section matters for a Free Grace reader chiefly because of 1:9, which is routinely offered as a condition for receiving eternal life. The grammar resists that. John writes in the first person plural from v. 6 onward, placing himself inside the group that sins and confesses, and Hodges notes that nowhere in the Johannine writings is confession made a condition for obtaining eternal life, where believing is the single condition throughout. The Greek text follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with NA28 divergences noted where they occur.

The opening is deliberately impersonal. Ὅ is a neuter relative, repeated four times, so what John announces is a what rather than a who. Hodges takes this as intentional: the person of Christ is not the theme here, but the eternal life that was with the Father and was manifested. The imperfect ἦν with ἀπ’ ἀρχῆς sets that life in continuous past existence rather than at a point.

The four verbs are not of one tense, and the difference carries weight. ἀκηκόαμεν and ἑωράκαμεν are perfects, and both show Attic reduplication, where the initial vowel lengthens and repeats rather than taking the ordinary consonantal reduplication. The perfect leaves the apostles' hearing and seeing as a standing possession. The two that follow, ἐθεασάμεθα and ἐψηλάφησαν, are aorists, and Hodges notes that these two are not repeated in v. 3 while the two perfects are. The gazing and the handling belong to a closed past; the hearing and seeing continue. ψηλαφάω is the verb used of the risen Christ's invitation in Luke 24:39, and it rules out any claim that contact with him was less than physical.

The verse is a parenthesis, which is why the WEB brackets it. Both occurrences of ἐφανερώθη are aorist passive, so the manifesting is a completed act done to the life rather than by it, and the agent left unstated is God.

Three present indicatives follow, ἑωράκαμεν excepted as a perfect: μαρτυροῦμεν and ἀπαγγέλλομεν are ongoing, which is what an apostolic witness does rather than did. τὴν ζωὴν τὴν αἰώνιον repeats the article, a construction that makes the adjective emphatic, so the life is named and then characterized. πρὸς τὸν πατέρα is the same preposition and construction as John 1:1, where the Word was πρὸς τὸν θεόν, and it carries the sense of being face to face with rather than merely beside. Hodges stresses that the revelation in view was made to the apostles themselves, which is the force of ἡμῖν at the end.

The main verb of the sentence begun in v. 1 finally arrives. ἀπαγγέλλομεν takes up the relative Ὅ of v. 1 and the purpose clause states why.

κοινωνία is the word on which the whole letter turns. It means things held in common, shared undertakings and shared possessions, and Hodges insists the fellowship here is not fellowship in general but fellowship with the apostolic circle, into which the readers are invited. The present subjunctive ἔχητε makes it ongoing rather than a single attainment. The second half of the verse then widens it. ἡ κοινωνία ἡ ἡμετέρα places the possessive in attributive position, our own fellowship, and that fellowship is μετὰ τοῦ πατρὸς καὶ μετὰ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ, with the preposition repeated so that neither is subordinate to the other. To be in fellowship with John's circle is therefore to be in fellowship with God.

ταῦτα is fronted and points forward to what the letter will say, as it does again at 2:1 and 5:13. γράφομεν is first person plural here where 2:1 shifts to the singular γράφω, which keeps the apostolic company in view a little longer.

The construction at the end is worth slowing down for. ῆ πεπληρωμένη is periphrastic, a present subjunctive of εἰμί with a perfect passive participle, and the combination stresses a settled resulting state rather than the act of filling. The joy is to stand full. The Byzantine ἡμῶν makes it the writers' joy where NA28 reads ἡμῶν as well in most editions; Hodges takes the fullness to come from seeing those they led to Christ remain true to the faith.

αὕτη stands before its noun and points forward to the ὅτι clause, so the message is defined by what follows rather than by anything already said. ἀγγελία occurs in the New Testament only here and at 3:11.

The two halves are a positive and a negative statement of one thing. ὁ θεὸς φῶς ἐστίν has no article on φῶς, which makes it qualitative, describing what God is like rather than identifying him with a thing. The denial is then piled up: σκοτία ἐν αὐτῷ οὐκ ἔστιν οὐδεμία puts a negative particle and a negative adjective on the same clause, which in Greek intensifies rather than cancels. Hodges renders the force as darkness is not in him, none, and reads it as aimed at teachers who allowed for darkness within the Deity itself.

Ἐὰν with the subjunctive is a third-class condition, which presents the case as a live possibility rather than a fact. The same construction opens vv. 8, 9 and 10, so the four stand as a set.

The first person plural is the feature a Free Grace reader should mark. John does not write if they say. He writes εἴπωμεν, placing himself inside the group that might make the claim, and he keeps the first person through v. 10. περιπατῶμεν is present subjunctive and therefore habitual, a manner of life rather than a stumble. Hodges takes the claim to be a lie because a believer walking in sin has lost touch with a God in whom there is no darkness, and he is careful about what the verse does and does not settle. It establishes that such a person is out of fellowship, not that he is unregenerate. οὐ ποιοῦμεν τὴν ἀλήθειαν is a Semitic idiom, doing the truth, which means acting consistently with it.

This is the verse most often pressed into service as a condition for receiving eternal life, and the grammar and the context both tell against that use of it. ὁμολογῶμεν is a present subjunctive of a verb meaning to say the same thing as, hence to agree or acknowledge, so confession here is agreeing with what the light has already shown. The tense makes it ongoing practice rather than a single transaction.

Hodges states the case plainly and it deserves stating as his. Confession of sin keeps believers in fellowship; it is nowhere in the Johannine writings a condition for obtaining eternal life, where faith is the single condition. The word for repentance appears nowhere in this epistle. And the grounds of the forgiveness are God's own character, πιστὸς ἐστιν καὶ δίκαιος, where δίκαιος is righteous rather than merely fair, which means that forgiving a confessed sin costs God's righteousness nothing because the blood of v. 7 has already satisfied it. The two aorist subjunctives ἀφῇ and καθαρίσῃ then divide the result, and Hodges reads the second as reaching further than the first: the sins confessed are forgiven, and πᾶσα ἀδικία, including what the believer never noticed, is cleansed.

The unbeliever remains alienated from the life of God, and what Revelation 20:12 pictures at the last judgment is men judged by their works rather than by sin as such. The propitiation is unlimited in its sufficiency; the life is received by believing.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 1:1–2:2

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The section is built in three unequal movements. Verses 1–4 are the prologue, one sentence whose main verb is withheld through four relative clauses and a parenthesis until ἀπαγγέλλομεν in v. 3. Verses 5–10 are a set of four third-class conditions, three of them negative and one positive, which test claims a believer might make about his own state. Then 2:1–2 answer the objection those conditions raise. The hinge is v. 5, which supplies the premise for everything that follows. Because God is light with no darkness in him, a believer's walk either corresponds to that or does not, and the four conditions work out both cases.

The conditions are not arranged at random. Verses 6, 8 and 10 each begin ἐὰν εἴπωμεν or its equivalent and each end with a clause describing what is not in us, and the three claims they refute grow in boldness: that one can walk in darkness and still have fellowship, that one has no sin as a principle, that one has not sinned at all. Verse 7 and v. 9 interrupt that series with the alternative, and both make the remedy something God does rather than something the believer achieves. The symmetry is the argument.

The first person plural is maintained from v. 6 to the end of the section, breaking only at the singular γράφω of 2:1. John places himself inside every claim he refutes. Nothing in the section is addressed to outsiders.

II. Key Theological Themes

Fellowship as the purpose of the letter. κοινωνία appears three times in these ten verses and only once more in the epistle. Hodges makes it the announced purpose of the whole letter rather than a theme of the prologue only, and the structure bears him out, since the purpose clause of v. 3 is the first thing the delayed main verb produces. The word means shared possession and shared undertaking. Verse 3 grounds it in the apostolic witness and then extends it to the Father and the Son, with μετά repeated so that the two are not ranked.

Light and the exposure of sin. Walking in the light is not the absence of sin. Verse 7 says that those walking in the light are being cleansed from all sin, which presupposes sin in those who walk there, and v. 8 forbids the claim to have none. The light is where sin is seen, and seeing it is the condition of confessing it. That is why v. 9 follows v. 8 rather than contradicting it.

Confession as a fellowship matter. This is where the section bears most directly on Free Grace exegesis, and it is worth stating the distinction precisely. Eternal life in the Johannine writings is received by believing and is never offered on any other term. Confession in 1:9 belongs to a different question: not how a person passes from death to life, but how a believer who has sinned is restored to the shared walk described in v. 7. Hodges puts the point as a flat denial that confession is anywhere made a condition for obtaining eternal life, and three features of the passage support it. The addressees are believers from v. 6 onward and are addressed as τεκνία at 2:1. The word for repentance is absent from the epistle. And the ground of the forgiveness is God's faithfulness and righteousness rather than the adequacy of the confession.

Advocacy and propitiation. The two titles in 2:1–2 answer two different anxieties. παράκλητος addresses what happens to a believer who sins, and Hodges restricts its office to the believer's faith rather than to his security, which the promises of the Fourth Gospel have already settled. ἱλασμός addresses the sufficiency of the satisfaction, and the explicit exclusion of a limit in the second half of 2:2 makes the propitiation unlimited in its scope. The inference that all are therefore saved does not follow, because removing sin as a barrier is not the same act as giving life.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

1:6 does not make a sinning believer an unbeliever. The verse establishes that such a person is lying when he claims fellowship. It says nothing about his standing. The distinction between fellowship and sonship runs through the whole section, and collapsing the two is the usual route by which 1:6–10 is turned into a test of regeneration.

1:8 and 1:10 describe a word failing to operate, not a person lacking it. The two clauses are parallel, ἡ ἀλήθεια οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν ἡμῖν and ὁ λόγος αὐτοῦ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐν ἡμῖν, and in both the pronoun is first person plural. Hodges reads them as describing truth that has lost its controlling force in a believer. A reading that makes them marks of an unregenerate person has to break the pronoun.

The aorists in 2:1 concern acts, not a condition. ἵνα μὴ ἁμάρτητε and ἐάν τις ἁμάρτῃ are both aorist subjunctive. John aims at the individual act of sin and concedes the individual act. He is not contrasting two classes of person, one that sins and one that does not, and 3:9 should be read in the light of this verse rather than against it.

2:2 states the unlimited scope in so many words. John could have written simply that Christ is the propitiation for our sins. That he adds οὐ περὶ τῶν ἡμετέρων δὲ μόνον and then ἀλλὰ καὶ περὶ ὅλου τοῦ κόσμου removes the ambiguity deliberately. The restriction of κόσμος here to the elect requires reading the word against the clause in which it stands.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Four divergences fall inside this section, and none of them touches the argument.

1:3. The Byzantine text reads ἀπαγγέλλομεν ἡμῖν; NA28 adds καί before the pronoun, “we declare also to you.” The purpose clause that follows already carries the καί, so the sense is the same either way.

1:4. Where the Byzantine text has γράφομεν ἡμῖν, NA28 reads γράφομεν ἡμεῖς, making the pronoun an emphatic subject rather than an indirect object. In the same verse the Byzantine tradition is itself split on the joy: Robinson–Pierpont prints ἡ χαρὰ ἡμῶν, our joy, while a substantial part of the tradition and the Textus Receptus read ὑμῶν, which is why the King James has “your joy.” Hodges reads the fullness as belonging to the writers, which suits the printed Byzantine reading.

1:7. NA28 omits the postpositive δέ that opens the verse in the Byzantine text, and omits Χριστοῦ from τὸ αἷμα Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτοῦ. The δέ marks the turn from v. 6 explicitly; without it the contrast is still carried by the sense.

2:1–2. The two verses are undisputed between the editions.
