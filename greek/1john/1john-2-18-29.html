Introduction to 1 John 2:18–29

The letter turns outward. Verses 18–27 are about people who have left, what their leaving showed, and what the readers already have that keeps them from being taken in; 2:28–29 close the movement and open the next. Two verses here carry more Free Grace weight than any others on the page. Verse 19 is routinely quoted to prove that a professing believer who departs was never saved, and v. 28 is read by some as a warning about losing salvation. In both cases the governing verb is μένω, the letter's word for continuing rather than for entering, and in both cases Hodges reads the issue as the readers' standing in the apostolic fellowship and their confidence at Christ's appearing rather than their possession of eternal life. The section is also the most textually varied on the site so far: eight divergences between the Byzantine text and NA28 fall in these twelve verses, and one of them, at 2:23, is a whole clause that the audio reads but the printed text here does not.

ἐσχάτη ὥρα has no article, which makes it qualitative: a last-hour kind of time rather than a datable final sixty minutes. The same anarthrous phrase closes the verse, so the statement is made twice and the body of the verse supplies the evidence between them.

The evidence is the plural. The readers had heard that ὁ ἀντίχριστος is coming, singular and articular, and John's point is that ἀντίχριστοι πολλοὶ have already appeared. γεγόνασιν is a perfect: they have come into being and are here. ὅθεν then draws the inference, and the word for the inference is γινώσκομεν, the same verb of recognition the letter has been using since 2:3. NA28 omits the article before ἀντίχριστος, which slightly weakens the contrast between the one expected and the many present.

This is the verse most often produced to prove that anyone who abandons the faith was never a believer, and the first thing to notice is what John actually says about the departure. The failure he charges them with is not a failure to believe but a failure to remain: the verb is μένω, and πιστεύω does not appear in the verse at all. The contrary-to-fact clause runs μεμενήκεισαν ἄν μεθ᾽ ἡμῶν, a pluperfect with ἄν: they would have remained with us. Remaining is the letter's subject, and it is where they failed.

The phrase that bears the weight is ἐξ ἡμῶν, used three times. ἐκ with the genitive here marks belonging to a company rather than describing a spiritual birth, and the company is the one John has been writing from since 1:1, where ἡμεῖς is the apostolic circle that heard and saw. Read that way the verse says these men never belonged to the apostolic fellowship and their going out proved it, which is exactly what the purpose clause states: ἵνα φανερωθῶσιν, that they might be made manifest.

Hodges resists the inference that the verse settles their eternal destiny, and the grammar does not supply it. John writes neither that they were unregenerate nor that they had never believed; he writes that they did not belong and did not stay. The common paraphrase, that they went out because they were never really saved, imports a category the sentence does not contain and then uses the sentence to prove it. What the verse does establish is that departure from the apostolic doctrine is a reliable mark of never having shared it. πάντες at the end is emphatic: not one of them belonged. NA28 reads the later aorist form ἐξῆλθαν and orders the second clause differently, neither of which affects the sense.

χρίσμα is the thing smeared on rather than the act of smearing, and the New Testament uses the noun only here and twice in v. 27. The source is τοῦ ἁγίου, the Holy One, which in Johannine usage is Christ.

The verse answers the threat of v. 19 with something the readers already possess, and the tense says so: ἔχετε is present and οἴδατε is a perfect with present force. Nothing is being urged on them here. The Byzantine text reads οἴδατε πάντα, you know all things, where NA28 reads πάντες, you all know. One letter divides them, and the Byzantine reading makes the object of the knowledge the point while NA28 makes its universality among the readers the point. Either way the claim is that the anointing has already equipped them, which is what v. 27 will develop.

Three ὅτι clauses hang on one verb, and the first two are a denial and its correction. οὐκ ἔγραψα ὑμῖν ὅτι οὐκ οἴδατε is answered by ἀλλ᾽ ὅτι οἴδατε αὐτήν. John writes to people who know, not to people who need informing.

That is worth pausing on, because it governs how the whole letter's tests should be read. The author assumes his readers' knowledge of the truth as the ground of writing, exactly as 2:12–14 assumed their forgiveness and victory. The third clause, πᾶν ψεῦδος ἐκ τῆς ἀληθείας οὐκ ἔστιν, uses ἐκ of origin as in 2:16, and it supplies the test the next verses will apply: a teaching is judged by its origin.

The question is rhetorical and the answer is given in the same breath. εἰ μή introduces the only exception, so the definite article on ὁ ψεύστης is not loose: this is the liar in the proper sense, the one the letter has in view.

What makes him so is a denial with content. ὁ ἀρνούμενος is a present participle, a settled position rather than a lapse, and the thing denied is ὅτι Ἰησοῦς οὐκ ἔστιν ὁ Χριστός. The second half widens it. Denying that Jesus is the Christ turns out to be denying τὸν πατέρα καὶ τὸν υἱόν, because the two cannot be separated, and that is the argument v. 23 states as a principle.

PTCP., substantival] τὸν υἱόν [DIRECT OBJECT] PREDICATE οὐδὲ τὸν πατέρα [OBJECT — οὐδέ: not even, so the loss follows rather than being added] ἔχει [VERB — possession language, as at 5:12] [NA28 adds: ὁ ὁμολογῶν τὸν υἱὸν καὶ τὸν πατέρα ἔχει. The Byzantine text prints only the negative half: everyone denying the Son does not have the Father either. οὐδέ is the word doing the work, meaning not even or neither, so the loss is presented as following automatically rather than as a second penalty.

ἔχει is the verb, and it is worth marking what it is not. John does not write that such a man has not known the Father or has lost him, but that he does not have him, which is the same possession language used of eternal life at 5:12. The verse is therefore a statement about where the Father is to be had, not a measurement of someone's sincerity.

This is the first of three places in the letter where the Byzantine text and the Westcott–Hort text used for the audio differ in length, and the difference here is a whole clause. NA28 adds the positive counterpart, that the one confessing the Son has the Father also. Its absence from the Byzantine majority is easy to explain mechanically, since both halves end in ἔχει and a copyist's eye can jump from the first to the second; the King James carries the clause in italics for the same reason. The reading is printed here as the Byzantine text has it, and the recording reads the longer form, so the audio runs ahead of the page at this verse.

Overall Exegesis: 1 John 2:18–29

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The section is framed by a crisis and answered by a possession. Verses 18–19 report that men have left; vv. 20–21 answer with what the readers already have; vv. 22–23 define what the departed deny; vv. 24–25 tell the readers what to do with what they heard and what is promised them; vv. 26–27 name the occasion and repeat the answer of v. 20 at length; and 2:28–29 close with the one imperative the section has been building toward and a sentence that opens the next movement.

The repetition is deliberate. καὶ ὑμεῖς opens both v. 20 and v. 27, each time with an emphatic nominative that has no verb of its own, and Ὀμεῖς opens v. 24 the same way. Three times John sets his readers over against the men who went out, and each time what distinguishes them is something already given rather than something still to achieve: the anointing, the word they heard, the promise.

One verb holds the section together. μένω appears six times in these twelve verses — of the departed who would have remained, of the word that is to remain in the readers, of the readers remaining in the Son and the Father, of the anointing that remains, and of the command to remain in him. Every occurrence concerns continuance in something already entered. That is the key in which vv. 19 and 28 should be read.

II. Key Theological Themes

What a departure proves. Verse 19 is the letter's test of a teacher rather than its test of a soul. The men went out, and John's inference is οὐκ ἦσαν ἐξ ἡμῶν, they did not belong to the apostolic company, with the purpose clause stating the result as manifestation. The paragraph is concerned with doctrine and fellowship, and v. 22 makes explicit what the departed hold: a denial that Jesus is the Christ.

The anointing as sufficiency, not independence. Verses 20 and 27 are easily overread. John is himself teaching the readers as he writes that they have no need to be taught, so the claim cannot be absolute. The teachers excluded are the ones who left, and the anointing is offered as the reason the readers can recognize them.

Eternal life as promise. Verse 25 is the only place in this section where eternal life is named, and it is named as ἡ ἐπαγγελία with αὐτός as the emphatic subject of the cognate verb. Nothing in the sentence is conditioned on the readers. Placed where it is, directly after the instruction of v. 24 and directly before the warning of v. 26, it functions as the fixed point against which the anxiety of v. 19 is measured.

Confidence at the appearing. Verse 28 gives the section its purpose. The pair is παρρησία and being shamed away from him, both of which befall someone who is present when Christ appears. John writes ἔχωμεν in the first person plural and so includes himself in the possibility, which is the strongest internal argument that what is at risk is a believer's confidence rather than his presence there at all.

III. Free Grace Exegetical Notes

Verse 19 does not say they were never saved. It says they did not belong and did not remain, and the verb withheld from them is μένω, not πιστεύω. ἐξ ἡμῶν marks membership in the company John writes from, the ἡμεῖς of 1:1 who heard and saw. Hodges declines to read a verdict on their destiny into the sentence, and the grammar supplies none: the common paraphrase, that they left because they were never really saved, introduces a category the verse does not contain and then treats the verse as proof of it. What the verse does establish is that leaving the apostolic doctrine is a reliable mark of never having shared it.

Verse 28 concerns loss of confidence, not loss of life. μένετε is an imperative addressed to people already in him, and shame ἀπ᾽ αὐτοῦ at his appearing is what a believer may suffer when his life is assessed. A reading that makes the ashamed turn out to have been unbelievers has to give μένω a sense it carries nowhere else in the letter and has to explain John's first person plural.

Verse 29 runs from the practice to the birth, not the other way. The sentence says that the one doing righteousness has been born of him, which is a statement of origin and of family likeness. Turned into a test by which a person decides whether he has been born of God, it would set the letter against 5:13, where that assurance rests on believing, and against 2:12, where the readers' standing is reported to them rather than demanded of them. The righteousness is also practiced rather than perfect, since ὁ ποιῶν is a present participle and 1:8 has already forbidden any claim to be without sin.

Verse 24's promise is continuance, not entry. The three forms of μένω move from an imperative to a conditional subjunctive to a future indicative, and the thing that is asked to move is the word, not the readers' sonship. If what they heard remains in them, they will remain in the Son and in the Father. Nothing in the sentence makes the relation itself contingent.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine Text and NA28

Eight divergences fall in these twelve verses, the densest stretch in the series. Most are trivial; three are worth stating.

2:23, a whole clause. The Byzantine text ends the verse at ἔχει. NA28 adds the positive counterpart, that the one confessing the Son has the Father also. Both halves end in the same word, so a copyist's eye can jump from the first to the second, which is the usual explanation for the shorter reading; the King James prints the clause in italics. This is also the first of three verses in the letter where the recording and the printed text differ in length, so the audio runs ahead of the page here.

2:27, promise against command. The Byzantine text closes the verse with μενεῖτε, a future indicative, so the clause reads as a promise; NA28 reads μένετε, a present imperative, making it a command. One letter separates them. The Byzantine reading matches the future of v. 24 and keeps the paragraph in the key of assurance.

2:28, the appearing. The Byzantine text reads ὅταν with the present subjunctive ἔχωμεν, treating the appearing as certain and the confidence as a state to be in. NA28 reads ἐὰν with the aorist σχῶμεν, which is conditional in form and makes the confidence something obtained at that moment.

The remaining five are minor. NA28 omits the article before ἀντίχριστος in v. 18, weakening the contrast between the one expected and the many present; it reads the later aorist form ἐξῆλθαν and a different word order in v. 19; it reads πάντες for πάντα in v. 20, so that the readers all know rather than know all things; it omits the inferential οὖν in v. 24; and it prefixes καί to πᾶς in v. 29, binding that sentence more tightly to what precedes.
