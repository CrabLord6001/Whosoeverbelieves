Introduction to James 4:7–17

Having announced that God "gives greater grace … to the humble" (4:6), James now spells out what humility looks like. Verses 7–10 form a rapid series of ten aorist imperatives — submit, resist, draw near, cleanse, purify, lament, mourn, weep, let your laughter be turned, humble yourselves — framed by "submit to God" (v. 7) and "humble yourselves before the Lord" (v. 10), with the promise "he will exalt you." This is a call to repentance addressed to believers who have been double-minded and worldly (4:4, 8).

Two short paragraphs then apply humility to specific sins. In vv. 11–12, speaking against a brother is shown to be an arrogant usurpation of God's role as the one Lawgiver and Judge. In vv. 13–17, the merchant who plans his year without reference to God is reminded that life is a vapor and told to say, "If the Lord wills." The section ends with a general principle: to know the good and not do it is sin (v. 17).

For a Free Grace reading, the key observations are: (1) "sinners" and "double-minded" in v. 8 are the brothers of v. 11, called to return, not to initial faith; (2) the exaltation promised in v. 10 recalls the reward themes of 1:9, 12 and 2:5; and (3) the God "able to save and to destroy" (v. 12) uses the same σῴζω language found in 1:21, 2:14, and 5:15, 20, and should be read in light of that usage.

The οὖν ties the commands directly to 4:6: because God gives grace to the humble, Ὑποτάγητε … τῷ θεῷ. Submission is the practical form of humility. The aorist imperative calls for a decisive act of realignment — the opposite of the "friendship with the world" of v. 4. Its counterpart is ἀντίστητε … τῷ διαβόλῳ: stand against the devil, the source of the "demonic" wisdom of 3:15 and the fire of Gehenna in the tongue (3:6).

The promise is striking in its simplicity: φεύξεται ἀφ᾽ ὑμῶν — "he will flee from you." The believer is not helpless before the devil. Submitted to God, he stands on ground from which the enemy retreats (cf. 1 Pet 5:8–9; Eph 6:10–13). This is addressed to believers who have been living by earthly wisdom; the way back is open, and the power is available.

ἐγγίσατε τῷ θεῷ, καὶ ἐγγιεῖ ὑμῖν — "draw near to God, and he will draw near to you." The verb comes from the vocabulary of worship: priests "drew near" to God in the sanctuary (Exod 19:22). The promise echoes the prophets' call to Israel: "Return to me, and I will return to you" (Zech 1:3; Mal 3:7). The relational language confirms the reading of 4:4: the believer's relationship with God has cooled through worldliness, and James calls him back to intimacy.

The next two commands, with two vocatives, form a chiastic pair: Καθαρίσατε χεῖρας, ἁμαρτωλοί (outward conduct) and ἁγνίσατε καρδίας, δίψυχοι (inner motives), recalling Psalm 24:3–4, "Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD? He who has clean hands and a pure heart." The address δίψυχοι closes the loop opened in 1:8: the double-minded man, unstable in all his ways, is now told what to do — purify the heart, so that it is no longer divided.

The labels ἁμαρτωλοί and δίψυχοι are sometimes taken to mean unbelievers. But the same people are "brothers" three verses later (v. 11), and James has already confessed that "we all stumble" (3:2). "Sinners" here describes believers in their sinful conduct, as in James 5:20, where a brother is called "a sinner" who has wandered. The call is to repentance within the family.

Three imperatives call for grief over sin: Ταλαιπωρήσατε καὶ πενθήσατε καὶ κλαύσατε — be wretched, mourn, weep. The worldly believer's γέλως (laughter) is to be turned to mourning, and his χαρά to κατήφεια (gloom, literally "downcast eyes"). This echoes Jesus's woe: "Woe to you who laugh now, for you will mourn and weep" (Luke 6:25), and His beatitude: "Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted" (Matt 5:4).

James is not condemning joy as such; he began the letter by commanding joy in trials (1:2). He is condemning the careless laughter of believers who are comfortable in their friendship with the world. The mourning he calls for is the grief of repentance, the "godly sorrow" that "produces repentance leading to salvation" (2 Cor 7:10) — where "salvation," as in James, means deliverance from sin's destructive course, since Paul is writing to believers in Corinth.

The paragraph closes as it began (v. 7), with a summary command and a promise: Ταπεινώθητε ἐνώπιον τοῦ κυρίου, καὶ ὑψώσει ὑμᾶς. The pattern of humiliation followed by exaltation runs throughout Scripture (1 Sam 2:7–8; Ezek 21:26; Luke 14:11; 18:14; Phil 2:8–9; 1 Pet 5:6). In James it completes a thread begun in 1:9–10: the lowly brother boasts in his ὕψος, his exaltation, while the rich brother boasts in his humiliation. Humbling oneself before God is the way to be lifted up by Him.

The exaltation promised may be both present (restored fellowship, the grace of 4:6) and eschatological (reward and honor in the kingdom; cf. 1:12; 2:5). In a Free Grace framework this exaltation is not eternal life itself — which is a gift already possessed by faith — but the lifting up of the humbled believer into God's favor and, ultimately, to shared honor with Christ (Rom 8:17b; 2 Tim 2:12).

James 4:11 — Do Not Speak Against One Another

James 4:11 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Μὴ καταλαλεῖτε ἀλλήλων, ἀδελφοί. Ὁ καταλαλῶν ἀδελφοῦ, καὶ κρίνων τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ, καταλαλεῖ νόμου, καὶ κρίνει νόμον· εἰ δὲ νόμον κρίνεις, οὐκ εἶ ποιητὴς νόμου, ἀλλὰ κριτής.
Don't speak against one another, brothers. He who speaks against a brother and judges his brother, speaks against the law and judges the law. But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law, but a judge.
Textual Note — καὶ κρίνων vs. ἢ κρίνων: (a) Byzantine: Ὁ καταλαλῶν ἀδελφοῦ, καὶ κρίνων τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ ("he who speaks against a brother and judges…"). (b) NA28/UBS5: ὁ καταλαλῶν ἀδελφοῦ ἢ κρίνων τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ ("… or judges…"). (c) Witnesses: ἤ is read by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; καί by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). (d) Significance: slight. The Byzantine καί presents slander and judging as one act (speaking against a brother is judging him); the critical ἤ presents them as two related sins. The WEB follows the Byzantine reading.

James applies humility to a specific sin of the tongue: Μὴ καταλαλεῖτε ἀλλήλων, ἀδελφοί. The verb means to speak against, run down, or slander. The address ἀδελφοί — three verses after "sinners" and "double-minded" (v. 8) — confirms that those called to repentance in vv. 7–10 are believers.

The argument is subtle. Speaking against and judging a brother is καταλαλεῖ νόμου καὶ κρίνει νόμον — speaking against and judging the law itself. Why? The law in view is the royal law, "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (2:8; Lev 19:18), and Leviticus 19:16 forbids going about as a slanderer among one's people. The one who slanders a brother violates that law and, in doing so, implicitly treats it as unworthy of obedience — placing himself above it. The conclusion follows: οὐκ εἶ ποιητὴς νόμου, ἀλλὰ κριτής. The believer was called to be a doer of the word (1:22–25); the slanderer has promoted himself to judge, a role that belongs to God alone (v. 12). This echoes 2:4, where partial believers became "judges with evil thoughts."

James 4:12 — One Lawgiver, Able to Save and Destroy

James 4:12 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Εἷς ἐστίν ὁ νομοθέτης, ὁ δυνάμενος σῶσαι καὶ ἀπολέσαι· σὺ δὲ τίς εἶ ὃς κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον;
Only one is the lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you to judge another?
Textual Note — καὶ κριτής; ὃς κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον: (a) Byzantine: Εἷς ἐστιν ὁ νομοθέτης, ὁ δυνάμενος σῶσαι καὶ ἀπολέσαι· σὺ δὲ τίς εἶ ὃς κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον; (b) NA28/UBS5: εἷς ἐστιν [ὁ] νομοθέτης καὶ κριτής, ὁ δυνάμενος σῶσαι καὶ ἀπολέσαι· σὺ δὲ τίς εἶ ὁ κρίνων τὸν πλησίον; — adding "and judge," with the participle ὁ κρίνων and πλησίον ("neighbor") instead of ἕτερον ("another"). The Textus Receptus agrees with the Byzantine text except that it lacks δέ (σὺ τίς εἶ). (c) Witnesses: the critical readings are carried by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; the Byzantine readings by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). (d) Significance: "and judge" makes explicit what the context already implies (v. 11): the one Lawgiver is also the one Judge. πλησίον ("neighbor") links the verse to the royal law (2:8); ἕτερον ("another") is more general. None of these changes the argument.

The ground of the prohibition is theological: Εἷς ἐστιν ὁ νομοθέτης — there is one Lawgiver. The fronted Εἷς echoes the Shema (2:19: "God is one") and 2:11 ("He who said … also said"). The Lawgiver is described as ὁ δυνάμενος σῶσαι καὶ ἀπολέσαι, "the one able to save and to destroy" — the power over life and death that belongs to God alone (Deut 32:39; 1 Sam 2:6; Matt 10:28). The rhetorical question then humbles the slanderer: σὺ τίς εἶ; — "you, who are you?" (cf. Rom 14:4, "Who are you to judge another's servant?").

How should "save and destroy" be read? The construction δυνάμενος σῶσαι repeats 1:21 (the implanted word "able to save your lives") and 2:14 ("can faith save him?"), and σῴζω will appear again in 5:15 and 5:20 ("save a soul from death"). In James's usage, salvation consistently concerns deliverance from sin's deadly consequences in the lives of believers. On the Free Grace reading, 4:12 affirms that God alone holds the power of life and death — He can preserve a believer's life or bring it to an end in discipline (cf. 1 Cor 11:30; Acts 5:1–11) — and so no believer may presume to pass sentence on a brother. The main point, whatever the precise scope of the verbs, is that the prerogative of judgment belongs to God, not to us.

Honesty Note: Most commentators (Moo, Davids, McKnight) take "save and destroy" as a reference to God's final judgment — eternal salvation or eternal destruction — noting that ἀπόλλυμι is often used of eternal ruin (e.g., John 3:16; Matt 10:28). This is a natural reading of the words taken by themselves, and the verse is certainly true at that level: God is the final Judge of all people. The Free Grace response is not to deny God's sovereignty over eternal destinies but to note that in James the σῴζω language consistently concerns believers and deliverance from death (1:15, 21; 5:15, 20), and that the point of v. 12 is the exclusivity of God's role as Judge rather than a statement about what happens to the slanderer. Either way, the verse forbids believers from usurping God's place.

With Ἄγε νῦν ("Come now!"), James turns to a new target — travelling merchants who plan their business confidently: a city chosen, a year allotted, trade conducted, profit assured. The four verbs in the Byzantine text are hortatory subjunctives ("let us go … let us spend … let us trade … let us profit"), giving the planning a tone of self-assured decision. Nothing in the plan is sinful in itself; the problem is what is missing — God.

Are these merchants believers? The same formula Ἄγε νῦν introduces the rich oppressors in 5:1, who appear to be outside the community. But 4:13–17 lacks the condemnation of 5:1–6 and ends with a principle for those who "know to do good" (v. 17). Most interpreters see the merchants as members (or at least attenders) of the congregation whose business lives have been conducted like "friends of the world" (4:4). Their sin is the arrogant independence James has been exposing since 4:1: living as if God were not sovereign over their plans.

The formula, later known as the conditio Jacobaea ("James's condition") and in Latin Deo volente (D.V.), appears in Paul's travel plans (Acts 18:21; 1 Cor 4:19; 16:7). This is humility (4:6, 10) applied to time and money. The believer who says "If the Lord wills" acknowledges the Lord as the one who "is able to save and to destroy" (4:12) — to preserve life or to end it — and places his plans under the sovereignty of the one Lawgiver.

Sin is not only doing what is forbidden (sins of commission) but failing to do the good one knows (sins of omission). In context, the merchants know they should acknowledge the Lord's will; failing to do so is sin. More broadly, the principle summarizes the whole letter's concern with hearing and not doing (1:22–25), with saying "go in peace" and not giving (2:15–16), and with faith that does not work (2:14–26).

For a Free Grace reader, the verse is a sobering reminder that the absence of good works in a believer's life is not neutral. James never says such a person has no faith; he says such failure is ἁμαρτία. It is sin to be confessed and forsaken (4:8–10; 5:16), sin that will be evaluated at the Judgment Seat (2:12–13), and sin that, if persisted in, brings the deadly consequences described in 1:15 and 5:20. The call is not to doubt one's salvation but to do the good one knows.

Overall Exegesis: James 4:7–17

This section surveys the architecture of the passage, its principal theological themes, its reception, and the textual situation.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The passage has three parts. (A) The call to repentance (vv. 7–10): ten aorist imperatives, framed by "submit to God" (v. 7) and "humble yourselves before the Lord" (v. 10), with three promises: the devil will flee, God will draw near, the Lord will exalt. The paragraph applies the "grace to the humble" of 4:6. (B) Against judging a brother (vv. 11–12): slander is judging, which usurps the role of the one Lawgiver and Judge. (C) Against presumptuous planning (vv. 13–17): "Come now" introduces merchants who plan without God; life is a vapor; the proper attitude is "If the Lord wills"; arrogant boasting is evil; knowing the good and not doing it is sin.

The unifying theme is humility, the practical outworking of 4:6. Pride appears in three forms — worldliness toward God (vv. 7–10), judgment toward a brother (vv. 11–12), and self-sufficiency toward the future (vv. 13–16) — and each is answered by submission to God as Lawgiver, Judge, and Lord of life.

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: Repentance within the Family

The "sinners" and "double-minded" of v. 8 are the "brothers" of v. 11 and the "adulterers" of v. 4. The call is to return — draw near, cleanse, purify, mourn, humble yourselves — not to initial faith. The promises are relational: God will draw near, and He will exalt. This supports the Free Grace reading of James as a letter concerned with the fellowship and fruitfulness of believers rather than with the reality of their regeneration.

Theme 2: The One Lawgiver and Judge

Speaking against a brother is judging the law and usurping God's role (vv. 11–12). There is one Lawgiver, "able to save and to destroy." The σῴζω language links this verse to 1:21, 2:14, and 5:15, 20; the honesty note under v. 12 presents both the Free Grace and the majority readings of its scope. Both agree on the main point: judgment belongs to God.

Theme 3: "If the Lord Wills"

Human life is a vapor (v. 14), and all planning must acknowledge the Lord's sovereignty over life and action (v. 15). The formula expresses a posture of dependence, not a superstition. It applies humility to the ordinary sphere of business and time, extending the letter's critique of worldliness and wealth (1:10–11; 2:1–7; 5:1–6).

Theme 4: Sins of Omission

"To him who knows to do good and does not do it, it is sin" (v. 17). This principle unites the letter's teaching on hearing and doing (1:22–25), mercy (2:13–16), and faith and works (2:14–26). Failure to do good is not a mere missed opportunity; it is sin that believers must confess and forsake.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance; Reception

James 4:7–8 ("Resist the devil … draw near to God") has been one of the most frequently cited texts in Christian spiritual guidance; 4:6–10 parallels 1 Peter 5:5–9 closely, suggesting a shared early Christian tradition of exhortation. The phrase "if the Lord wills" (4:15) became a standard Christian formula; the abbreviation D.V. (Deo volente) was used for centuries in letters and announcements. James 4:17 has been foundational in moral theology's distinction between sins of commission and omission.

Canonically, the passage echoes the prophets' calls to return (Zech 1:3; Mal 3:7; Joel 2:12–13), the Psalms (24:3–4), Proverbs (27:1), and Jesus's teaching on mourning and humility (Matt 5:4; Luke 6:25; 14:11) and on the rich fool (Luke 12:16–21).

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 9 — μεταστραφήτω (Byz) vs. μετατραπήτω (NA28): synonyms. v. 10 — τοῦ κυρίου (Byz) vs. κυρίου (NA28): article only. v. 11 — καὶ κρίνων (Byz) vs. ἢ κρίνων (NA28). v. 12 — καὶ κριτής absent (Byz) vs. present (NA28); ὃς κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον (Byz) vs. ὁ κρίνων τὸν πλησίον (NA28). v. 13 — καί vs. ἤ, subjunctives vs. futures, ἕνα present/absent. v. 14 — γάρ and δέ present (Byz) vs. absent (NA28); ἔσται ("it will be"; Byzantine text, with ἐστιν, "it is," in the RP margin) vs. ἐστε ("you are") — the WEB follows the critical ἐστε. v. 15 — subjunctives (Byz) vs. futures (NA28).

Many of the variants in this section involve the interchange of -ο- and -ω- endings (future vs. subjunctive), which were pronounced alike in Koine. None affects doctrine. The reader is encouraged to verify individual sigla against the NA28 apparatus and the Robinson–Pierpont and Hodges–Farstad editions before citing them in teaching.
