Introduction to the Epistle of James

The Epistle of James presents one of the most vigorous debates in New Testament theology. Addressed to "the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion" (1:1), the letter is conventionally attributed to James the Lord's brother, who led the Jerusalem church until his martyrdom c. AD 62 (Josephus, Antiquities 20.9.1). This identification, favored by the majority of patristic sources and defended by modern scholars including Peter Davids (NIGTC), Douglas Moo (Pillar), and Ralph Martin (WBC), locates the letter within Jewish-Christian wisdom tradition and situates its hortatory imperatives as pastorally urgent rather than theologically systematic.

From a Free Grace perspective, the epistle is addressed throughout to already-regenerate believers. The imperatives of 1:2–12 are discipleship commands, not soteriological conditions. Zane Hodges (The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing, GES) argued decisively that James's concern is with the quality of Christian living under trial — specifically, whether believers will access God's wisdom, maintain undivided trust, and thereby earn eschatological rewards. The "στέφανος τῆς ζωῆς" (crown of life, 1:12) is a prize for perseverance, not a synonym for regenerating faith. This document traces those themes verse by verse through the Byzantine Majority Text.

James opens with the standard three-part Greek letter formula (sender → addressee → greeting), but his selection of terms is carefully weighted. He identifies himself as Ἰάκωβος θεοῦ καὶ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ δοῦλος — a "slave of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ." The double genitive, coordinated by καί, does not diminish either master but theologically equates them in authority: one cannot serve God in the full OT sense without serving Jesus as κύριος. Notably James does not call himself an apostle (contrast Paul's standard openings), nor does he explicitly identify himself as the Lord's brother. His chosen title δοῦλος echoes the OT pattern of Moses (Deut 34:5), Joshua (Josh 24:29), and the prophets as "servants of YHWH," consciously locating him within the prophetic-wisdom tradition that dominates the letter's idiom.

The addressees — ταῖς δώδεκα φυλαῖς ταῖς ἐν τῇ διασπορᾷ — have been variously interpreted: (a) as literal ethnic Jews dispersed across the Roman world, (b) as Jewish Christians specifically, or (c) as a typological address to the whole church as eschatological Israel. Davids (NIGTC) and Martin (WBC) favor Jewish Christians; McKnight (NICNT) leans toward the church as reconstituted Israel. From a Free Grace perspective, what is decisive is that the letter is addressed to people already in covenant relationship with God through Christ — they are in the διασπορά as the community of faith, not as unbelievers being summoned to faith. This anchors the imperatives of chapters 1–5 as commands to believers regarding conduct, not conditions of initial salvation. The epistolary greeting χαίρειν (Greetings!) anticipates the thematic development of χαρά (joy) in the very next verse, creating a deliberate lexical echo.

The double accusative construction πᾶσαν χαρὰν ἡγήσασθε — "count as all joy" — is striking. The verb ἡγέομαι does not denote an emotional feeling but a cognitive and volitional reckoning, a deliberate appraisal (cf. Phil 3:7–8, where Paul uses the same verb for his reevaluation of all prior gains as loss). James does not say "feel joyful" but "reckon it as complete joy." The aorist imperative intensifies the urgency: this is not a general disposition to cultivate gradually but an immediate, decisive act of evaluation when trials arrive. The adjective πᾶσαν — "all" — rules out any partial or grudging acceptance; James demands undivided, complete joy in the face of πειρασμοῖς ποικίλοις.

The word πειρασμός is central to this entire opening section (1:2, 12, 13, 14). Here in v. 2 and v. 12, the term carries the sense of external trials — adverse circumstances that test character — in contrast to its use in 1:13–14 where it shades toward inner temptation arising from desire. The distinction is crucial for Free Grace exegesis: the πειρασμοί of 1:2 are not moral failures but providential pressures that, when met with wisdom and endurance (vv. 3–5), produce mature Christian character. Hodges rightly notes that the addressees are already believers — ἀδελφοί μου — and James's command is not "believe so that you may be saved" but "choose to evaluate your hardships through the lens of what God is accomplishing." This pastoral tone frames the entire pericope as discipleship formation, not soteriological instruction.

The participial clause γινώσκοντες ὅτι functions causally: the believer's capacity to count trials as complete joy (v. 2) is grounded in prior theological knowledge. The substance of that knowledge is the causal chain: τὸ δοκίμιον τῆς πίστεως κατεργάζεται ὑπομονήν. The noun δοκίμιον is particularly evocative — borrowed from the metallurgical process of assaying precious metals to prove their purity (LXX Ps 11:7 [12:6]; Prov 27:21). Trials do not destroy faith; they reveal and refine its quality. The verb κατεργάζεται (present middle indicative) expresses a continuous, reliable, almost mechanical result: the testing always and thoroughly produces endurance when properly undergone.

The key term ὑπομονή — endurance, steadfast remaining — becomes James's watchword for mature Christian response to suffering. Moo (Pillar) notes that the term does not connote mere passive resignation but active, courageous perseverance under pressure (cf. LXX usage in 4 Macc 1:11; 9:8). From a Free Grace perspective, this endurance is the product of tested faith, not its prerequisite — the chain runs from external trial → testing of faith → endurance → maturity (v. 4), all presupposing that the believer already possesses regenerate faith and is growing in its exercise. Nothing here implies that endurance is a condition for final salvation; it is rather the measure of sanctified Christian character that positions the believer for the eschatological reward of 1:12.

Verse 4 pushes the logic of vv. 2–3 to its telic conclusion. The imperative ἐχέτω — a third-person imperative, unusual and forceful — commands that endurance be permitted to complete its work without interruption or truncation. The key theological term here is τέλειος, which appears twice: once as an adjective modifying ἔργον ("a perfect/complete work") and once as a predicate adjective describing the state believers may achieve ("that you may be perfect"). The word τέλειος derives from τέλος — the goal or end — and carries the sense of having reached full development, of being undivided and mature. This is not moral sinlessness (Moo, Pillar: "moral completeness or maturity") but rather that integration of character which comes only through trial successfully endured.

The pairing of τέλειοι and ὁλόκληροι is a rhetorical doublet of near-synonyms. Where τέλειος accents the attainment of a goal, ὁλόκληρος (lit. "whole-lot") accents wholeness of parts — nothing missing, no capacity underdeveloped. The phrase ἐν μηδενὶ λειπόμενοι ("lacking in nothing") completes the triad negatively, recalling the very verb λείπω that will appear in v. 5 ("if any of you lacks wisdom"). The structural echo is deliberate: the person who lets endurance have its complete work will not be among those who lack wisdom, because the process of trial-tested endurance is itself the pathway to the comprehensive maturity James envisions. Free Grace readers should note that this full-development language targets the sanctified life of the already-regenerate, not an entrance condition. The concern is always with what kind of believers James's audience will be.

Verse 5 bridges the abstract theology of vv. 2–4 with concrete pastoral instruction. The first-class conditional εἰ … λείπεται σοφίας treats the deficiency as a genuine reality James's readers may face — the wisdom needed to interpret and navigate trials. This σοφία is not merely intellectual knowledge but the practical, Spirit-mediated discernment to see trials through God's purposes, to make the reckoning of v. 2 actual. God is described with two participial modifiers that together construct a portrait of extravagant, shame-free generosity: he is the one διδόντος … ἁπλῶς ("giving generously") and οὐκ ὀνειδίζοντος ("not reproaching"). The adverb ἁπλῶς — from ἁπλοῦς (single, undivided, unfolded) — connotes a giving that holds nothing back, with no hidden conditions or reluctance. The negated participle οὐκ ὀνειδίζοντος removes the social shame a benefactor might attach to a gift, countering any fear that asking for wisdom is an admission of weakness that will be held against the petitioner.

The promise δοθήσεται αὐτῷ is a future passive — the divine passive — and carries the force of divine guarantee. McKnight (NICNT) notes the echo of Jesus's teaching: "Ask and it will be given to you" (Matt 7:7–11). Hodges observes that this promise is specifically designed to equip believers for the trial-navigation process of vv. 2–4: wisdom here is not generic intelligence but the particular grace-enabling resource for endurance. The simplicity of the solution — ask God — underscores that James is addressing believers who have unhindered access to the Father, not people needing to establish that relationship for the first time. This passage is therefore about the quality and confidence of the believer's prayer life, not about an initial faith response.

The qualification of the asking imperative — ἐν πίστει μηδὲν διακρινόμενος — introduces James's pivotal contrast between the person of undivided trust and the διακρινόμενος. This internal division, not external wavering on doctrinal matters, is the target of James's warning. The doubter does not lack faith altogether; he is a believer whose confidence is diluted by competing loyalties — precisely the dynamic James will later describe as δίψυχος (double-minded, v. 8).

The comparison to a κλύδωνι θαλάσσης ἀνεμιζομένῳ καὶ ῥιπιζομένῳ — a wind-driven, tossed wave — is a vivid metaphor for external determination. The wave has no direction of its own; it is entirely at the mercy of forces acting upon it. Similarly, the doubting believer in prayer is not self-directing but is buffeted between competing orientations: toward God and toward other sources of confidence. This connects the present passage thematically to James's overall concern with the wholeness and integration of the Christian life. Davids (NIGTC) notes the parallel with OT texts on prayer and confidence (Ps 86:11; Isa 57:20), and with the Synoptic tradition on faith-prayer (Matt 21:21–22; Mark 11:23–24). Hodges emphasizes that the call for undivided faith is addressed to believers specifically — those who already possess faith are being called to actualize it without reservation in petitionary prayer.

Verse 7 delivers the sobering consequence of divided-hearted prayer: the doubter — ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἐκεῖνος, "that man" — should not entertain the expectation that he will receive τι (anything) from the Lord. From a Free Grace perspective, this is entirely consistent with the broader NT teaching on prayer and faith (cf. Matt 21:21–22; Mark 11:23–24; John 14:13–14). The passage does not say the doubter loses salvation but that his doubting cuts off the specific benefit — wisdom — that he was seeking. Hodges and Wilkin emphasize that James here describes a functional spiritual problem among believers: they possess faith in Christ for eternal life, but they fail to exercise that same trust in God's provision within the trial. The wisdom God so generously offers (v. 5) is withheld not because God is reluctant but because the doubter has not truly asked with the reliance on God's promise that the asking requires.

This expansive scope suggests James is making a point not merely about the mechanics of petition but about the character-formation that runs through the entire letter: divided loyalty at the level of prayer and trust reflects and produces a life of divided loyalty in conduct, speech, and ethical decision-making. Hodges notes that the repetition of δίψυχος in 4:8 — where James calls the audience to "purify your hearts, you double-minded" — confirms that James is addressing regenerate believers caught in spiritual ambivalence, not unregenerate persons.

With δέ, James pivots from warning (vv. 6–8) to positive exhortation — but the topic shifts from prayer to social reversal. The ἀδελφὸς ὁ ταπεινός is the economically humble, socially low-status member of the community (the word ταπεινός carries social freight in Hellenistic Greek: it describes those without honor, without standing, the poor). James commands this person to καυχάσθω — to boast, to glory — in his ὕψος (high position, exaltation). The contrast is striking: the world's assessment of this person is lowliness; the believer's true assessment, from the standpoint of the gospel, is exaltation. James has in view the theological reversal Paul articulates in 1 Cor 1:26–29 and which Jesus proclaims in the Beatitudes (Matt 5:3) and the Magnificat (Luke 1:52).

The ὕψος of the lowly believer is not yet visible in the present age — it is an eschatological dignity. From a Free Grace perspective, this reflects the believer's standing in Christ: already seated in heavenly places (Eph 2:6), already joint-heirs (Rom 8:17), already possessing eternal life (John 5:24). The exhortation to boast in this high position is a call to order one's self-understanding by theological reality rather than social position. This theme of the inversion of worldly values runs throughout James (2:1–7; 4:6; 5:1–6) and is grounded in the gospel's claim that the believer's worth is determined not by wealth or social honor but by union with Christ. Note that James says the brother — a regenerate believer — should boast in eschatological exaltation, not that he must earn it.

His ταπείνωσις is therefore not a humiliation to be ashamed of but the sober recognition that sets him free from the false identity wealth constructs. This passage anticipates the sustained critique of wealth in James 5:1–6 and the earlier OT prophetic tradition on riches' deceptive security.

Verse 11 expands the simile of v. 10 into a vivid tricolon of gnomic aorists, each describing a stage in the rapid destruction of the desert flower under the combined assault of sun and sirocco: the grass withers, the flower drops, the beauty perishes. The gnomic aorist is characteristic of timeless, universally-applicable truths in Greek — James is not narrating a single event but encoding a standing law of nature. James applies this eschatological contrast specifically to the rich man's πορεῖαι — his pursuits, his life-projects, his business journeys — the very sphere in which he has invested his identity.

The closing verb μαρανθήσεται — from μαραίνω, "to wither/extinguish" — is future passive and carries a note of certainty: the fading of the rich man's wealth-based identity is not a possibility but an inevitability. From a Free Grace standpoint, this is not a judgment saying about eternal destiny but a wisdom observation about the futility of locating one's boast in economic status. The passage does not say the rich man will perish eternally; it says his wealth and the identity it constructs will inevitably fade. The proper response — καυχάσθω ἐν τῇ ταπεινώσει αὐτοῦ (v. 10) — is therefore the wisdom that aligns with reality: the wealthy believer who has absorbed this truth is freed to identify with his standing before God rather than with his possessions.

James 1:12 — The Crown of Life

Textual Note (Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5): James 1:12 contains one textual variant of moderate significance, concerning the subject of ἐπηγγείλατο. (a) Byzantine: ὃν ἐπηγγείλατο ὁ κύριος τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν αὐτόν — "which the Lord promised to those who love him." (b) NA28/UBS5: ὃν ἐπηγγείλατο τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν αὐτόν — with no expressed subject, "which he promised…," God being understood from the context. A third group of witnesses reads ὁ θεός, "which God promised." (c) Witnesses: the subjectless reading is carried by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; ὁ κύριος by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐); ὁ θεός by a smaller group, including some early versional and patristic support. (d) Significance: critical editors regard both ὁ κύριος and ὁ θεός as scribal clarifications of an originally unstated subject. The Byzantine reading identifies the promiser as the Lord, which in NT usage most often means Jesus, and so strengthens the parallel with Revelation 2:10, where Christ promises the crown of life to the faithful at Smyrna. On any reading the promise is divine.

James 1:12 is the theological crown of the opening pericope, resolving the chain of vv. 2–11 with an eschatological promise. The beatitude formula Μακάριος ἀνήρ echoes both the OT Psalms (Ps 1:1; 32:1–2) and the Beatitudes of Jesus (Matt 5:3–12), anchoring this verse in the wisdom-sapiential tradition. The object of blessedness is the person who ὑπομένει πειρασμόν — who endures trial, a present participle denoting the ongoing, habitual quality of the endurance described in vv. 3–4. The chain is complete: trial → testing of faith → endurance → maturity (v. 4) → the approval state → the crown.

The most theologically significant term in this verse — and perhaps in the entire letter from a Free Grace perspective — is τὸν στέφανον τῆς ζωῆς. The noun στέφανος is the athletic victor's wreath, awarded not for participation but for winning. In the NT, στέφανος-language is consistently reward language: the crown of righteousness (2 Tim 4:8), the crown of glory (1 Pet 5:4), the crown of life (Rev 2:10). Hodges and Wilkin (Grace in Focus) insist that this crown is an eschatological prize for faithful endurance, distinct from and additional to the gift of eternal life received through faith alone. The genitive τῆς ζωῆς ("of life") describes the content or character of the crown — it is a crown characterized by eschatological ζωή, the fullness of life in God's kingdom — not a synonym for the bare possession of eternal life that all believers already have (John 5:24). To read the "crown of life" as merely equivalent to salvation received by faith collapses the reward-gift distinction that James, Paul (1 Cor 3:12–15), and the Revelation letters all presuppose.

The recipient class is defined as τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν αὐτόν — "those who love him." The present participle ἀγαπῶσιν denotes an ongoing, characterizing love — the covenant loyalty that finds expression in the very endurance the verse describes. Those who love Christ are those who, when trials come, exercise the undivided faith of vv. 6–8 rather than retreating into double-mindedness. The divine promise (ἐπηγγείλατο — already made, standing) is the guarantee that grounds both the command to endure and the confidence that the prize awaits. The parallel in Revelation 2:10 — "Be faithful unto death, and I will give you the crown of life" — confirms that this is reward language addressed to believers, not invitation language to unbelievers. The crown is a prize to be won by faithful believers, not a gift to be received by initial believing.

Overall Exegesis: James 1:1–12

This concluding section surveys the rhetorical architecture of the passage, its key theological themes, its canonical and historical significance, and the major textual considerations.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

James 1:1–12 forms the letter's opening unit, functioning simultaneously as epistolary salutation, theological introduction, and pastoral exhortation. The structure moves through three interlocking movements: (A) the prologue-salutation (v. 1), which establishes James's identity as a servant of God and Christ and the audience as dispersed covenant people; (B) the trials-wisdom-endurance sequence (vv. 2–8), which constructs the theological rationale for joyful endurance through a carefully chained argument (ἡγήσασθε → γινώσκοντες → ἔχέτω → εἰ δέ → αἰτείτω); and (C) the social reversal and crown-promise conclusion (vv. 9–12), which reframes the trials-endurance argument in eschatological and social terms, culminating in the beatitude of v. 12.

The rhetorical binding of the pericope is achieved through several devices. The lexical echo of χαίρειν (v. 1) → χαρὰν (v. 2) anchors the opening command to joy in the greeting itself. The root λείπ- appears in λειπόμενοι (v. 4) and λείπεται (v. 5), connecting the goal of lacking-nothing-maturity to the specific need for wisdom. The δόκιμος / δοκίμιον word-group (vv. 3, 12) frames the entire middle section: the testing-process of v. 3 finds its outcome in the approved-state of v. 12. The πειρασμός of v. 2 becomes the πειρασμόν of v. 12, creating an inclusio that holds the pericope together. Together these devices reveal a carefully composed unit, not a loose collection of wisdom sayings.

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: The Teleology of Trials

James's most distinctive contribution to NT theology of suffering is the explicit teleology he assigns to πειρασμοί. Unlike passages that emphasize endurance as a response to suffering without explaining its mechanism (e.g., Rom 8:18), James traces a specific causal chain: trial → testing of faith → endurance → maturity → lacking nothing (vv. 2–4). The chain presupposes that trials are providentially purposeful: they are not random misfortune but divinely appointed occasions for the exercise and development of faith. The concept of δοκίμιον ("testing-process," v. 3) drawn from metallurgical refining language (LXX Ps 11:7) means that trials do not introduce impurity but reveal and refine whatever faith is already present. This is consistent with the Free Grace conviction that the tested person is already a believer — the trial does not determine whether they have faith but what faith, when exercised, produces.

Theme 2: Wisdom as the Grace-Resource for Trials

The pivot to wisdom in v. 5 is structurally essential: the reader may wonder how anyone is to achieve the undivided joy-in-trials of v. 2 or the completeness of v. 4. The answer is σοφία — not human cleverness but the divinely-given practical wisdom that enables believers to see trials through God's teleological lens. James locates this wisdom in direct petition to God (αἰτείτω, v. 5), connecting it to the broader NT teaching on prayer-and-faith (Matt 7:7–11; John 14:13–14; 15:7). The characterization of God as the one who gives ἁπλῶς, καὶ οὐκ ὀνειδίζοντος — generously and without reproach — removes every obstacle from the believer's asking. Wisdom is not a reward for prior holiness but an available grace-gift for those who ask with undivided confidence. This democratizes the resource: any believer, regardless of social standing or prior achievement, can access the wisdom needed to navigate trials faithfully.

Theme 3: Undivided Faith vs. Double-Mindedness

The contrast between the undivided faith-asker and the δίψυχος ἀνήρ (vv. 6–8) establishes one of James's central anthropological categories. Double-mindedness (δίψυχος) is the structural opposite of the τέλειος (complete/mature) person of v. 4. Where the τέλειος person has achieved an integration of soul and will under God's purpose, the δίψυχος person is internally fractured, attempting to maintain simultaneous allegiance to God and to other sources of security. This internal division has practical consequences: it makes prayer ineffective (v. 7) and renders the entire life unstable (v. 8 — ἀκατάστατος ἐν πάσαις ταῖς ὁδοῖς αὐτοῦ). James returns to this category in 4:8, connecting it to worldliness and the friendship-with-the-world-as-enmity-with-God theme. The δίψυχος person is not an unbeliever but a believer who has not allowed the endurance-process to achieve its complete work.

Theme 4: Eschatological Reversal — The Poor and the Rich

The social-reversal theology of vv. 9–11 anticipates James's sustained engagement with economic injustice (2:1–7; 5:1–6). James's point is not merely ethical but theological: the proper basis for self-understanding is not social or economic position but one's standing before God. The ταπεινός brother already possesses ὕψος — eschatological exaltation — by virtue of his union with Christ. The wealthy person, by contrast, is called to glory in ταπείνωσις — the recognition that his distinguishing possession (wealth) is as transient as the grass-flower under the sirocco. The imagery from Isaiah 40 connects this passage to the prophetic tradition of wealth-critique: riches are not morally neutral but epistemologically dangerous, capable of constructing a false identity that competes with the believer's true identity in Christ.

Theme 5: The Crown of Life as Eschatological Reward

James 1:12 is the eschatological horizon of the entire pericope. The στέφανος τῆς ζωῆς — crown of life — functions in James as eternal life functions in Paul's reward passages (1 Cor 9:24–27; 2 Tim 4:7–8): it is the prize awarded to the faithful competitor who has completed the course. The parallel in Revelation 2:10 — "Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life" — addressed to the persecuted church at Smyrna, confirms that this is reward language for enduring believers. The key interpretive question is whether ζωῆς (life) in the genitive is (a) a genitive of apposition (the crown = life, i.e., eternal life the gift of salvation) or (b) a genitive of quality or content (a crown characterized by eschatological ζωή, i.e., the fullness of kingdom life as a reward). Free Grace exegetes (Hodges, Wilkin) strongly favor the second reading, on the grounds that: (1) the στέφανος-terminology throughout the NT is reward terminology, not regeneration terminology; (2) the recipients are already defined as those who love the Lord — already-believing persons; (3) to read the crown as merely synonymous with eternal life received by faith alone collapses the reward-structure that undergirds the entire letter's exhortation to faithful endurance. If endurance merely guarantees salvation, and all believers are guaranteed salvation equally regardless of endurance, then the motivational force of the crown-promise is evacuated. The Free Grace reading preserves that force: faithful endurance, processed through wisdom and undivided faith-prayer, produces the δόκιμος character that inherits an eschatological crown beyond the basic gift of eternal life.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

The Epistle of James has had a complex reception history, most famously captured in Luther's dismissal of it as an "epistle of straw" due to its apparent tension with Pauline justification-by-faith-alone. Luther's concern was that James 2:14–26 appeared to teach justification by works. However, the patristic reception was more positive: Origen, Eusebius (who noted its disputed canonical status), and Chrysostom treated it as fully authoritative. The letter was eventually received into the NT canon across both Western and Eastern traditions, though it remained one of the "antilegomena" (disputed books) in some quarters. Modern scholarship has broadly rehabilitated James: Davids, Moo, Johnson, McKnight, and Martin all treat it as a coherent theological document with deep roots in Jewish wisdom tradition, the Jesus tradition (particularly Q-sayings), and early Christian paraenesis.

From a canonical standpoint, James 1:1–12 sets themes that resonate throughout the NT wisdom corpus: the teleology of suffering (cf. Rom 5:3–5; 1 Pet 1:6–7), the prayer-faith connection (Matt 7:7–11), the danger of wealth (Luke 12:16–21; 1 Tim 6:17–19), and the eschatological horizon of endurance (Rev 2:10; 3:11). James is not an isolated letter but a wisdom-exhortation that both draws from and contributes to the NT's coherent account of the sanctified Christian life.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

For James 1:1–12, the Byzantine Majority Text and NA28 are in substantial agreement throughout. The most significant variant is in v. 12, discussed in the note above: ὁ κύριος (Byzantine) vs. no expressed subject (NA28), with ὁ θεός in a smaller group of witnesses. In v. 5 the Byzantine text reads οὐκ ὀνειδίζοντος where NA28 has μὴ ὀνειδίζοντος; the sense is unchanged. One minor variation: in v. 8, some manuscripts insert a connective or adjust the case of the appositive to make the relationship to v. 7 syntactically smoother; the Byzantine text's verbless construction is the more difficult reading and is to be preferred. In v. 11, the order of the descriptive elements is stable across traditions; the Syriac Peshitta renders καύσωνι as the eastern desert wind (the khamsin), confirming early reading of the image as a sirocco. There are no variants in this passage that carry significant theological weight beyond the subject of ἐπηγγείλατο in v. 12.
