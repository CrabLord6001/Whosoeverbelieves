Introduction to James 3:13–4:6

This section traces the problem of the tongue (3:1–12) to its root in the heart. James contrasts two kinds of wisdom (3:13–18): one "earthly, unspiritual, demonic," marked by bitter jealousy and selfish ambition, which produces disorder; the other "from above," pure, peaceable, and full of mercy, which produces a harvest of righteousness. He then diagnoses the quarrels in the community (4:1–3) as the fruit of warring desires and prayerless or self-serving prayer, and names the underlying condition in the strongest covenant language: "adulterers and adulteresses" whose friendship with the world is enmity with God (4:4). The section closes with one of the most difficult verses in the letter (4:5), where the Byzantine and critical texts differ, and with the great assurance that God "gives more grace" to the humble (4:6).

From a Free Grace perspective, three points are central. First, the wisdom from above is the answer to 3:8 ("no human can tame the tongue") and the fulfilment of 1:5 ("if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God"). Second, the "adulterers" of 4:4 are the brothers of 4:11 — believers in covenant relationship with God who are being unfaithful to Him, as Israel was called an adulteress by the prophets while remaining His people. "Enemy of God" describes their functional stance, not the loss of eternal life. Third, 4:6 is the letter's clearest statement that the whole Christian life of wisdom, speech, and humility rests on grace.

The question Τίς σοφὸς καὶ ἐπιστήμων ἐν ὑμῖν; returns to the teachers of 3:1: the "wise and understanding" were those who aspired to instruct others (cf. Deut 1:13, where Moses seeks "wise and understanding" men as leaders). James's answer is that wisdom is not proven by speech but by life: Δειξάτω ἐκ τῆς καλῆς ἀναστροφῆς τὰ ἔργα αὐτοῦ. The verb δείκνυμι is the same one used in 2:18 ("show me your faith … I will show you"), and in both places the showing is directed toward other people. The wise man's works are visible in his conduct.

The manner of this demonstration is ἐν πρᾳΰτητι σοφίας — "in the gentleness that belongs to wisdom." True wisdom is not argumentative or domineering but meek, the same disposition with which believers are to receive the implanted word (1:21). This verse supports the Free Grace reading of 2:18–26: works "show" or vindicate a believer's wisdom and faith before others. The issue is not proving to God that one is saved, but displaying before people the quality of one's walk.

The negative case: ζῆλον πικρόν ("bitter jealousy") and ἐριθείαν ("selfish ambition") in the heart. The word ζῆλος can describe good zeal, but the adjective πικρός — the same word as the "bitter" water of 3:11 — marks it here as envious rivalry. ἐριθεία originally meant working for hire and came to mean self-seeking partisanship (Paul lists it among the works of the flesh, Gal 5:20). This is where the bitter water of the tongue comes from.

Those who harbor these things must not κατακαυχᾶσθε καὶ ψεύδεσθε κατὰ τῆς ἀληθείας — boast and lie against the truth. Claiming to be wise while being driven by envy is a lie against the very truth (1:18) that gave the believer new birth. The first-class condition assumes the problem is real among the readers. Once again James addresses believers whose conduct contradicts their identity.

The "wisdom" that produces jealousy and ambition is not ἄνωθεν κατερχομένη — "coming down from above." The phrase deliberately echoes 1:17, where every perfect gift is ἄνωθεν … καταβαῖνον. True wisdom, like every good gift, descends from the Father of lights (1:5, 17). This counterfeit wisdom is instead characterized by a descending triad: ἐπίγειος (earthly — its horizon is this world), ψυχική (natural, unspiritual — operating without the Spirit, cf. 1 Cor 2:14), and δαιμονιώδης (demonic — ultimately inspired by evil powers).

The triad parallels the traditional trio of world, flesh, and devil, and it fits the letter's pattern: the tongue is set on fire by Gehenna (3:6), the demons believe and shudder (2:19), and the believer must resist the devil (4:7). A believer can operate by this kind of wisdom, just as he can speak with a tongue set on fire from Gehenna. James's point is not that such a believer is unregenerate, but that in that moment the source of his thinking is not God.

The fruit of earthly wisdom is ἀκαταστασία, disorder or instability. The word is a cognate of ἀκατάστατος in 1:8, where the double-minded man is "unstable in all his ways." What is true of the divided individual becomes true of the divided community: where jealousy and ambition rule, there is disorder and πᾶν φαῦλον πρᾶγμα, every kind of worthless practice. Paul uses the same word for the disorder that God opposes in the assembly: "God is not a God of confusion (ἀκαταστασίας) but of peace" (1 Cor 14:33) — the very contrast James draws in vv. 17–18.

James now describes ἡ ἄνωθεν σοφία, the wisdom from above — the wisdom the believer may ask God for "without reproach" (1:5). It is πρῶτον μὲν ἁγνή, first and above all pure: the source is holy, in contrast to the "earthly, unspiritual, demonic" wisdom of v. 15. Then come seven qualities, most of them relational: peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy and good fruits, impartial, and sincere. The portrait resembles Paul's fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22–23) and his description of love (1 Cor 13:4–7).

Several terms reach back through the letter. The wisdom from above is μεστὴ ἐλέους, "full of mercy," the exact reverse of the tongue μεστὴ ἰοῦ, "full of poison" (3:8), and the embodiment of the mercy that triumphs over judgment (2:13). It is ἀδιάκριτος, a negated form of διακρίνω: the wisdom from above is neither "divided" like the doubter (1:6) nor "making distinctions" like the partial judges (2:4). This is the answer to 3:8. No human can tame the tongue, but the believer who asks for and receives wisdom from above is given a disposition that produces peace instead of poison. Sanctification is a gift received, not an achievement generated.

The section closes with an agricultural proverb. Καρπὸς … τῆς δικαιοσύνης — the harvest of righteousness — ἐν εἰρήνῃ σπείρεται, is sown in peace, by (or for) τοῖς ποιοῦσιν εἰρήνην, those who make peace. The mixed metaphor (fruit being "sown") compresses the whole process: peace is the seed, the manner, and the harvest. Echoing Jesus's beatitude on the peacemakers (Matt 5:9), James contrasts the harvest of peace with the "disorder" of v. 16 and the "wars" of 4:1.

"Righteousness" here, as in 1:20, is ethical — the righteous conduct God approves. Anger does not produce it (1:20); peace does. This is not the imputed righteousness of justification, which the believer received by faith (2:23, quoting Gen 15:6), but the practical righteousness that grows in the believer who lives by the wisdom from above.

The chapter break obscures the continuity: having described the wisdom that produces peace (3:17–18), James asks where the πόλεμοι καὶ μάχαι — wars and fights — ἐν ὑμῖν come from. This takes up 1:14–15 (desire conceives and gives birth to sin) and 3:14 (bitter jealousy in the heart). James again assumes that regenerate people have such desires; his concern is that they not be ruled by them.

James lays out three cycles of frustrated desire: you desire and do not have; you murder and envy and cannot obtain; you fight and war but do not have. The escalation is deliberate. Desire unmet turns to envy, envy to violence, and violence to open conflict — the genealogy of 1:15 played out in a community. The verb φονεύετε ("you murder") has troubled readers so much that Erasmus proposed the conjecture φθονεῖτε ("you envy"), but no manuscript supports it. James may be using hyperbole in the manner of Jesus (Matt 5:21–22; cf. 1 John 3:15), or he may be alluding to actual violence in a period of Zealot agitation. Either way, the language shows how seriously he regards the community's hostility.

The diagnosis: οὐκ ἔχετε διὰ τὸ μὴ αἰτεῖσθαι ὑμᾶς — "you do not have, because you do not ask." The believers seek by grasping what they could receive by asking. This returns to 1:5: God gives generously to all who ask, without reproach. The same God who gives wisdom (1:5; 3:17) is the answer to the frustrated desires that lead to strife — if the desires are ones He can grant (v. 3).

The address Μοιχοὶ καὶ μοιχαλίδες is the sharpest in the letter. It draws on the prophets' portrayal of Israel as God's wife who has been unfaithful (Hos 1–3; Jer 3:6–10; Ezek 16). The point of the metaphor is critical for interpretation: an adulteress is, by definition, a married woman. The prophets did not say Israel had never been God's wife; they said she was an unfaithful one. James addresses people in covenant relationship with God — the same people he calls "brothers" in 4:11 — who are flirting with a rival lover, the world.

The principle: ἡ φιλία τοῦ κόσμου ἔχθρα τοῦ θεοῦ ἐστίν. "Friendship with the world" means adopting its values and ambitions — the jealousy, selfish ambition, and pleasure-seeking of 3:14–4:3, and the favoritism of 2:1–4. The conclusion follows: whoever βουληθῇ — deliberately chooses — to be the world's friend ἐχθρὸς τοῦ θεοῦ καθίσταται, "sets himself up as God's enemy." The contrast with Abraham is pointed. By works of faith Abraham was called φίλος θεοῦ (2:23); by friendship with the world, a believer takes the stance of ἐχθρὸς τοῦ θεοῦ.

The Free Grace reader should observe that friendship and enmity here are relational stances, not legal standings. Abraham's friendship with God was the intimacy of an obedient believer, not his justification (which came by faith, Gen 15:6). In the same way, the believer who befriends the world forfeits that intimacy and positions himself against God's purposes. He does not thereby cease to be God's child, any more than Israel ceased to be God's wife when she committed adultery (Hos 2:14–23; 3:1). The remedy James offers is not "get saved" but "submit … draw near … humble yourselves" (4:7–10).

Honesty Note: Some commentators suggest that "enemy of God" means the person is unregenerate — that genuine believers cannot adopt such a stance, or that the passage addresses false professors mixed into the congregation. Others (e.g., Moo, McKnight) read it as a severe warning to professing believers that persistent worldliness puts one's eternal standing in jeopardy. The Free Grace response is (1) the prophetic adultery metaphor presupposes a real covenant relationship; (2) James addresses the same people as "brothers" throughout (4:11; 5:7, 9, 12, 19); and (3) the remedy of vv. 7–10 is repentance and return, not initial faith. The student should weigh whether James's enmity language describes a relational breach within God's family or a sign of exclusion from it.

With the Byzantine κατῴκησεν, "the Spirit" is the subject of the relative clause ("the Spirit who dwells in us") and naturally also the subject of "yearns": "the Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously." This is widely regarded as the most difficult verse in James. There are at least three problems: (1) no OT passage says these words exactly, so "the Scripture says" must refer to the general sense of Scripture (e.g., Exod 20:5; 34:14, "the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God"; Zech 8:2) or to an unknown text; (2) the subject of ἐπιποθεῖ is ambiguous — God, the Holy Spirit, or the human spirit; and (3) φθόνος normally denotes the vice of envy, which makes it strange to apply to God.

The Byzantine reading κατῴκησεν ("who dwelt") simplifies the syntax: the relative pronoun is the subject of "dwelt," and the most natural subject of "yearns" is the Spirit Himself. The sense is then: "The Spirit who has taken up residence in us yearns jealously" — that is, the indwelling Holy Spirit desires the believer's undivided loyalty and is grieved (cf. Eph 4:30) by the adultery of friendship with the world. This reading fits the context of 4:4 exactly: the believer's divine Lover is jealous for him. It also agrees with the OT theme of God's jealousy for His covenant people, which is not petty envy but the passionate claim of a spouse. The main alternative (with the critical κατῴκισεν) — "God jealously longs for the spirit He made to dwell in us" — reaches much the same theological point by a different route. A third option, that the human spirit "yearns enviously" (i.e., is prone to envy), fits 4:1–2 but makes "Scripture says in vain" awkward and weakens the transition to v. 6.

For Free Grace theology, the phrase ὃ κατῴκησεν ἐν ἡμῖν is significant. James affirms (with "us," including himself) that the Spirit dwells in the readers. The "adulterers and adulteresses" of v. 4 are Spirit-indwelt believers. Their worldliness grieves the Spirit within them; it does not evict Him. The warning is intensely serious precisely because the relationship is real.

After the severity of vv. 4–5 comes the turning point: Μείζονα δὲ δίδωσιν χάριν — "but he gives greater grace." The Spirit's jealous claim on the believer (v. 5) might seem to leave the double-minded believer hopeless. James answers that God's grace is greater — greater than the pull of the world, greater than the warring desires of v. 1, greater than the tongue no human can tame (3:8). Grace here is not only God's favor but His enabling help, given continually (δίδωσιν, present tense).

The quotation from Proverbs 3:34 (also cited in 1 Pet 5:5) states the condition of access: Ὁ θεὸς ὑπερηφάνοις ἀντιτάσσεται, ταπεινοῖς δὲ δίδωσιν χάριν. God "arrays himself against" the proud — a military verb that answers the language of war in vv. 1–2. But He gives grace to the ταπεινοί, the humble. This recalls the lowly brother of 1:9 who boasts in his exaltation. Humility here is not the condition of receiving eternal life (which is by faith alone), but the posture in which the believer receives the ongoing grace needed to live the Christian life.

This verse is the hinge between diagnosis and remedy. Verses 7–10 (the next document in the series) will spell out what humility looks like — submit to God, resist the devil, draw near, cleanse the hands, purify the double-minded heart — and conclude, "Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you" (4:10). For a letter often read as law, this is its gospel center for the believer's daily life: the answer to failure is greater grace.

Overall Exegesis: James 3:13–4:6

This section surveys the architecture of the passage, its principal theological themes, its reception, and the textual situation.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The passage moves from diagnosis to remedy in four steps. (A) Two wisdoms (3:13–18): the question "Who is wise?" is answered by conduct; false wisdom (jealousy, ambition — earthly, unspiritual, demonic) produces disorder; true wisdom (from above — pure, peaceable, merciful, impartial) produces a harvest of righteousness in peace. (B) The source of quarrels (4:1–3): external wars come from internal pleasures; desire leads to envy and conflict; believers lack because they do not ask, or ask for selfish ends. (C) The covenant indictment (4:4–5): adulterers and adulteresses — friendship with the world is enmity with God; the indwelling Spirit yearns jealously. (D) The remedy announced (4:6): God gives greater grace — resisting the proud, giving grace to the humble.

The section is bound together by contrasts of peace and war (εἰρήνη, 3:17–18; πόλεμοι, μάχαι, 4:1–2), of above and earth (ἄνωθεν, ἐπίγειος, 3:15, 17), and of friendship and enmity (4:4). It links backward to the tongue (μεστή, 3:8 vs. 3:17; πικρός, 3:11 vs. 3:14), to the double-minded man (ἀκαταστασία, 3:16; ἀδιάκριτος, 3:17), and to prayer for wisdom (1:5; 4:2–3); and forward to the call to humility (4:7–10).

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: Wisdom from Above as the Answer to the Untamable Tongue

"No human can tame the tongue" (3:8) is answered by the wisdom that "comes down from above" (3:15, 17) — the gift God gives generously to those who ask (1:5, 17). Its qualities (pure, peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy, impartial, sincere) are the reverse of the tongue's poison and of the partiality condemned in chapter 2. The Christian life, in James, is enabled by a gift received, not powered by self-effort.

Theme 2: Internal War and Prayer

Conflict in the community arises from desires waging war in the believer's members (4:1; cf. 1:14–15). Believers lack because they do not ask, or because they ask to satisfy their pleasures (4:2–3). James thereby unites his teaching on prayer: the doubter receives nothing (1:6–7), the selfish asker receives nothing (4:3), and the believer who asks in faith and humility receives wisdom and grace (1:5; 4:6).

Theme 3: Spiritual Adultery within the Covenant

The "adulterers and adulteresses" (4:4) are covenant partners who are unfaithful, as Israel was in the prophets. Friendship with the world makes a believer God's enemy in stance and conduct — the opposite of Abraham, "the friend of God" (2:23). In the Free Grace reading, this is a breach of intimacy within a real relationship, not proof that the relationship never existed. The alternative views are presented in the honesty note under 4:4.

Theme 4: The Jealous, Indwelling Spirit

The Byzantine reading κατῴκησεν in 4:5 presents the Holy Spirit as the one who has taken up residence in believers and who jealously desires their undivided loyalty. Whether the subject is the Spirit (Byzantine) or God who caused the spirit to dwell (critical), the verse affirms God's passionate, covenantal claim on the believer — a jealousy grounded in love, not petty envy.

Theme 5: Greater Grace for the Humble

"He gives greater grace" (4:6) is the theological center of the section and perhaps of the letter's practical teaching. God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (Prov 3:34). The humility required is not a condition of eternal life but the posture in which believers receive the continuing grace that makes sanctification possible. Both Peter (1 Pet 5:5) and James use this proverb in exhortations to believers.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance; Reception

The two-wisdoms passage has been a classic text on Christian character; its list of the qualities of heavenly wisdom (3:17) has often been compared to the fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22–23) and the portrait of love (1 Cor 13). James 4:4 ("friendship with the world is enmity with God") has been central in Christian teaching on worldliness, and 4:6 (with 1 Pet 5:5) in teaching on humility. Augustine cited 4:6 frequently in his writings on grace against the Pelagians, as evidence that even humility and obedience depend on God's giving.

James 4:5 has perplexed interpreters from the patristic period onward. Some ancient and modern interpreters have searched for a lost apocryphal source for the "Scripture" cited; others have taken the verse as a summary of OT teaching on God's jealousy. The conjecture φθονεῖτε in 4:2, proposed by Erasmus and adopted by Luther, illustrates how strongly interpreters have felt the harshness of "you murder," though the manuscript evidence is unanimous for φονεύετε.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

3:17 — ἀδιάκριτος καὶ ἀνυπόκριτος (Byz) vs. ἀδιάκριτος, ἀνυπόκριτος (NA28): connective only. 3:18 — τῆς δικαιοσύνης (Byz) vs. δικαιοσύνης (NA28): article only. 4:1 — single πόθεν (Byz) vs. repeated πόθεν (NA28): stylistic. 4:2 — Byzantine and NA28 agree (οὐκ ἔχετε, without the Textus Receptus's δέ); the conjecture φθονεῖτε has no manuscript support. 4:4 — Μοιχοὶ καὶ μοιχαλίδες (Byz) vs. μοιχαλίδες (NA28): the critical reading emphasizes the prophetic image of Israel as unfaithful wife; the Byzantine addresses men and women. 4:5 — κατῴκησεν (Byz) vs. κατῴκισεν (NA28): one vowel, but a real difference in who "yearns" — the indwelling Spirit (Byz) or God, who caused the spirit to dwell (NA28).

The 4:5 variant is the most interpretively significant in this section and one of the most significant in the letter. The reader is encouraged to verify individual sigla against the NA28 apparatus and the Robinson–Pierpont and Hodges–Farstad editions before citing them in teaching.
