Introduction to James 5:1–11

This section falls into two sharply contrasting halves. In 5:1–6, James turns from the merchants of 4:13–17 to the wealthy oppressors, introduced with the same "Come now" but addressed in the manner of an OT prophetic oracle of doom: their wealth has rotted, their withheld wages cry out to the Lord of Hosts, and they have fattened themselves for a day of slaughter. Notably, there is no call to repent here — only announcement of judgment — which distinguishes this paragraph from every other address in the letter.

In 5:7–11, James turns back to "brothers" with the word that governs the rest of the letter: μακροθυμήσατε, "be patient." The farmer waiting for the early and late rains, the prophets who suffered, and Job who endured become models for believers awaiting the παρουσία of the Lord. In the middle stands a warning that ties the section to the whole letter: do not grumble against one another, "so that you may not be judged — behold, the Judge stands at the door" (v. 9).

For the Free Grace reader, three threads converge here. First, the oppressors of vv. 1–6 appear to stand outside the believing community, which sharpens the contrast with the erring brothers James addresses elsewhere. Second, the warning of v. 9 is the fourth in the letter's chain of judgment texts addressed to believers (2:12–13; 3:1; 4:11–12), and the Judge who "stands at the door" is the Lord whose Judgment Seat evaluates His own. Third, v. 11 closes the circle opened in 1:2–4, 12: Job's ὑπομονή is the endurance under trial that James commended at the outset, and "the end of the Lord" shows what that endurance yields.

The summons Ἄγε νῦν repeats 4:13, but the tone shifts entirely. There James corrected presumptuous planners and gave them a remedy ("you ought to say, 'If the Lord wills'"). Here he issues a prophetic oracle of doom. The command κλαύσατε ὀλολύζοντες — "weep, howling" — uses a verb (ὀλολύζω) found nowhere else in the NT but common in the LXX prophets when announcing the day of the Lord (Isa 13:6; Amos 8:3). The rich are not invited to repent but told to begin mourning over ταῖς ταλαιπωρίαις … ταῖς ἐπερχομέναις, miseries already on their way.

Who are these rich? In 1:10 James addressed a rich brother, and in 4:13–17 he corrected businessmen who seem to belong to the community. Here there is no "brother," no vocative of affection, and no call to repentance — only announced judgment, as in 2:6–7, where the rich oppress believers and blaspheme the noble name. Most interpreters therefore take vv. 1–6 as addressed to wealthy unbelievers outside the congregation, spoken in the prophetic manner for the encouragement of the believers who suffer under them, much as the OT prophets addressed foreign nations in oracles their own people would hear. This reading also explains why the passage flows directly into "be patient, therefore, brothers" (v. 7).

The two perfect verbs, σέσηπεν and γέγονεν, function as prophetic perfects: the ruin is described as already complete because it is certain. In the ancient world, wealth was held in three forms — agricultural produce, garments, and precious metals — and James addresses each in turn: produce rots (v. 2a), garments are consumed by moths (v. 2b), and metals corrode (v. 3).

The imagery is close to Jesus's teaching: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (Matt 6:19–20). The point is not that wealth is evil but that hoarded wealth is already perishing; those who have built their security on it have built on what is passing away, like the flower of the field in 1:10–11.

Gold and silver do not rust, which is precisely the point: κατίωται is rhetorical, declaring that even the most enduring form of wealth has corroded. The ἰός (rust) becomes a legal witness — εἰς μαρτύριον ὑμῖν, "as testimony against you." Hoarded wealth testifies in the divine courtroom that it was never used for mercy toward the poor (cf. 1:27; 2:15–16). The same noun ἰός described the tongue's "deadly poison" in 3:8; in both cases something within a person's own possession turns lethal.

The corrosion then φάγεται τὰς σάρκας ὑμῶν ὡς πῦρ — "will devour your flesh like fire," an image of eschatological judgment. The closing sentence is bitterly ironic: Ἐθησαυρίσατε ἐν ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις. They have been "storing up treasure" — in the last days, the very time when judgment is imminent. Some read it as "you have stored up treasure for the last days," which is even sharper: what they have accumulated is wrath. Either way, they have laid up exactly the wrong thing at exactly the wrong time (contrast Matt 6:20).

James 5:4 — The Wages That Cry Out

James 5:4 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Ἰδού, ὁ μισθὸς τῶν ἐργατῶν τῶν ἀμησάντων τὰς χώρας ὑμῶν, ὁ ἀπεστερημένος ἀφ᾽ ὑμῶν, κράζει· καὶ αἱ βοαὶ τῶν θερισάντων εἰς τὰ ὦτα κυρίου Σαβαὼθ εἰσεληλύθασιν.
Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you have kept back by fraud, cry out; and the cries of those who reaped have entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies.
Textual Note — ἀπεστερημένος vs. ἀφυστερημένος: (a) Byzantine: ὁ ἀπεστερημένος ἀφ᾽ ὑμῶν (from ἀποστερέω, "defraud, rob, withhold wrongfully"). (b) NA28/UBS5: ὁ ἀφυστερημένος ἀφ᾽ ὑμῶν (from ἀφυστερέω, "withhold, keep back"). (c) Witnesses: ἀφυστερημένος is read by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; ἀπεστερημένος by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). (d) Significance: slight but not nil. The Byzantine verb is the stronger and more common term for outright fraud (cf. Mark 10:19, "do not defraud"; 1 Cor 6:7–8), and it matches the LXX prohibition in Malachi 3:5 and Deuteronomy 24:14–15 against defrauding a hired worker. The rarer ἀφυστερέω is preferred by critical editors on the principle of the more difficult reading. The WEB's "kept back by fraud" reflects the Byzantine sense.

The specific sin is named: withholding the wages of day-laborers. The Law required that a hired worker be paid the same day, "for he is poor and counts on it" (Deut 24:14–15; Lev 19:13 — again the chapter that underlies so much of James). Malachi lists those who "oppress the wage earner in his wages" among those God will judge (Mal 3:5). The Byzantine participle ἀπεστερημένος calls it fraud outright.

James personifies the injustice twice. The wages themselves κράζει — cry out, as Abel's blood cried from the ground (Gen 4:10). And the βοαί of the reapers εἰσεληλύθασιν — have entered (perfect tense: they have arrived and remain) into the ears of κυρίου Σαβαώθ, the Lord of Hosts. The title is a transliteration of the Hebrew for "armies," and it is chosen deliberately: the defrauded workers have no advocate, but the commander of heaven's armies has heard them. For believers suffering under such employers — the very people dragged into court in 2:6 — this is the assurance that undergirds the call to patience in v. 7.

James 5:5 — Fattened for the Day of Slaughter

James 5:5 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Ἐτρυφήσατε ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς καὶ ἐσπαταλήσατε· ἐθρέψατε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν ὡς ἐν ἡμέρᾳ σφαγῆς.
You have lived in luxury on the earth, and taken your pleasure. You have nourished your hearts as in a day of slaughter.
Textual Note — ὡς: (a) Byzantine: ἐθρέψατε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν ὡς ἐν ἡμέρᾳ σφαγῆς ("as in a day of slaughter"). Three aorists summarize a way of life: Ἐτρυφήσατε … ἐσπαταλήσατε … ἐθρέψατε. The first two describe luxury and wanton self-indulgence; Paul uses the second of the widow who "lives in pleasure" and is "dead while she lives" (1 Tim 5:6). The location is telling: ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, on the earth — the only sphere these men recognize, like the "earthly" wisdom of 3:15.

The third verb turns the image grim. ἐθρέψατε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν — "you fattened your hearts" — uses the vocabulary of feeding livestock, and the phrase ἐν ἡμέρᾳ σφαγῆς echoes Jeremiah 12:3, where the wicked are set apart "for the day of slaughter." The rich are cattle at the trough, growing fat, unaware that the feast day they are being prepared for is the day of their own butchering. The Byzantine ὡς makes the comparison explicit.

The indictment climaxes: Κατεδικάσατε, ἐφονεύσατε τὸν δίκαιον. The asyndeton (no "and") makes the two verbs land like hammer blows. The sequence is judicial: a verdict obtained (καταδικάζω) and then a death — whether by literal execution through corrupt courts (cf. 2:6) or by the slower killing of defrauding a poor man of the wages he needs to live (v. 4).

Who is ὁ δίκαιος? The article with the singular adjective is most naturally generic: "the righteous person," standing for all the poor believers oppressed by the wealthy (cf. Wisdom 2:10–20, where the ungodly plot against "the righteous man"). Some interpreters have seen a reference to Christ, the Righteous One (Acts 3:14; 7:52; 1 John 2:1), and a few have noted the tradition that James himself was called "James the Just." The generic reading fits the context best, though the echo of Christ's own unresisting death is unmistakable.

The last clause, οὐκ ἀντιτάσσεται ὑμῖν, can be read three ways: (1) as a statement about the righteous man — he does not resist, having no power in the courts (cf. Isa 53:7; 1 Pet 2:23); (2) as a question — "Does he not oppose you?" — with God or the righteous one as subject; or (3) with God as the unstated subject. The verb is the one James used in 4:6: "God opposes the proud." That verbal link is suggestive. The righteous man does not resist, but the God who resists the proud has heard the cries (v. 4), and He will act.

James 5:7 — Be Patient Like the Farmer

James 5:7 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Μακροθυμήσατε οὖν, ἀδελφοί, ἕως τῆς παρουσίας τοῦ κυρίου. Ἰδού, ὁ γεωργὸς ἐκδέχεται τὸν τίμιον καρπὸν τῆς γῆς, μακροθυμῶν ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν, ἕως λάβῃ ὑετὸν πρώϊμον καὶ ὄψιμον.
Be patient therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord. Behold, the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it, until it receives the early and late rain.
Textual Note — ὑετόν: (a) Byzantine: μακροθυμῶν ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν, ἕως λάβῃ ὑετὸν πρώϊμον καὶ ὄψιμον ("until it receives the early and late rain"). (b) NA28/UBS5: μακροθυμῶν ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ ἕως λάβῃ πρόϊμον καὶ ὄψιμον, with no noun expressed and with the dative αὐτῷ; some witnesses supply καρπόν ("fruit") instead. The Textus Receptus reads ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ, ἕως ἂν λάβῃ ὑετόν; neither the Byzantine text nor NA28 has ἄν. (c) Witnesses: the shorter reading is carried by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; ὑετόν by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). (d) Significance: minimal for sense. "Early and late" were standing agricultural terms for the autumn rains that softened the ground for sowing and the spring rains that filled out the grain (Deut 11:14; Joel 2:23), so "rain" is understood in any case. The Byzantine text makes it explicit. The WEB supplies "rain" as well.

The οὖν connects the two halves of the section: because the Lord of Hosts has heard and judgment is coming on the oppressors, Μακροθυμήσατε … ἀδελφοί. The verb μακροθυμέω (literally "to be long-tempered") is patience with people and with delay, distinct from the ὑπομονή of 1:3–4 (endurance under pressure), though v. 11 will bring the two together. The horizon is ἡ παρουσία τοῦ κυρίου — the coming of the Lord, the letter's clearest eschatological reference.

The model is the farmer, who ἐκδέχεται τὸν τίμιον καρπὸν τῆς γῆς, waiting for the precious crop. He cannot hurry it; he can only wait through the months between the πρώϊμον rains of autumn, which soften the ground for sowing, and the ὄψιμον rains of spring, which swell the grain before harvest (Deut 11:14; Joel 2:23). The believer's situation is the same: the harvest is certain, the timing is not his, and the waiting is active rather than idle. Note that the crop is τίμιος, precious — worth the wait. Compare 3:18, where the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace, and 1:4, where endurance must be allowed to finish its work.

The command is repeated and extended: Μακροθυμήσατε καὶ ὑμεῖς, στηρίξατε τὰς καρδίας ὑμῶν. The verb στηρίζω means to prop up, make firm, establish. It is the remedy for the heart problems the letter has diagnosed throughout — the double-minded man "unstable in all his ways" (1:8), the heart harboring bitter jealousy (3:14), the divided heart needing purification (4:8). The rich fattened their hearts (v. 5); believers are to fortify theirs.

The ground is ἡ παρουσία τοῦ κυρίου ἤγγικεν — "the coming of the Lord has drawn near." The perfect tense describes a settled state of nearness (the same verb and tense as in Jesus's proclamation, "the kingdom of God has drawn near," Mark 1:15). Note the reciprocity with 4:8: believers are to draw near to God, and God will draw near to them; here the Lord Himself is drawing near in His return. Eschatology in James is pastoral: the certainty of Christ's coming is what makes patience possible under oppression, and it also makes conduct urgent, as the next verse shows.

Pressure from outside tends to produce friction inside. Having called for patience under oppression, James warns against the grumbling it breeds: Μὴ στενάζετε κατ᾽ ἀλλήλων, ἀδελφοί. The verb στενάζω is to groan or sigh; directed "against one another," it is the muttered complaint that turns fellow sufferers into targets. This is the same community problem addressed in 4:11 (speaking against a brother) and 2:4 (becoming judges).

The motive clause, ἵνα μὴ κριθῆτε, echoes Jesus: "Do not judge, so that you may not be judged" (Matt 7:1). And the ground is vivid: ὁ κριτὴς πρὸ τῶν θυρῶν ἕστηκεν — the Judge has taken His stand at the doors. The perfect tense pictures Him already in position, about to enter (cf. Matt 24:33, "he is near, at the very gates"). He is the one Lawgiver and Judge of 4:12, and His nearness is the same nearness announced in v. 8.

This is the fourth warning of judgment addressed to believers in the letter (2:12–13; 3:1; 4:11–12; 5:9), and the Free Grace reading takes them as a coherent set: the Judgment Seat of Christ, where believers give account of their conduct — including how they treated one another under pressure — and receive or forfeit reward (Rom 14:10–12; 1 Cor 3:12–15; 2 Cor 5:10). James addresses them as "brothers" in the same breath. The warning is not that grumbling will send them to hell but that the Judge is at the door and they will answer to Him.

James offers the prophets as a ὑπόδειγμα — a pattern to be copied — of two things held together: κακοπάθεια (suffering hardship) and μακροθυμία (patience). The relative clause is pointed: these are men οἳ ἐλάλησαν τῷ ὀνόματι κυρίου, who spoke in the Lord's name. Their suffering came not despite their faithfulness but because of it (cf. Matt 5:12; 23:34–37; Heb 11:32–38). James's readers, suffering at the hands of the rich, are in good company.

This also answers a possible objection. If God has heard the cries of the oppressed (v. 4) and the Judge is at the door (v. 9), why does the oppression continue? Because that has always been the pattern: God's own spokesmen endured, and their vindication came in God's time, not theirs.

The section closes where the letter began. μακαρίζομεν τοὺς ὑπομένοντας — "we count blessed those who endure" — uses the cognate verb of the beatitude in 1:12: "Blessed (μακάριος) is the man who endures (ὑπομένει) trial, for having been approved he will receive the crown of life." The noun ὑπομονή is the same one that "the testing of your faith produces" (1:3) and that must be allowed its complete work (1:4). The Byzantine present participle keeps this in the readers' own present: those who are enduring, right now, are the blessed ones.

Job is the illustration — his only mention in the NT. The book of Job shows a man who suffered without explanation, complained bitterly, questioned God at length, and yet never let go of Him. That is ὑπομονή: not stoic calm, but remaining under the pressure without abandoning God. This is worth noting pastorally. James does not present Job as unruffled; he presents him as one who held on.

The phrase τὸ τέλος κυρίου is best taken as "the outcome brought about by the Lord" — the restoration of Job in chapter 42 — rather than "the end of the Lord" in the sense of Christ's death. The noun τέλος is the root of τέλειος, the maturity theme running from 1:4 through 3:2, and of ἐτελειώθη in 2:22, where Abraham's faith was brought to completion by works. The pattern is consistent: God brings His purposes for the enduring believer to a good end, "because the Lord is πολύσπλαγχνος καὶ οἰκτίρμων" — full of compassion and merciful, language drawn from God's self-revelation to Moses (Exod 34:6; Ps 103:8). For believers facing oppression, loss, and delay, the letter's answer is not an explanation but a character: the God who ends the story is compassionate.

Overall Exegesis: James 5:1–11

This section surveys the architecture of the passage, its principal theological themes, its reception, and the textual situation.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The passage has two contrasting halves joined by οὖν in v. 7.

(A) Oracle against the rich (vv. 1–6). Summons to wail (v. 1); the threefold corruption of wealth — produce, garments, metals (vv. 2–3); the crying wages and the Lord of Hosts (v. 4); luxury and the day of slaughter (v. 5); the condemnation and killing of the righteous (v. 6). The form is prophetic: announcement of doom with no call to repent.

(B) Exhortation to the brothers (vv. 7–11). Be patient until the coming of the Lord, like the farmer awaiting the rains (v. 7); be patient and strengthen your hearts, for the coming is near (v. 8); do not grumble against one another, for the Judge is at the door (v. 9); take the prophets as an example of suffering and patience (v. 10); we count blessed those who endure — Job and the compassionate outcome the Lord gave (v. 11).

The two halves are bound by contrast: the rich store treasure in the last days (v. 3), while believers await the Lord's coming (vv. 7–8); the rich fatten their hearts (v. 5), while believers strengthen theirs (v. 8); the rich condemned the righteous (v. 6), while believers are warned not to judge one another (v. 9). The section also closes the letter's major inclusio, returning to the endurance, testing, and blessedness of 1:2–4, 12.

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: God Hears the Oppressed

Withheld wages cry out, and the shouts of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of Hosts (v. 4). James stands in the line of the Law (Lev 19:13; Deut 24:14–15) and the prophets (Mal 3:5; Amos 8:4–6) in treating the exploitation of workers as a sin God personally avenges. This is the theological foundation for the patience commanded in v. 7: believers need not retaliate, because God has heard.

Theme 2: The Deceptive Security of Wealth

Wealth rots, garments are eaten, metals corrode, and the corrosion becomes courtroom evidence (vv. 2–3). The theme runs through the letter (1:10–11; 2:6–7; 4:13–16) and matches Jesus's warning about treasures on earth (Matt 6:19–20). What indicts the rich is not that they had wealth but that they hoarded it while defrauding the poor who produced it.

Theme 3: Patience Grounded in the Parousia

Twice James commands μακροθυμήσατε (vv. 7, 8) and grounds it in the Lord's coming, which "has drawn near." The farmer illustrates the posture: active waiting through a process he cannot hasten. Eschatology in James is pastoral rather than speculative — the certainty of Christ's return sustains believers under oppression and makes present conduct urgent.

Theme 4: The Judge at the Door

Verse 9 completes the letter's chain of judgment warnings to believers (2:12–13; 3:1; 4:11–12). The Free Grace reading takes these together as referring to the evaluation of believers' works at the Judgment Seat of Christ, where reward may be gained or lost (1 Cor 3:12–15; 2 Cor 5:10). The addressees are "brothers," and the sin in view is grumbling against one another under shared pressure.

Theme 5: Endurance, Job, and the Compassion of God

The letter's opening theme returns: endurance under trial, and blessedness for those who endure (1:2–4, 12; 5:11). Job is the model — not of composure but of holding on to God through incomprehensible loss. The phrase "the end of the Lord" points to the outcome God gave Job, and the reason is given in God's own covenant self-description: He is compassionate and merciful (Exod 34:6). For Free Grace theology, this is the proper motivation for perseverance: not fear of losing eternal life, but confidence in the character of the God who brings His purposes to a good end and rewards those who endure.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance; Reception

James 5:1–6 is among the most forceful denunciations of economic injustice in the NT and has been prominent in Christian social teaching across traditions. The passage's prophetic form — woe, indictment, announced judgment — deliberately imitates Amos, Isaiah, and Jeremiah, and its use of "Lord of Hosts" (Σαβαώθ), rare in the NT, ties it to that tradition.

"The patience of Job" (5:11) became proverbial in English through this verse, though the phrase in Greek is endurance rather than placid patience, and the book itself shows Job protesting vigorously. The description of God as "compassionate and merciful" draws on the confession of Exodus 34:6, the most frequently repeated self-description of God in the OT. James 5:7–11 has long served the church as a foundational text on Christian patience under persecution, alongside 1 Peter.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 4 — ἀπεστερημένος, "defrauded" (Byz) vs. ἀφυστερημένος, "withheld" (NA28): the Byzantine verb is the stronger term for fraud and matches the LXX vocabulary of Lev 19:13 and Deut 24:14–15. v. 5 — ὡς present (Byz) vs. absent (NA28): with ὡς, the rich are fattened "as for" a day of slaughter; without it, they feast "on" that day. v. 7 — ὑετόν, "rain," expressed (Byz) vs. unexpressed (NA28); also ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν (Byz) vs. ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ (NA28). v. 9 — κριθῆτε (Byz and NA28); the TR reads κατακριθῆτε ("be condemned"), a distinction that bears on the exegesis. v. 10 — ἀδελφοί μου and the bare dative τῷ ὀνόματι (Byz) vs. ἀδελφοί and ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι (NA28). v. 11 — ὑπομένοντας, present, "those who are enduring" (Byz) vs. ὑπομείναντας, aorist, "those who endured" (NA28); ἴδετε, "see!" (Byz text; εἴδετε in the RP margin) vs. εἴδετε, "you saw" (NA28); ὁ κύριος absent (Byz) vs. present (NA28) as subject of ἐστιν.

The variants at vv. 4 and 11 are the most interesting exegetically, and neither affects doctrine. The reader is encouraged to verify individual sigla against the NA28 apparatus and the Robinson–Pierpont and Hodges–Farstad editions before citing them in teaching.
