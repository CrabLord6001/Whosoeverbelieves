Introduction to James 1:13–27

James 1:13–27 completes the letter's opening chapter and supplies the theological foundation for chapter 2. It falls into two movements. In 1:13–18, James turns the word πειρασμός from external trial (1:2, 12) to inner temptation: God is not its source; human desire is, and it gives birth to sin and death. Against that dark genealogy James sets a second birth — God "brought us forth by the word of truth" (1:18). In 1:19–27, he applies this: believers must receive the implanted word, which is "able to save your souls" (1:21), and must be doers of it, not hearers only (1:22–25), expressing true religion in controlled speech and practical mercy (1:26–27).

For the Free Grace reading of the whole letter, two verses here are decisive. Verse 18 establishes that the readers are already regenerate: their new birth is God's sovereign act, accomplished by the word. Verse 21 then speaks of a salvation of the ψυχή that these regenerate people still need — which cannot be regeneration itself. That distinction, established here, governs how σῶσαι is read in 2:14. Likewise, the "perfect law of liberty" (1:25) is the standard by which believers will be judged in 2:12, and the "orphans and widows in their affliction" (1:27) anticipate the destitute brother and sister of 2:15–16.

James now exploits the double meaning of πειράζω. The same word that described providential trials in 1:2, 12 ("testing") can describe solicitation to evil ("temptation"). Genesis 22:1 LXX says God ἐπείραζεν Abraham — the testing James will invoke in 2:21–22. But God never solicits anyone to sin. A believer under trial may be tempted to blame God for the pressure that exposes his desires; James forbids the excuse. The preposition ἀπό rules out even indirect divine causation of temptation.

The grounds are twofold. God is ἀπείραστος κακῶν — a rare word, most naturally "incapable of being tempted by evil," since nothing in His nature responds to it. And He πειράζει … οὐδένα: He tempts no one. The trial is from God; the temptation within the trial is not. For the Free Grace reader this preserves the pastoral logic of 1:2–12: trials are God's instruments for maturing believers and positioning them for the crown, but when a believer fails under trial, the responsibility is his own.

The true agent of temptation is ἡ ἰδία ἐπιθυμία — "one's own desire." The change of preposition from ἀπό (v. 13) to ὑπό (v. 14) is precise: God is not even the remote source; desire is the direct agent. The two participles come from the language of hunting and fishing. ἐξελκόμενος pictures prey drawn out of its hiding place; δελεαζόμενος (from δέλεαρ, bait) pictures the fish taking the lure. Desire both drags and deceives.

This is written to believers. James does not suggest that regeneration eliminates ἐπιθυμία; he assumes that "each one" of his brothers is vulnerable to it. That realism agrees with Paul's account of the flesh warring against the Spirit in the believer (Gal 5:16–17, treated in our Galatians series) and supports the Free Grace refusal to make sinlessness, or near-sinlessness, the evidence of new birth.

James sketches a grim genealogy. Desire συλλαβοῦσα ("having conceived") gives birth to sin; sin ἀποτελεσθεῖσα ("having come to full growth") brings forth death. The birth imagery is sustained and deliberate. The participle ἀποτελεσθεῖσα is a dark parody of 1:4: endurance, allowed its perfect work, makes the believer τέλειος; sin, allowed its full course, reaches its own "maturity" in death. And the verb ἀποκύει reappears in v. 18 of God's act of begetting believers. There are two families: desire → sin → death, and God → the word of truth → new life.

What death is meant? Many take it as eternal death. But James is describing a process in the life of "each one" of the brothers (v. 14), and he later speaks of turning a sinning brother back so as to "save a soul from death" (5:20). The Free Grace reading, following Hodges, takes θάνατος here as the destructive end of sin in the believer's experience — ultimately physical death as divine discipline (cf. 1 Cor 11:30; 1 John 5:16) — rather than the loss of eternal life, which the believer cannot forfeit (John 10:28). This is the danger from which the implanted word is "able to save your souls" (v. 21).

Honesty Note: Most commentators (e.g., Moo, Davids, McKnight) take θάνατος in 1:15 as eschatological death — final separation from God — contrasted with the "crown of life" of 1:12. That contrast is real and should be weighed: life/death antitheses in Scripture often have eschatological weight (Rom 6:23). The Free Grace response is that James describes a sequence within believers ("each one," "my beloved brothers," vv. 14, 16), that 5:19–20 applies "death" language to a sinning brother, and that the crown of life in 1:12 is itself reward language rather than a synonym for eternal life. Both readings agree that sin is deadly and that James's warning must be taken with full seriousness.

This short prohibition is a hinge. Μὴ πλανᾶσθε looks back: do not be deceived into thinking God tempts you (v. 13). Having denied that God is the source of temptation, James affirms what God is the source of: πᾶσα δόσις ἀγαθὴ καὶ πᾶν δώρημα τέλειον. The first half of the line may be a hexameter in Greek, suggesting a quoted proverb. The two nouns are near-synonyms — the act of giving and the gift given — and together they claim that every good thing comes ἄνωθεν. The word τέλειον ties this verse to 1:4 (endurance's "perfect work") and forward to 1:25 ("perfect law"). The wisdom promised in 1:5 is one such gift; the new birth of 1:18 is the greatest.

God is ὁ πατὴρ τῶν φώτων, the Creator of the heavenly lights (Gen 1:14–18). Unlike the sun, moon, and stars, which change position and cast shifting shadows, God has no παραλλαγή ("variation") or τροπῆς ἀποσκίασμα ("shadow caused by turning"). His goodness does not wax and wane. For the Free Grace reader, this immutability is the ground of assurance: the God who gave new birth "of his own will" (v. 18) does not change His mind about the gift. Eternal life, being His gift, is as constant as the Giver (cf. Rom 11:29).

This is the theological anchor of the whole letter. βουληθεὶς ἀπεκύησεν ἡμᾶς λόγῳ ἀληθείας — "having willed, he brought us forth by the word of truth." The fronted participle βουληθείς puts all the emphasis on God's initiative: new birth originates in His will, not in human performance (cf. John 1:13). The aorist ἀπεκύησεν describes a completed act. The instrument is λόγος ἀληθείας — the gospel message (cf. 1 Pet 1:23; Eph 1:13). And the verb deliberately answers v. 15: sin ἀποκύει death; God ἀπεκύησεν us.

Although a few interpreters have taken v. 18 as a reference to the original creation of humanity, the language of "word of truth" and "firstfruits" fits new birth far better, and most commentators (Moo, Davids, McKnight, Johnson) read it so. The first-person plural ἡμᾶς includes James and his readers. This is decisive for Free Grace interpretation of the letter: whatever James says afterwards about "saving souls" (1:21), "dead faith" (2:17), or "can that faith save him?" (2:14) is said to people who have already been brought forth by God's will. The later warnings cannot be warnings about acquiring regeneration.

The purpose is that believers be ἀπαρχή τις τῶν αὐτοῦ κτισμάτων — "a kind of firstfruits of his creatures." In Israel the firstfruits were the first sheaf offered to God, belonging to Him and pledging the full harvest to come. Believers are the first installment of God's renewed creation (cf. Rom 8:23), and their lives are meant to display it — which leads directly into the ethical imperatives of vv. 19–27.

James 1:19 — Swift to Hear, Slow to Speak

James 1:19 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Ὥστε, ἀδελφοί μου ἀγαπητοί, ἔστω πᾶς ἄνθρωπος ταχὺς εἰς τὸ ἀκοῦσαι, βραδὺς εἰς τὸ λαλῆσαι, βραδὺς εἰς ὀργήν·
So, then, my beloved brothers, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger;
Textual Note — Ὥστε vs. Ἴστε … δέ: (a) Byzantine: Ὥστε, ἀδελφοί μου ἀγαπητοί, ἔστω πᾶς ἄνθρωπος ("So then, my beloved brothers, let every man…"). (b) NA28/UBS5: Ἴστε, ἀδελφοί μου ἀγαπητοί· ἔστω δὲ πᾶς ἄνθρωπος ("Know this, my beloved brothers; and let every man…"). (c) Witnesses: Ἴστε is read by the leading Alexandrian witnesses; Ὥστε by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). (d) Significance: moderate. The Byzantine ὥστε makes v. 19 an explicit inference from v. 18: because God begot us by the word, we must be quick to hear it. This verse is, as flagged at the start of the series, one of the pivotal Free Grace cruxes in James. The readers are told to put off moral filth and to δέξασθε τὸν ἔμφυτον λόγον, "receive the implanted word." The adjective ἔμφυτος means "implanted" or "rooted in." The word is already in them — planted at their new birth by "the word of truth" (v. 18). What they must now do is welcome it ἐν πρᾳΰτητι, with teachable humility, instead of anger (v. 20). Their reception is not initial belief but ongoing responsiveness.

This implanted word is τὸν δυνάμενον σῶσαι τὰς ψυχὰς ὑμῶν — "able to save your souls." The addressees are already regenerate (v. 18), so this salvation cannot be regeneration or the gift of eternal life. What then? The noun ψυχή very often means "life" (e.g., Matt 16:25, "whoever wants to save his psychē will lose it"). Hodges and Wilkin read "save your souls" as "save your lives": the word, received and obeyed, delivers the believer from the death that sin produces (v. 15). The phrase returns at the end of the letter: turning a sinning brother back "will save a soul from death" (5:20, σώσει ψυχὴν ἐκ θανάτου). The letter is bracketed by this salvation of the ψυχή from death.

The significance for chapter 2 is direct. The exact construction δύναμαι + σῶσαι recurs in 2:14: "Can faith save him?" (μὴ δύναται ἡ πίστις σῶσαι αὐτόν;). If 1:21 speaks of a salvation that regenerate believers still need, the most natural reading of 2:14 is the same kind of salvation — deliverance from sin's deadly consequences and from the merciless judgment of 2:13 — rather than regeneration. This is one of the strongest internal arguments for the Free Grace reading of James 2 presented in the previous document.

Honesty Note: Most commentators (Moo, Davids, McKnight) take "save your souls" in 1:21 as eschatological salvation — final deliverance at the judgment — noting that James elsewhere speaks of the Lord's coming and the Judge standing at the door (5:7–9). Many also note that the NT uses "salvation" in a future sense for believers (Rom 13:11; 1 Pet 1:5, 9). These are genuine points, and future salvation of believers is not foreign to the NT. The Free Grace response is that the question is not whether the salvation is future but what it is deliverance from. Because the readers are already born of God (1:18), and because James speaks of death as the end of sin (1:15) and of saving a soul "from death" (5:20), deliverance of the life from sin's deadly consequences fits the letter's own vocabulary best. Moo's reading, notably, also recognizes that James addresses Christians and that this salvation is not their initial conversion — so the two views agree more than is often assumed.

The benefit — the saving of the life (v. 21) and the blessing of v. 25 — comes to the doer. Jesus's parable of the two builders (Matt 7:24–27), which James appears to echo, makes the same point to disciples: both builders heard; only one built on the rock, and only his house stood.

The contrast to the forgetful hearer is the one who παρακύψας — stoops down to look intently — into the νόμον τέλειον τὸν τῆς ἐλευθερίας, and παραμείνας, continues in it. The verb παρακύπτω is used of Peter and Mary stooping to look into the empty tomb (John 20:5, 11) and of angels longing to look into salvation (1 Pet 1:12): concentrated, sustained attention. And παραμένω — remaining — is the practical form of the ὑπομονή (endurance) that runs through chapter 1.

The "perfect law of liberty" is the word of v. 21 viewed as a standard of conduct: God's moral will as summarized in the love command and taught by Jesus (2:8), written on the heart of the regenerate (Jer 31:33). It is τέλειος because it is complete and brings the believer to maturity (1:4); it is a law of ἐλευθερία because it is obeyed by those who have been set free, not by those seeking to earn freedom (cf. Gal 5:1, 13–14). The previous document showed that believers will be judged by this same "law of liberty" (2:12). Here we see its positive side: the doer is blessed now in the doing.

The closing beatitude, οὗτος μακάριος ἐν τῇ ποιήσει αὐτοῦ ἔσται, echoes 1:12 (Μακάριος ἀνήρ). The future ἔσται may include both present blessing experienced in obedience and future reward, for which 1:12 promised the crown of life. Either way, the blessing is conditioned on doing — which is exactly why it cannot be the gift of eternal life, received freely by faith (John 4:10–14). The Free Grace framework allows both to stand: eternal life is the unconditional gift of new birth (1:18); blessing and reward are the fruit of doing the word (1:25).

James is not discussing salvation but religious practice. A member of the congregation (ἐν ὑμῖν, Byzantine) may regard himself as devout while failing to χαλιναγωγεῖν — to put a bridle on — his tongue. The same rare verb reappears in 3:2, where the one who does not stumble in word is "able to bridle the whole body." Verse 26 thus previews chapter 3 and picks up "slow to speak" from v. 19.

Such a person ἀπατῶν καρδίαν αὐτοῦ — deceives his own heart — like the hearer-only who deceives himself (v. 22). His religion is μάταιος: futile, empty, a word the LXX uses of idols. The Free Grace reader should note the precision: James does not say this man's faith is false or his new birth doubtful. He says his religion — his outward religious life — is worthless. The category is usefulness, as it is throughout 1:22–2:26.

James closes the chapter with a positive definition. Religion that is καθαρὰ καὶ ἀμίαντος — words drawn from the vocabulary of cultic purity — "before God and Father" consists of two things expressed by epexegetical infinitives. First, ἐπισκέπτεσθαι ὀρφανοὺς καὶ χήρας ἐν τῇ θλίψει αὐτῶν: to look after orphans and widows in their distress. In the OT, God is "father of the fatherless and defender of widows" (Ps 68:5); the believer who is begotten by the Father (v. 18) reflects His character by caring for those He cares for. The verb ἐπισκέπτομαι is the verb of God's own gracious "visitation" (Luke 1:68) and of Jesus's judgment scene ("I was sick and you visited me," Matt 25:36).

Second, ἄσπιλον ἑαυτὸν τηρεῖν ἀπὸ τοῦ κόσμου: to keep oneself unstained by the world. James will develop "the world" as the rival allegiance that makes a believer double-minded and an adulterer toward God (4:4, 8). The two infinitives form a balanced whole: compassion outward, purity inward. True religion is neither mere social action nor mere personal piety; it is both.

This verse prepares the way for chapter 2. The widows and orphans of 1:27 become the poor man in the assembly (2:2–6) and the destitute brother or sister (2:15–16). The "world" whose stain must be avoided is the world that judges by gold rings and fine clothing (2:1–4). The pure religion of 1:27 is precisely the "works" that make faith alive in 2:14–26 — works of mercy toward the needy. James's argument about faith and works is therefore not a new topic in chapter 2 but the unfolding of what he has defined as true religion here.

Overall Exegesis: James 1:13–27

This section surveys the architecture of the passage, its principal theological themes, its reception, and the textual situation, and shows how 1:13–27 lays the foundation for James 2.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

The passage has two movements joined by the hinge of v. 16.

Movement 1 — Two Births (vv. 13–18). (A) Prohibition: do not blame God for temptation (v. 13). (B) The genealogy of death: desire → sin → death (vv. 14–15). (C) Hinge: "Do not be deceived" (v. 16). (D) The Giver of every good gift (v. 17). (E) The genealogy of life: God's will → the word of truth → new birth → firstfruits (v. 18). The verb ἀποκυέω appears only in vv. 15 and 18 in the whole NT, deliberately contrasting what sin begets with what God begets.

Movement 2 — Hearing and Doing (vv. 19–27). (A) Swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger (vv. 19–20). (B) Receive the implanted word, able to save your lives (v. 21). (C) Be doers, not hearers only (v. 22). (D) The mirror parable: the forgetful hearer vs. the persevering doer, blessed in his doing (vv. 23–25). (E) Worthless religion vs. pure religion (vv. 26–27). The triad of v. 19 is expanded in reverse: "slow to anger" (v. 20), "quick to hear" (vv. 21–25), "slow to speak" (v. 26).

Binding devices include the τέλειος family (vv. 17, 25; cf. 1:4 and ἀποτελεσθεῖσα, v. 15), the word (λόγος, vv. 18, 21, 22, 23), and the self-deception motif (πλανᾶσθε v. 16; παραλογιζόμενοι v. 22; ἀπατῶν v. 26).

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: The Source of Temptation and the Goodness of God

James distinguishes the trial, which God may send for the believer's maturing (1:2–4, 12; cf. Gen 22:1), from the temptation to sin, which arises from the believer's own desire (1:13–14). God is untemptable and tempts no one; He is the unchanging Father of lights from whom only good gifts come (v. 17). This protects both God's character and the believer's responsibility, and it undergirds the pastoral call to joy in trials: the believer can trust the God who sends the trial precisely because He is not the author of the temptation within it.

Theme 2: New Birth by the Word of Truth

Verse 18 is the letter's clearest statement of regeneration, and its significance for Free Grace reading can hardly be overstated. New birth is by God's will (βουληθείς), accomplished (ἀπεκύησεν, aorist), by the word of truth (the gospel), and it constitutes believers as firstfruits of the new creation. Everything James says afterward — about saving the ψυχή, being doers, dead faith, and justification by works — is addressed to people who have already received this birth. The letter's warnings therefore concern the life, usefulness, and reward of the regenerate, not the acquisition of eternal life.

Theme 3: The Saving of the ψυχή

The implanted word is "able to save your souls" (1:21). Since the readers are already born of God, this salvation is not regeneration. The Free Grace reading (Hodges, Wilkin) takes ψυχή as "life" and the salvation as deliverance from the death that sin produces (1:15; cf. 5:20). The construction δύναμαι + σῶσαι recurs in 2:14, providing the strongest internal key to the question "Can that faith save him?" This theme brackets the letter: 1:21 and 5:20 both speak of the saving of the ψυχή in the context of sin and death among believers.

Theme 4: Hearing, Doing, and Blessing

The believer is to be a doer of the word, not a hearer only (1:22). The adverbial μόνον anticipates 2:24. The doer who looks intently into the perfect law of liberty and continues in it "will be blessed in his doing" (1:25) — a beatitude that echoes 1:12 and connects obedience with present blessing and future reward. The perfect law of liberty introduced here is also the standard of the believer's judgment in 2:12. The whole sequence — hearing, doing, blessing, judgment — belongs to the life of the regenerate before God.

Theme 5: Pure Religion as Mercy and Purity

True religion is defined by care for orphans and widows and by separation from the world's stain (1:27). This definition becomes the lens for chapter 2: favoritism toward the rich (2:1–13) is worldly defilement, and the "works" whose absence makes faith dead (2:14–26) are works of mercy toward the needy. James's theology of faith and works is thus rooted in his definition of true religion.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance; Reception

James 1:17 ("every good and perfect gift") has had wide liturgical use, and its description of God as the "Father of lights" without variation has been important in discussions of divine immutability from the patristic period onward. James 1:18, along with 1 Peter 1:23, has long been read as a key text on regeneration through the word, and Protestant theology in particular emphasized the "word of truth" as the instrument of new birth. James 1:22 ("be doers of the word") became a watchword of practical Christianity in Puritan and Pietist traditions. The call to care for orphans and widows (1:27) has inspired a long history of Christian social ministry.

Canonically, the passage draws on Genesis (creation of the lights, Gen 1:14–18; Abraham's testing, Gen 22:1), the Psalms (the perfect law, Ps 19:7), the Wisdom tradition (swift to hear, slow to speak — Prov 10:19; Sir 5:11), and the Jesus tradition (the wise and foolish builders, Matt 7:24–27; the final judgment, Matt 25:36). Its birth imagery parallels 1 Peter 1:23 and John 3:3–8, and its law-of-liberty language anticipates Paul's "law of Christ" (Gal 6:2) and freedom in the Spirit (Gal 5:1, 13), treated in our Galatians series.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

v. 17 — Byzantine and NA28 agree on ἢ τροπῆς ἀποσκίασμα against a difficult early variant (ἀποσκιάσματος) in a small group of early witnesses. v. 19 — Ὥστε (Byz) vs. Ἴστε … δέ (NA28): the Byzantine reading makes v. 19 an explicit inference from the new birth of v. 18. v. 20 — κατεργάζεται (Byz) vs. ἐργάζεται (NA28): the Byzantine verb echoes 1:3. v. 25 — first οὗτος (Byz) vs. omission (NA28): stylistic. v. 26 — ἐν ὑμῖν (Byz) vs. omission (NA28); αὐτοῦ (Byz) vs. ἑαυτοῦ (NA28); accent θρῆσκος vs. θρησκός: ἐν ὑμῖν makes explicit the congregational setting. v. 27 — παρὰ θεῷ (Byz) vs. παρὰ τῷ θεῷ (NA28): article only.

As in the other documents, the Byzantine readings are supported by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐) and the critical readings by the leading Alexandrian witnesses. None of the variants in 1:13–27 affects doctrine, but two (vv. 19, 20) strengthen the internal links of James's argument. The reader is encouraged to verify individual sigla against the NA28 apparatus and the Robinson–Pierpont and Hodges–Farstad editions before citing them in teaching.
