Introduction to James 2:14–26

No passage in the New Testament has been more contested in the debate over faith and works. Luther found it hard to reconcile with Paul; the Council of Trent cited 2:24 in support of the increase of justification through works; Reformed theology has read it as teaching that genuine faith necessarily produces works, so that a faith without works shows itself to be spurious. The Free Grace tradition, represented especially by Zane Hodges (The Epistle of James; Dead Faith: What Is It?), Robert Wilkin, and Joseph Dillow, offers a distinct reading: James is addressing believers (ἀδελφοί μου, v. 14) about the usefulness of their faith in the face of the judgment just described in 2:12–13, not about how a person obtains eternal life.

The passage flows directly from 2:1–13. The partiality section ended with judgment by the law of liberty and the maxim "mercy triumphs over judgment." James now asks what faith that is not expressed in works of mercy can accomplish in that setting — "Can that faith save him?" His first illustration (vv. 15–16) is precisely a failure of mercy toward a destitute brother or sister. The questions this document tracks verse by verse are: (1) what "save" means in v. 14; (2) what "dead" faith is (vv. 17, 20, 26); (3) who is speaking in vv. 18–19, where the Byzantine and critical texts diverge significantly; and (4) what "justified by works" means in vv. 21–25. Where the Free Grace reading differs from the majority of commentators, the alternative is stated in honesty notes.

James opens with a question about ὄφελος — benefit, usefulness — and closes the paragraph (v. 16) with the same question. The frame matters: James is asking what good faith does, not whether it exists. The person in view is one of the ἀδελφοί μου. He "says he has faith," and James never says the claim is false. What he lacks is ἔργα. The article in ἡ πίστις is anaphoric, pointing back to the faith just described (faith without works), which is why many versions render "Can that faith save him?" The question with μή expects a negative answer.

The crux is σῶσαι. Readers conditioned by Pauline usage assume "save from hell / give eternal life." But James's own usage should govern. In 1:21 the implanted word "is able to save your souls" (σῶσαι τὰς ψυχὰς ὑμῶν), addressed to believers already "brought forth by the word of truth" (1:18) — so the salvation of 1:21 cannot be regeneration. In 5:15 "the prayer of faith will save the sick." In 5:19–20 turning an erring brother "will save a soul from death." Across the letter, σῴζω is deliverance of believers from the destructive consequences of sin, culminating in death (1:15). Immediately before 2:14, the context is the merciless judgment awaiting the believer who shows no mercy (2:13). Hodges and Wilkin therefore read the question as: "Can faith alone — without works of mercy — deliver a believer from that merciless judgment?" The answer is no; only mercy triumphs over judgment.

This reading keeps James and Paul from colliding without softening either. Paul insists that eternal life is received by faith apart from works (Rom 4:5; Eph 2:8–9; Gal 2:16, as treated in our Galatians series). James asks a different question about a different deliverance. The Free Grace reader does not deny that works matter enormously; he denies that they are the condition or the proof of possessing eternal life.

Honesty Note: The majority of commentators (Moo, McKnight, Davids, Johnson, among others) take σῶσαι in v. 14 as eschatological salvation in the full sense — deliverance from final condemnation — and read James's point as: a faith that produces no works is not saving faith. They note that σῴζω in the NT most often carries this sense, that 2:12–13 speaks of final judgment, and that the anaphoric article ("that faith") implies a kind of faith distinct from true faith. The Free Grace response is (1) that James's own uses of σῴζω (1:21; 5:15, 20) are addressed to believers and concern deliverance from death and judgment consequences rather than regeneration; (2) that the anaphoric article identifies faith without works, not a counterfeit faith — James never says this man's faith is not genuine; and (3) that the "kind of faith" reading requires assurance of eternal life to rest on an assessment of one's works, which neither James (3:2) nor Paul permits. The student should note that the decision here largely determines how the rest of the passage is read.

The illustration is carefully chosen. The needy people are ἀδελφὸς ἢ ἀδελφή — fellow believers, not strangers — which heightens the obligation (cf. Gal 6:10, "especially to those who are of the household of faith"). They are γυμνοί (inadequately clothed) and λειπόμενοι τῆς ἐφημέρου τροφῆς, lacking even a single day's food. The participle λειπόμενοι repeats the verb of 1:4–5: the mature believer is "lacking nothing," and the believer who lacks wisdom may ask God, who gives generously. Here the brother lacks food, and the question is whether the believer will be the channel of that same generous giving.

The scene also continues 2:1–13. There the poor man was dishonored in the assembly; here the poor brother is dismissed with words. In both cases the failure is a failure of mercy — precisely the failure that 2:13 said would meet merciless judgment.

The speaker offers three imperatives: Ὑπάγετε ἐν εἰρήνῃ, θερμαίνεσθε καὶ χορτάζεσθε. The first is a pious Jewish farewell; the second and third may be middle ("warm yourselves, feed yourselves") — a dismissal that places the burden back on the destitute. Whatever the voice, the words cost nothing. The shift from the singular εἴπῃ τις to the plural μὴ δῶτε implicates the whole community. The contrast with God in 1:5 is stark: God is ὁ διδοὺς πᾶσιν ἁπλῶς; the congregation μὴ δῶτε.

The apodosis τί τὸ ὄφελος; closes the inclusio with v. 14. The parallel is exact: words of blessing without provision are useless to the brother; faith without works is useless to the believer. The analogy concerns usefulness. The speaker's words are real words; they are simply unproductive. Nothing in the illustration suggests that the words were counterfeit — only that they accomplished nothing. That is the point James will now apply to faith.

James applies the illustration: οὕτως καὶ ἡ πίστις … νεκρά ἐστιν καθ᾽ ἑαυτήν. Faith that has no works is νεκρά — dead. What does James mean by "dead"? The adjective νεκρός does not mean "nonexistent." A corpse is a real body; it is simply without life and therefore without function. Paul can say that apart from the law "sin was dead" (Rom 7:8) — not nonexistent, but inactive. The illustration James has just given confirms the sense: the words spoken to the destitute brother were real words that did nothing. So faith without works is real faith that accomplishes nothing in the sphere James is discussing.

The phrase καθ᾽ ἑαυτήν ("in itself," "by itself") reinforces this. Faith considered in isolation from works has no life-giving effect on the believer's experience or on others. Hodges (Dead Faith: What Is It?) notes that James's closing analogy in v. 26 — the body without the spirit — requires a body that was alive. A corpse is not a body that never lived; it is a body whose life has departed. On this reading, James warns that a believer's faith, if not expressed in works, can become lifeless and useless — a sobering pastoral warning rather than a test of whether one was ever regenerate.

Honesty Note: The majority reading (Moo, McKnight, Davids) takes "dead faith" as a way of saying "not genuine faith" — a mere intellectual assent that was never saving. This reading notes that James describes the man only as saying he has faith (v. 14), that v. 19 compares such faith to that of demons, and that "dead" in Scripture can describe what lacks true spiritual life (Eph 2:1). The Free Grace reply is that (1) the claim in v. 14 is not called false, (2) the illustration of vv. 15–16 involves real but useless words, (3) the analogy of v. 26 presupposes something once alive, and (4) James never uses "not genuine" language when he easily could have. The distinction is significant: on the majority reading, the passage is a test of salvation; on the Free Grace reading, it is a warning to believers about fruitlessness.

James 2:18 — The Objector

James 2:18 (Byzantine Majority Text)
Ἀλλ᾽ ἐρεῖ τις, Σὺ πίστιν ἔχεις, κἀγὼ ἔργα ἔχω. Δεῖξόν μοι τὴν πίστιν σου ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου, κἀγὼ δείξω σοι ἐκ τῶν ἔργων μου τὴν πίστιν μου.
Yes, a man will say, "You have faith, and I have works." Show me your faith without works, and I by my works will show you my faith.
⚠ Translation Note: The WEB here follows the critical text ("Show me your faith without works"), while the Greek line above is the Byzantine reading ("Show me your faith from your works"). The WEB is reproduced verbatim per series format; the Byzantine sense is: "Show me your faith from your works, and I will show you my faith from my works."
Textual Note — ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου vs. χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων (the most significant variant in James 2): (a) Byzantine: Δεῖξόν μοι τὴν πίστιν σου ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου, κἀγὼ δείξω σοι ἐκ τῶν ἔργων μου τὴν πίστιν μου. (b) NA28/UBS5: δεῖξόν μοι τὴν πίστιν σου χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων, κἀγώ σοι δείξω ἐκ τῶν ἔργων μου τὴν πίστιν — χωρίς ("without") instead of ἐκ ("from"), no second σου, different order (σοι δείξω), and no final μου. (c) Witnesses: χωρίς is read by the leading Alexandrian witnesses and a broad early spread; ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου by the majority of later witnesses (𝔐). NA28 regards χωρίς as original and ἐκ as an assimilation to the second half of the sentence. (d) Significance: high, because the variant determines how far the objector's words extend. With χωρίς, the challenge "show me your faith without works" is naturally James's rebuttal of the objector. With ἐκ, the sentence is a symmetrical pair ("show me your faith from your works, and I will show you my faith from my works") that fits comfortably as a continuation of the objector's own speech — which is the reading Hodges defends (see exegesis). The Byzantine reading therefore opens an interpretive option that the critical text makes much harder.

Verse 18 is widely acknowledged as the most difficult verse in the letter. Dibelius called it one of the most difficult passages in the New Testament. The problem is identifying who says what. The formula Ἀλλ᾽ ἐρεῖ τις introduces an objector, as in diatribe style (cf. Rom 9:19; 1 Cor 15:35). But the words that follow — "You have faith, and I have works" — sound more like James's own position than an objection to it. Commentators have offered several solutions: (1) the objector is an ally of James (Mayor, some others), which makes Ἀλλ᾽ awkward; (2) "you" and "I" are generic ("one person has faith, another has works"), so the objector argues that faith and works are separate gifts, like the charismata (Dibelius, Ropes, and many moderns); (3) the objector's words are limited to a brief statement and the rest is James's reply (the usual reading of the critical text).

The Byzantine reading opens a fourth, and simpler, solution defended by Zane Hodges. With ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου, the objector's speech is a balanced, sarcastic challenge addressed to James: "You [James] have faith, and I have works. Show me your faith from your works, and I will show you my faith from my works." The point of the objection is that faith and works are not linked — that one cannot read faith off from works, because the connection James claims does not exist. Any works James can point to, the objector can match; works prove nothing about faith. On Hodges's reading the objector continues in v. 19 with a supporting argument (see below), and James's own rebuttal begins at v. 20 with Θέλεις δὲ γνῶναι, ὦ ἄνθρωπε κενέ.

This reading has three advantages: it gives Ἀλλ᾽ its natural adversative force; it explains why the objector's speech sounds symmetrical (because it is); and it removes the need to treat "show me your faith by your works" as a principle that one can verify another's salvation by examining his works — a principle that would sit uneasily with James's own confession that "we all stumble in many things" (3:2). It also keeps the focus where 2:14 put it: the usefulness of faith, not the verification of it.

Honesty Note: Most modern commentators follow the critical text (χωρίς) and read the second half of v. 18 as James's reply: "Show me your faith apart from works (you can't!), and I will show you my faith by my works." On this reading, works are the only visible demonstration of faith, and many (e.g., Moo) draw the corollary that faith without works cannot be shown to exist. The external evidence for χωρίς is strong and early, and NA28 explains ἐκ as a scribal assimilation to the second clause. A Byzantine-priority reader should acknowledge that the Hodges reading depends heavily on the Byzantine text and on extending the objector's speech through v. 19 — a punctuation decision the manuscripts do not settle. The Free Grace position does not stand or fall on this verse; even on the critical text, "show me" concerns what can be demonstrated to another person, which is justification before men rather than before God (see vv. 21–25).

The content of belief in v. 19 is specific: ὅτι ὁ θεὸς εἷς ἐστίν — the Shema of Deuteronomy 6:4, the confession of Jewish monotheism. It is not faith in Jesus Christ for eternal life (cf. John 3:16; 6:47; 20:31). The demons, too, hold this conviction, and it produces in them not good works but φρίσσουσιν — shuddering terror.

On the majority reading, James is speaking: even demons have orthodox belief, so mere belief is worthless; faith that does not issue in works is no better than demonic belief. On Hodges's reading, the objector is still speaking, continuing his argument from v. 18: "You believe that God is one — fine. The demons believe that too, and they shudder." That is, the same belief produces entirely different effects in different beings; there is no necessary connection between a belief and a particular kind of work. Demons believe and tremble; men believe and do good (or don't). Hence, the objector argues, James cannot claim that faith and works are linked. James's reply in v. 20 then addresses the objector as ὦ ἄνθρωπε κενέ.

Whichever reading is adopted, the demons' "faith" cannot be used as a model of saving faith that failed to produce works. Demons are not candidates for redemption (Heb 2:16), and what they believe is a truth about God, not the promise of eternal life to the one who believes in Christ. The Free Grace position insists on this distinction: saving faith is belief in Jesus's promise of eternal life (John 11:25–27), not belief in monotheism.

With Θέλεις δὲ γνῶναι James turns decisively on the objector. The proof James offers next is not an argument that dead faith is fake faith, but a demonstration from Abraham and Rahab of what faith accomplishes when it works.

James's first proof is Abraham, ὁ πατὴρ ἡμῶν — the same figure Paul uses in Romans 4 and Galatians 3 to prove justification by faith apart from works. The juxtaposition is deliberate, and the contrast between the two apostles must be handled precisely. James says Abraham was ἐξ ἔργων ἐδικαιώθη when he offered Isaac (Gen 22). Paul says Abraham was justified by faith when he believed God's promise (Gen 15:6), long before Isaac was born and before he was circumcised (Rom 4:10). The two events are separated by decades.

The verb δικαιόω has a range of meaning: "declare righteous" (forensic, Rom 3:24–28), but also "vindicate," "show to be right," or "prove righteous" (e.g., Luke 7:29, 35 — "wisdom is justified by her children"; Matt 11:19; 1 Tim 3:16). Hodges argues that in James the justification in view is a vindication before men — note the "show me" language of v. 18 and "you see" in vv. 22, 24 — in contrast to Paul's justification before God (Rom 4:2, "if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God"). Paul himself allows that there is a kind of justification by works — one that brings boasting before others — but denies that it avails before God. James is talking about that very kind.

Series continuity matters here. The offering of Isaac is introduced in Genesis 22:1 LXX with the words ὁ θεὸς ἐπείραζεν τὸν Ἀβραάμ — "God tested Abraham." Abraham's supreme act of works was his response to a πειρασμός. James 1:2–4, 12 promised that the testing of faith produces endurance and maturity, and that the one who endures trial will be δόκιμος. Abraham is the paradigm of that promise: his faith was tested, it expressed itself in works, and he was approved.

Verse 22 explains the relationship between faith and works in Abraham's case. Two clauses do the work. First, ἡ πίστις συνήργει τοῖς ἔργοις αὐτοῦ — faith "was co-working" with his works. The imperfect pictures an ongoing partnership, and the compound συν-εργ- puns on ἔργοις. Faith is the prior reality; works are its partners. Second, ἐκ τῶν ἔργων ἡ πίστις ἐτελειώθη — "by works faith was brought to maturity."

The verb τελειόω is the verbal cognate of τέλειος, the key term of 1:4: "let endurance have its perfect work, that you may be perfect (τέλειοι) and complete." This is one of the clearest threads in the letter. Abraham's faith did not begin at Mount Moriah; it was already present (Gen 15:6). What happened at Moriah is that tested faith, working together with obedience, reached its τέλος. This fits the Free Grace reading exactly: works do not create or prove the existence of faith; they bring an existing faith to maturity. It also answers the objector of vv. 18–19, who claimed that faith and works have no connection. James's reply: in Abraham, they were co-workers, and the works completed the faith.

James now quotes the very verse Paul uses: Ἐπίστευσεν δὲ Ἀβραὰμ τῷ θεῷ, καὶ ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην (Gen 15:6). This is decisive for understanding James. He does not deny that Abraham was credited with righteousness by faith; he quotes it as Scripture. What he adds is that this Scripture ἐπληρώθη — was "fulfilled" — when Abraham offered Isaac. The word "fulfilled" does not mean that Gen 15:6 was incomplete or provisional. It means the earlier declaration found its full expression and public vindication in the later obedience. Abraham was reckoned righteous by faith in Genesis 15; he was shown to be righteous by works in Genesis 22.

The result of this vindication was a title: φίλος θεοῦ ἐκλήθη — "he was called the friend of God" (cf. Isa 41:8; 2 Chr 20:7). This is significant for Free Grace theology. Friendship with God is not identical with possessing eternal life. Jesus distinguishes the two: "You are my friends if you do whatever I command you" (John 15:14), spoken to disciples already clean through His word (John 15:3). Abraham's works did not make him a child of God; they made him a friend of God — a relationship of intimacy and honor that obedience deepens. James will return to friendship language in 4:4: "friendship with the world is enmity with God." The double-minded believer (1:8; 4:8) forfeits the intimacy Abraham enjoyed.

Verse 24 is the verse Luther found most difficult and Trent cited most readily. The grammar deserves close attention. μόνον is an adverb, not an adjective agreeing with πίστεως. James does not say "not by faith alone" (which would require πίστεως μόνης), as though denying the Reformation formula. He says a person is justified by works "and not only by faith" — that is, there is a justification by faith, and there is also a justification by works. The adverb presupposes the reality of justification by faith. This matches vv. 21–23 exactly: Abraham was justified by faith (Gen 15:6), and later he was justified by works (Gen 22). Two events, two justifications.

The Free Grace understanding, following Hodges, is that these are justifications in two different spheres. Justification by faith is God's declaration that the believer is righteous — the gift of Romans 4 and Galatians 2–3 that brings eternal life and cannot be lost. Justification by works is the vindication of the believer before others — and, in the light of 2:12–13, the approval he receives at the Judgment Seat — when his faith is seen to be working. Paul's statement in Romans 4:2 anticipates exactly this distinction: justification by works gives grounds for boasting, "but not before God." The shift to Ὁρᾶτε (plural) and the emphasis on what can be "seen" both suggest the human, observable sphere.

Honesty Note: Several major positions compete here. (1) Reformed/evangelical majority (Moo, and many others): δικαιόω in James means God's eschatological verdict (or its demonstration), and works are the necessary evidence of the faith that justifies; James and Paul use "faith" and "works" differently (Paul: works of the law as a ground; James: works as the fruit of faith). Moo in particular argues that "justify" in James refers to the final judgment rather than to vindication before men. (2) Roman Catholic (Council of Trent, Session VI, ch. 10, citing Jas 2:24): justification, once received, is increased through good works. (3) Free Grace (Hodges, Wilkin): justification by faith before God and justification by works before men (and in the believer's evaluation) are distinct. The student should note that position (1) must explain why James uses the same verb and the same Genesis text as Paul yet means "evidence" rather than "declaration," while position (3) must explain why James could quote Gen 15:6 — a justification before God — as "fulfilled" by a justification before men. Hodges's answer is that the declaration of Gen 15:6 was invisible, and its visible fulfilment came through works.

James's second example is deliberately chosen as the opposite of Abraham in every respect: a Gentile rather than the patriarch of Israel, a woman rather than a man, a prostitute rather than the friend of God. The formula is identical — οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων ἐδικαιώθη — which shows that the principle is not limited to great saints. The works in view are specific: she welcomed (ὑποδεξαμένη) the messengers and sent them out by another route (ἑτέρᾳ ὁδῷ ἐκβαλοῦσα), at great personal risk (Josh 2).

As with Abraham, Rahab's faith preceded her works. She confessed, "the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above and on earth beneath" (Josh 2:11), and Hebrews 11:31 attributes her deliverance to faith: πίστει Ῥαὰβ ἡ πόρνη οὐ συναπώλετο. Her works were the expression of a faith already present. And notice what her works accomplished: they saved her life and her family's from the destruction of Jericho (Josh 6:22–25). This is exactly the kind of deliverance that σῴζω in 2:14 denotes on the Free Grace reading — rescue from temporal destruction and judgment. Rahab's case thus illustrates the answer to the question of 2:14: faith that works does save — in the sense James intends.

The paragraph closes with the refrain for the third time, now grounded in an analogy: ὥσπερ … τὸ σῶμα χωρὶς πνεύματος νεκρόν ἐστιν, οὕτως καὶ ἡ πίστις χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων νεκρά ἐστιν. The analogy is surprising at first. We might expect faith to be compared to the spirit (the inner reality) and works to the body (the outward expression). James inverts this: faith is the body, and works are the spirit — the animating breath that makes faith alive and active.

The analogy strongly supports the Free Grace reading of "dead." A dead body is a real body. Its death does not prove that it was never a body, nor that it never lived; on the contrary, only a body that once had breath can be called a corpse. Hodges draws the obvious conclusion: dead faith is real faith that has become inactive and unproductive for lack of works. The warning is addressed to brothers (v. 14) whose faith, though genuine, has ceased to be vital. Such faith cannot deliver them from the merciless judgment of 2:13, cannot help the destitute brother of 2:15–16, and cannot bring them the vindication and friendship with God that Abraham and Rahab enjoyed.

The body–spirit analogy also answers the claim that James regards faith and works as a single reality. Body and spirit are distinct; one can exist without the other — but not alive. James's call, then, is not "prove you are saved," but "breathe life into your faith." That is a call a believer can obey.

Overall Exegesis: James 2:14–26

This section surveys the architecture of the passage, its principal theological themes, its reception history, and the textual situation. Because this is the most debated passage in the letter, the major alternative readings are summarized alongside the Free Grace interpretation.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

James 2:14–26 is a diatribe — a form of argument that uses rhetorical questions, an imaginary interlocutor, sharp address, and exempla. The structure is clear:

(A) Thesis question (v. 14): What good is faith without works? Can that faith save? (B) Illustration (vv. 15–16): the destitute brother or sister dismissed with words, closing with the same question, τί τὸ ὄφελος; (inclusio with v. 14). (C) First conclusion (v. 17): faith without works is dead. (D) Objection (vv. 18–19): an interlocutor challenges the connection between faith and works — on Hodges's reading of the Byzantine text, through the end of v. 19. (E) Rebuttal and second conclusion (v. 20): "O empty man … faith apart from works is dead." (F) Scriptural exempla (vv. 21–25): Abraham (vv. 21–23) with an inference (v. 24), and Rahab (v. 25), both with the formula οὐκ ἐξ ἔργων ἐδικαιώθη. (G) Third conclusion with analogy (v. 26): body without spirit.

The Byzantine text gives the passage a precise triple refrain — νεκρά ἐστιν in vv. 17, 20, and 26, with χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων identical in vv. 20 and 26. Other binding devices include the ὄφελος inclusio (vv. 14, 16), the "seeing/showing" vocabulary (δεῖξον, δείξω v. 18; βλέπεις v. 22; ὁρᾶτε v. 24), and the ἐργ- wordplay (συνήργει τοῖς ἔργοις, v. 22; in the critical text, ἀργή, v. 20).

II. Key Theological Themes

Theme 1: What "Save" Means in James

The interpretation of the whole passage turns on σῶσαι in v. 14. James's other uses of σῴζω (1:21; 4:12; 5:15, 20) consistently concern believers and deliverance from the consequences of sin — death (1:15; 5:20), sickness (5:15), and judgment (4:12). The immediately preceding verses (2:12–13) announce a merciless judgment for believers who show no mercy. Read in that context, the question "Can that faith save him?" asks whether faith without works of mercy can deliver a believer from that judgment. The answer is no. This reading preserves the full force of James's warning while leaving Paul's doctrine of justification by faith alone, apart from works, untouched. The majority reading takes σῶσαι as final salvation and concludes that faith without works is not saving faith; this document has presented that reading in the honesty note under v. 14.

Theme 2: The Nature of "Dead" Faith

James never calls the faith of v. 14 false, spurious, or counterfeit. He calls it νεκρά. The analogies he uses — words without provision (vv. 15–16) and a body without breath (v. 26) — describe realities that exist but do not function. The critical reading ἀργή ("useless") in v. 20, whether original or not, shows that early readers understood deadness as uselessness. The Free Grace conclusion is that dead faith is genuine but inactive faith in a believer, and the passage is a warning against spiritual fruitlessness rather than a test of regeneration.

Theme 3: The Objector and the Byzantine Text

Verse 18 is where text-critical choices most directly shape exegesis. The Byzantine ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου makes possible Hodges's reading, in which the objector argues (vv. 18–19) that faith and works have no necessary connection — the same belief makes demons shudder but may make men act differently — and James refutes him (vv. 20–26) by showing from Scripture that in Abraham faith and works "co-worked" and faith was "perfected." The critical χωρίς makes v. 18b James's own challenge. The student should recognize that this is a genuine case where Byzantine priority affects interpretation, and should be able to state the external evidence on both sides honestly.

Theme 4: Justification in James and Paul

James and Paul both quote Genesis 15:6 and both appeal to Abraham. Paul: Abraham was justified by faith apart from works (Rom 4:1–5; Gal 3:6). James: Abraham was justified by works when he offered Isaac, and thereby Gen 15:6 was fulfilled (Jas 2:21–23). The two statements refer to different events in Abraham's life separated by decades. James's adverb μόνον (v. 24) presupposes justification by faith and adds a second justification by works. Free Grace interpreters locate the two in different spheres: before God (by faith) and before men and at the evaluation of believers (by works). Paul himself anticipates this distinction in Romans 4:2. James's use of "works" also differs from Paul's: James speaks of works of mercy and obedience flowing from faith (2:15–16, 21, 25), not "works of the law" as a means of acceptance, which Paul opposes (Gal 2:16; 3:10, as treated in our Galatians series).

Theme 5: Abraham's Tested Faith and Friendship with God

Abraham's justification by works came through a πειρασμός (Gen 22:1 LXX, ἐπείραζεν). This links the passage directly to James 1:2–4, 12: trials test faith, endurance does its perfect work, and the approved believer receives the crown of life. Abraham's faith "was perfected" (ἐτελειώθη, 2:22) — the verbal form of the τέλειος maturity of 1:4. The outcome was a title of honor: "friend of God." Friendship with God is not the same as possessing eternal life; it is an intimacy that obedience cultivates (John 15:14) and that worldliness forfeits (Jas 4:4). The Abraham exemplum thus binds together James's theology of trials (ch. 1), mercy (ch. 2), and the call to single-minded devotion (ch. 4).

Theme 6: Works of Mercy

It is easy to discuss James 2:14–26 abstractly and forget that James's first example of "works" is feeding and clothing a destitute brother or sister (vv. 15–16). The passage continues 2:1–13, where mercy triumphs over judgment. For James, the works that make faith alive are concrete acts of love toward the needy, and the danger he addresses is a faith that speaks pious words and does nothing. Any reading of the passage — Free Grace or otherwise — that loses this pastoral edge has missed James's purpose.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance; Reception

In the patristic period, James 2 was generally read harmoniously with Paul. Augustine's On Faith and Works (De fide et operibus) addresses James 2 in opposing the view that baptized believers may live as they please and still be saved; he distinguishes Paul's works preceding faith from James's works following faith. In the Reformation, Luther's 1522 preface to the New Testament called James "an epistle of straw" compared to the chief books, largely because of 2:14–26, though he did not remove it from his Bible. The Council of Trent (Session VI, ch. 10) cited James 2:24 in support of the increase of justification through good works. Calvin, by contrast, argued that James uses "justify" in the sense of declaring or demonstrating righteousness before men — an early form of the "demonstrative" interpretation — while insisting that faith which is genuine will produce works.

The Free Grace interpretation developed in the twentieth century, most fully in Zane Hodges's The Gospel Under Siege, Dead Faith: What Is It?, and his James commentary, and in the work of Robert Wilkin and Joseph Dillow. Its distinctive contributions are (1) reading σῶσαι in the light of James's own usage and the judgment context of 2:12–13; (2) reading "dead" as inactive rather than nonexistent; (3) the objector reading of vv. 18–19 supported by the Byzantine text; and (4) distinguishing the spheres of justification. Critics argue that the approach underplays the strong warning language and the NT's general expectation that faith bears fruit; proponents respond that it takes James's warnings more seriously by addressing them to real believers.

IV. Textual Notes: Byzantine vs. NA28/UBS5

James 2:14–26 contains the most exegetically significant Byzantine/critical divergences in the letter so far:

v. 15 — ἐὰν δέ (Byz) vs. ἐάν (NA28); ὦσιν (Byz) vs. [ὦσιν] bracketed (NA28): minor. v. 18 — ἐκ τῶν ἔργων σου … δείξω σοι … τὴν πίστιν μου (Byz) vs. χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων … σοι δείξω … τὴν πίστιν (NA28): major — affects the extent of the objector's speech and the meaning of the verse. v. 19 — ὁ θεὸς εἷς ἐστίν (Byz) vs. εἷς ἐστιν ὁ θεός (NA28): word order, with several orders attested. v. 20 — νεκρά (Byz, with א, A, and C corrector) vs. ἀργή (NA28, with B, C*): moderate — the Byzantine reading here has significant early support, and the variant bears on the meaning of "dead." v. 24 — τοίνυν (Byz) vs. omission (NA28): minor, makes the inference explicit. v. 26 — χωρὶς τῶν ἔργων (Byz) vs. χωρὶς ἔργων (NA28): article only.

The pattern matches the previous documents: the Byzantine text is fuller and more explicit, and the Alexandrian text shorter. Verse 20 is a useful counterexample to any assumption that the Byzantine reading is always late — its reading νεκρά is shared by Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus. The reader is encouraged to verify individual sigla against the NA28 apparatus and the Robinson–Pierpont and Hodges–Farstad editions before citing them in teaching.
