Introduction to Galatians 2:11–21

The biographical argument ends in a confrontation. Peter, who had been eating freely with Gentile believers at Antioch, withdrew when men came from James, and the rest of the Jewish believers — Barnabas included — followed him. Paul opposed him publicly, and the rebuke of v. 14 opens out into the letter's first full statement of its thesis: a person is not justified by works of law but through faith in Jesus Christ. Where the speech to Peter ends and Paul's address to the Galatians resumes is not marked in the Greek, and the question is a real one; what is not in doubt is that vv. 15–21 state the doctrine the rest of the letter will defend.

The paragraph break is sharp. Paul has just described the Jerusalem leaders extending the right hand of fellowship; now he describes opposing one of them publicly. Both belong to his argument: he is neither Jerusalem's subordinate nor its opponent, and the Antioch episode proves the first point as firmly as 2:6–10 proved the second.

κατὰ πρόσωπον αὐτῷ ἀντέστην — "I opposed him to his face." The aorist marks one decisive act. Paul is not describing a feud but a single public correction, and he reports it without embarrassment because the principle at stake was the gospel.

κατεγνωσμένος ἦν is a periphrastic construction with a perfect participle: he stood condemned, already, by what he had done. Paul does not say he condemned Peter; he says the conduct carried its own verdict.

The Byzantine text reads Πέτρος here and in v. 14, where NA28 has Κηφᾶς. Paul uses both names for the same man within this chapter, so nothing turns on the variant.

The three imperfects tell the story: συνήσθιεν (he was eating with them), then ὑπέστελλεν and ἀφώριζεν (he was drawing back, separating himself). The first describes Peter's settled practice; the last two describe a withdrawal unfolding over time rather than a single decision.

Table fellowship is the issue, and it was not a matter of etiquette. To eat with Gentile believers was to treat them as full members of the people of God without circumcision. To withdraw was to say, in the most public way available, that they were not.

ὑποστέλλω is used of a soldier shrinking back from the line, and ἀφώριζεν is the same verb Paul used in 1:15 of God setting him apart for the gospel — here turned to the opposite purpose, separating himself from brothers.

The motive is stated plainly: φοβούμενος τοὺς ἐκ περιτομῆς. Peter did not change his convictions; he acted against them out of fear. That is precisely what makes the next verse's word for it the right one.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads [καὶ] οἱ, the Byzantine text has καὶ οἱ.

συνυπεκρίθησαν — they joined in the play-acting. The verb is from the stage, and Paul's charge is specific: Peter and those who followed him were acting a part that did not match what they believed. He does not accuse them of false doctrine. He accuses them of conduct at odds with the doctrine they held.

The compound in συν- appears twice in the verse, and συναπήχθη is passive: Barnabas was carried away. Paul's own partner, the man who had vouched for him in Jerusalem and travelled with him to Galatia, was swept along. Naming him costs Paul something, and the detail measures how fast the damage spread.

The lesson Paul wants the Galatians to draw is about the force of example. If Peter's withdrawal could carry Barnabas, the agitators' pressure can carry them.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads Κηφᾷ, the Byzantine text has Πέτρῳ. Where NA28 reads καὶ οὐχὶ Ἰουδαϊκῶς ζῇς πῶς, the Byzantine text has ζῇς καὶ οὐκ Ἰουδαϊκῶς τί.

οὐκ ὀρθοποδοῦσιν — "they are not walking straight." The verb is rare enough that Paul may have coined it, and the image is of a path from which one can veer. The standard is πρὸς τὴν ἀλήθειαν τοῦ εὐαγγελίου, the same phrase as 2:5: the gospel has a truth that conduct can either match or contradict.

The rebuke was ἔμπροσθεν πάντων, before everyone, because the offence had been public and its effect on the Gentile believers was public.

The charge itself is a first-class condition, assuming the protasis true: Εἰ σύ, Ἰουδαῖος ὑπάρχων, ἐθνικῶς ζῇς. Peter had been living like a Gentile at Antioch — that was his own practice, adopted freely. The inconsistency is the lever.

The decisive word is ἀναγκάζεις, the same verb as 2:3. In Jerusalem no one compelled Titus to be circumcised; at Antioch Peter's withdrawal compelled Gentiles to Judaize — not by issuing a rule but by making Jewish practice the price of full fellowship. Paul's point is that pressure of that kind is compulsion whatever its form, and that it reverses what Jerusalem itself had conceded.

Ἰουδαΐζειν means to adopt a Jewish manner of life. It is the verb from which the agitators get their usual label, and this is its only occurrence in the New Testament.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 2:15
SUBJECT OF THE LONG SENTENCE RUNNING THROUGH v. 16 Ἡμεῖς [EMPHATIC SUBJECT — "we"] φύσει Ἰουδαῖοι ["Jews by nature"] καὶ οὐκ ἐξ ἐθνῶν ἁμαρτωλοί ["and not sinners from among the Gentiles"] [Paul quotes the conventional distinction — and v. 16 dissolves it]

Where the speech to Peter ends is one of the genuine open questions in Galatians. The Greek has no quotation marks, and interpreters have ended it at v. 14, v. 16 or v. 21. What is clear is that vv. 15–21 flow directly out of the rebuke, so that the argument addressed to Peter becomes the argument addressed to the Galatians without a seam.

Ἡμεῖς φύσει Ἰουδαῖοι — "we who are Jews by nature" — states the distinction as Peter and Paul had both inherited it. ἐξ ἐθνῶν ἁμαρτωλοί is the conventional Jewish designation for Gentiles: outside the law, and therefore in the category of sinners.

Paul concedes the category only to dissolve it. The whole weight of v. 16 falls on the fact that these Jews by birth, with every advantage the distinction implies, had to be justified by exactly the same means as the Gentile "sinners" they were distancing themselves from.

This is the letter's thesis, and the structure is worth seeing whole: the principle (no one is justified by works of law, but through faith), the personal case (so we too believed), and the scriptural ground (Ps 143:2). The phrase ἐξ ἔργων νόμου appears three times in one verse, each time negated.

δικαιόω is forensic — to declare righteous, to pronounce a favourable verdict — not to make inwardly good. The setting is a courtroom, and the question is what secures the verdict.

The Byzantine text reads ἐὰν μὴ διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, "except through faith in Jesus Christ." Taken woodenly, ἐὰν μή could suggest that faith is an exception within works of law rather than an alternative to them — and some have read it that way. The rest of the verse rules that out: Paul immediately restates the contrast twice more, with ἐκ πίστεως set against ἐξ ἔργων νόμου. The construction is exceptive in form but adversative in force.

πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ is the most discussed genitive in Paul. Read as an objective genitive it means faith in Christ — Christ is the object believed on. Read as a subjective genitive it means the faithfulness of Christ — his own obedience as the ground of justification. The decisive consideration here is the middle clause: Paul says καὶ ἡμεῖς εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν ἐπιστεύσαμεν, "we too believed in Christ Jesus," using the verb with εἰς and naming Christ as the object. If the genitives meant Christ's own faithfulness, that clause would be an odd thing to place between them. The objective reading — faith directed to Christ as its object — fits the argument Paul is making, which is about how a person comes to be justified.

The closing allusion to Psalm 143:2 generalises the point as far as it will go: οὐ δικαιωθήσεται ἐξ ἔργων νόμου πᾶσα σάρξ. No human being is justified this way. The Byzantine word order places the negation before the prepositional phrase where NA28 reverses them; the sense is the same.

The objection Paul anticipates runs like this: if Jews must be justified in Christ by faith exactly as Gentiles are, then the law-observant Jew is, in the terms of v. 15, found to be a "sinner" after all — and if that is what seeking justification in Christ reveals, has Christ made us sinners? Μὴ γένοιτο is Paul's strongest rejection, an optative that occurs fourteen times in his letters. The answer is not argued here; it is refused, and v. 18 supplies the reason.

The logic turns on what actually creates the transgression. Being found a sinner by the gospel is a disclosure, not a corruption. Christ reveals the condition; he does not produce it.

διὰ νόμου νόμῳ ἀπέθανον compresses a large argument into four words: through the law I died to the law. The law itself was the instrument. Its curse fell on Christ (3:13), and those joined to him died under it — so the law's own operation is what ended its jurisdiction over them.

Both datives are datives of reference. Paul died with respect to one sphere in order to live with respect to another: ἵνα θεῷ ζήσω. The purpose of the death is life to God, which forestalls any suggestion that freedom from law means freedom from God.

This is also Paul's answer to the charge that his gospel licenses sin, a charge the next chapters will keep answering.

ὃ δὲ νῦν ζῶ ἐν σαρκί keeps the statement earthbound. The life in question is the ordinary bodily life he is living at that moment, and it is lived ἐν πίστει — in the sphere of faith.

Οὐκ ἀθετῶ τὴν χάριν τοῦ θεοῦ answers a charge rather than making one. Paul's opponents evidently held that his law-free gospel set God's gift aside. He replies that the opposite is true, and the conditional that follows shows why.

The condition is contrary to fact: εἰ γὰρ διὰ νόμου δικαιοσύνη, ἄρα Χριστὸς δωρεὰν ἀπέθανεν. If righteousness were available through law, the cross was pointless.

δωρεάν is the sharpest word in the verse. It can mean "as a gift, freely," but here it carries its other sense — without cause, to no purpose, for nothing. Paul has reduced the agitators' position to a single intolerable consequence, and he leaves it standing without further comment.

The verse closes the biographical half of the letter and sets up chapter 3. Having stated that justification is by faith and that any law-righteousness would empty the cross, Paul turns in 3:1 to prove it from the Galatians' own experience of the Spirit.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 2:11–21

Passage Summary: Galatians 2:11–21 narrates Paul's public rebuke of Peter at Antioch and then states the letter's thesis. Peter's withdrawal from table fellowship was not a lapse of manners but a contradiction of the gospel, since it compelled Gentiles to live as Jews in order to be fully received. From that charge Paul moves to the positive statement — justification comes through faith in Jesus Christ and not by works of law — and closes with the crucified-with-Christ confession of v. 20 and the stark conclusion of v. 21: if righteousness comes through law, Christ died for nothing.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

A. The narrative (vv. 11–14). Paul completes the biographical argument of chapters 1–2 with an episode in which he, not Jerusalem, is the one correcting. The sequence is tightly told: Peter's practice (imperfect, v. 12a), the arrival of the men from James, the withdrawal (two imperfects, v. 12b), the contagion reaching Barnabas (v. 13), and the public rebuke (v. 14).

B. The hinge. The verb ἀναγκάζω binds the chapter together. In v. 3, Jerusalem did not compel Titus to be circumcised; in v. 14, Peter's withdrawal compels Gentiles to Judaize. Paul's charge is that conduct can impose what doctrine declined to require.

C. The thesis (vv. 15–21). The speech to Peter opens out — without any seam in the Greek — into the letter's doctrinal statement. Verse 16 states the principle three times over; vv. 17–18 dispose of the obvious objection; vv. 19–20 give the personal and christological ground; v. 21 reduces the alternative to absurdity. Where Paul stops addressing Peter and starts addressing the Galatians is genuinely unmarked, and that is probably deliberate: the same argument answers both.

D. The bridge to chapter 3. Verse 21 ends on the cross emptied of purpose; 3:1 opens with Christ publicly placarded as crucified. The join is not accidental.

II. Key Theological Themes

1. Justification is forensic and by faith (v. 16). δικαιόω is a verdict, not a transformation, and the verse denies ἐξ ἔργων νόμου three times while affirming faith in Christ as the means. The citation of Psalm 143:2 extends the denial to πᾶσα σάρξ — every human being, Jew and Gentile alike. This is the foundation on which the whole letter rests, and it is stated before any argument is offered for it.

2. The πίστις Χριστοῦ question. The genitive in vv. 16 and 20 can be read as objective (faith in Christ) or subjective (the faithfulness of Christ). The intervening clause in v. 16 — "we too believed in Christ Jesus," with the verb and εἰς — is the strongest internal reason for the objective reading, and it keeps the verse answering the question actually at issue: how a person comes to be justified. Readers should know the subjective reading is seriously defended; the exegesis here follows the objective.

3. Conduct can contradict doctrine (vv. 11–14). Paul never accuses Peter of believing wrongly. The charge is ὑπόκρισις — a practice at odds with a conviction — and Paul treats that as a gospel matter serious enough for public rebuke. The truth of the gospel is something one can walk crookedly toward (οὐκ ὀρθοποδοῦσιν) while holding it in principle.

4. Union with Christ (v. 20). συνεσταύρωμαι — perfect passive — places the believer's death in Christ's, as an accomplished fact with abiding effect. The Christian life that follows is not an attempt to achieve something but the living out of a life already given, "in faith," in the body, now.

5. Grace nullified by law-righteousness (v. 21). Paul's conclusion is not that law-keeping is unhelpful but that it is incompatible: if righteousness came that way, Christ died δωρεάν, for nothing. Adding any requirement to faith does not supplement the cross; it empties it. That is why the letter treats the agitators' programme as a different gospel rather than a stricter version of the same one.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

The Antioch incident is among the most historically valuable passages in the New Testament, because Paul reports a public disagreement between apostles without resolving it in the text. He records what he said; he does not record Peter's reply or the outcome. Later readers from Jerome onward found this uncomfortable enough that some proposed the confrontation was staged for the benefit of onlookers — a reading Augustine firmly rejected, insisting that Paul meant what he wrote. Augustine had the better of that exchange, and the passage has stood since as evidence that apostolic authority did not mean apostolic infallibility in conduct.

Verses 15–21 are the seedbed of the doctrine of justification by faith as it was formulated at the Reformation, and v. 16 in particular has carried enormous weight in that history. The more recent debate over πίστις Χριστοῦ, and over whether "works of the law" denotes moral self-effort or the boundary markers that separated Jew from Gentile, has refined the discussion without displacing Paul's central claim: the verdict of righteousness is received through faith in Christ and is not secured by law.

For the argument of Galatians, this passage is the pivot. Everything before it is biography establishing Paul's standing to say what he says; everything after it is exposition of what he has now said. Chapter 3 begins by appealing to the Galatians' own reception of the Spirit — the first of several proofs that the thesis of 2:16 is not Paul's innovation but their own experience.
