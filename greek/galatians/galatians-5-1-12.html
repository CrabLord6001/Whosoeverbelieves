Introduction to Galatians 5:1–12

The argument turns to application. Christ set them free, so they are not to put the yoke back on. Paul states the cost plainly: to take circumcision as a requirement is to become a debtor to the whole law and to sever oneself from the benefit Christ offers. Verses 5–6 give the positive counterpart — faith working through love — and the rest of the passage returns to the agitators, with Paul's sharpest words in the letter at v. 12.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads ἡμᾶς χριστὸς ἠλευθέρωσεν· στήκετε οὖν, the Byzantine text has οὖν ᾗ χριστὸς ἡμᾶς ἠλευθέρωσεν, στήκετε.

Verse 5:1 functions as both conclusion and commission — the inferential διό of 4:31 reaches its rhetorical destination here. The dative τῇ ἐλευθερίᾳ is best read as a dative of sphere: the freedom Christ purchased is not merely the goal but the very environment in which believers are to stand. The aorist ἠλευθέρωσεν grounds the imperative in completed historical fact — the liberation has already occurred; the call to stand firm presupposes it. Longenecker rightly notes that the figura etymologica (ἐλευθερίᾳ … ἠλευθέρωσεν) is rhetorically emphatic, hammering the freedom-word twice in quick succession.

The present imperative στήκετε (from στήκω, a late Hellenistic form of the perfect of ἵστημι) denotes ongoing, active maintenance of a position already occupied — not an invitation to enter freedom but a command to hold the ground already won. The negative counterpart ἐνέχεσθε (present passive imperative) carries the nuance of being caught or entangled in something — the passive voice suggesting that the danger is allowing oneself to be seized from without. The ζυγὸς δουλείας echoes the yoke-language of Acts 15:10 (Peter's speech at Jerusalem) and contrasts directly with the "yoke" of Jesus in Matt 11:29–30. From a Free Grace perspective, neither imperative bears on the question of justification — Paul is not warning that the Galatians will lose their standing before God. Rather, he is urging them not to abandon the sphere of grace as the operative principle of their sanctification and daily Christian walk, as established throughout chapters 3–4.

The emphatic self-identification ἐγὼ Παῦλος is unparalleled in Paul's letters in this form and signals that the apostle is staking his personal apostolic authority on what follows (cf. 2 Cor 10:1; Eph 3:1; Col 1:23 for similar self-identifications, though less emphatic). The particle Ἴδε (originally the aorist imperative of ὁράω but functioning here as an attention-getter like English "Look!") signals a major turning point in the argument. The third-class conditional (ἐάν + present subjunctive) treats circumcision as a genuine current possibility for the Galatians — they have not yet acted, and Paul intends to stop them.

The apodosis is theologically striking: Χριστὸς ὑμᾶς οὐδὲν ὠφελήσει. This cannot mean that Christ's atoning work would be nullified — Paul does not argue that circumcision destroys one's salvation. The point is functional and covenantal: if the Galatians seek circumcision as a means of improving their standing before God or completing their justification, they are operating on a different principle entirely, one in which Christ has no role. Moo correctly observes that ὠφελήσει here refers to benefit within the system the Galatians are contemplating — a works-law system in which Christ is simply irrelevant. Wilkin and the Free Grace tradition rightly stress that this is a warning about sphere and principle, not about the permanence of one's justification.

The verb μαρτύρομαι (middle voice) carries the weight of a solemn legal attestation — Paul is not merely opining but formally declaring before witnesses. The word πάλιν ("again") implies he has said this before, presumably on his first visit to Galatia. The substantival participle περιτεμνομένῳ (present tense — "the one who is in the act of being circumcised") narrows the audience to those currently contemplating or undergoing the rite for covenantal purposes, not to those circumcised as infants in Jewish families.

The crushing logic of the verse lies in ὅλον τὸν νόμον ποιῆσαι — the whole law, not selected portions. The agitators appear to have advocated circumcision as a kind of supplementary addition to faith in Christ. Paul demolishes this halfway position: the Torah is not a menu from which items can be selected. If a Gentile accepts circumcision as a means of covenantal standing, he has enrolled in a system that requires total and perfect compliance with all 613 commandments. Bruce notes that this would have been well understood by any Jewish interlocutor — circumcision was the rite of entry into the Mosaic covenant as a whole. The aorist infinitive ποιῆσαι may have a perfective nuance — to perform it completely, to discharge the obligation in full — which makes the burden even heavier. This verse directly back-references 3:10 (Deut 27:26: "cursed is everyone who does not continue in all things written in the book of the law").

Galatians 5:4 — Severed from Christ, Fallen from Grace

This is the most theologically contested verse in the passage and one of the most debated in Paul's letters. Two key terms demand careful attention. κατηργήθητε ἀπὸ Χριστοῦ uses the verb καταργέω, which in Paul consistently means "to render inoperative, to sever from a connection, to nullify a relationship" (cf. Rom 7:2, 6; Gal 3:17). The construction with ἀπό + genitive denotes separation: those who seek justification by law have rendered Christ operationally irrelevant to their justification project. This is not a statement about loss of eternal life but about the logical consequence of the chosen system — if you are justified by law, Christ contributes nothing to that endeavour (cf. v.2: "Christ will profit you nothing").

The phrase τῆς χάριτος ἐξεπέσατε — "you have fallen from grace" — is routinely misread as a warning about losing salvation, but the genitive τῆς χάριτος is a genitive of separation (falling away *from* grace), not a genitive of content. The word χάρις here denotes the grace-principle, the grace-sphere, the grace-method of relating to God — not grace as the abstract divine disposition of favour that secures salvation. Hodges and Wilkin are correct that the verse is describing a methodological defection: the Galatians are abandoning grace as their operating system and adopting law in its place. The relative clause οἵτινες ἐν νόμῳ δικαιοῦσθε uses a generic relative (οἵτινες = "you who are the kind of people who…") to identify the category of person being described, suggesting this is a logical consequence rather than a fait accompli for all readers. Schreiner, who reads the verse as a warning about apostasy and loss of salvation, struggles to reconcile this with Paul's confidence in vv.10 that "the one who is troubling you will bear his judgment" — implying the readers themselves are not yet irrecoverable.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:5

The emphatic pronoun ἡμεῖς sets up a sharp contrast with the "you" of vv.2–4 — "we" (Paul and those who operate on the grace-faith principle) as opposed to those seeking justification by law. The verb ἀπεκδεχόμεθα is Paul's strongest word for eschatological expectation (cf. Rom 8:19, 23, 25; 1 Cor 1:7; Phil 3:20) — it denotes eager, concentrated, forward-leaning awaiting. The two instrumental / source phrases work together: πνεύματι (dative of means — by the Spirit's agency) and ἐκ πίστεως (source — from faith as the operative principle) recall the Spirit-faith contrast of 3:2–5 and anchor this verse in the argument of the whole letter.

The phrase ἐλπίδα δικαιοσύνης is the exegetical crux. Three interpretations are current: (1) the hope that consists in righteousness (gen. of apposition) — righteousness is itself the eschatological object; (2) the hope that righteousness produces (gen. of source); (3) the hoped-for vindication/righteousness (objective genitive) — the final eschatological declaration of righteousness at the judgment seat. The third reading, favoured by Moo and Longenecker, has much to commend it: Paul may be gesturing at the future verdict rather than the present justification, suggesting that the believer's righteousness-standing, already secured by faith, will be publicly confirmed and fully manifested at the last day. For Free Grace exegesis this connects to the distinction between present positional justification (already received by faith, 2:16) and future experiential vindication — neither of which is endangered by the Galatians' contemplated defection to law.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:6
γὰρ ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ οὔτε περιτομή τι ἰσχύει οὔτε ἀκροβυστία [MAIN CLAUSE — "for in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything"] ἀλλὰ πίστις δι᾽ ἀγάπης ἐνεργουμένη [CONTRAST — what does count — "but faith working through love"] δι᾽ ἀγάπης [MEANS — channel through which faith operates — "through love"]

The phrase ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ sets the sphere within which the declaration is made — the only arena that counts is union with Christ. Within that sphere, the circumcision/uncircumcision distinction has zero covenantal valence (cf. 3:28; 6:15; 1 Cor 7:19). This is not indifferentism — Paul has just argued strenuously against circumcision — but a statement about its utter irrelevance as a means of standing before God.

The crux of v.6 is the participle ἐνεργουμένη. It is either middle voice ("faith activating itself through love") or passive ("faith being activated/energized by love"). The middle reading, preferred by Dunn and Betz, sees love as the channel or instrument through which faith expresses itself in concrete action — faith is not inert but produces love as its natural outworking. The passive reading, preferred by some Reformed commentators, makes love the activating force that puts faith to work. The middle is grammatically more natural here. Crucially, this is not a statement that faith must be accompanied by love to be saving faith — Paul has made abundantly clear that justification is ἐκ πίστεως, full stop (2:16; 3:6, 8, 11, 24). Rather, πίστις δι᾽ ἀγάπης ἐνεργουμένη describes the kind of faith that characterizes the Christian life in the grace-sphere — a living, active faith that flows naturally into love for God and neighbour. For Free Grace theology, this verse distinguishes saving faith (simple trust in Christ) from the quality of faith that marks the mature, Spirit-filled believer.

Paul shifts to the athletic metaphor that will recur in 5:1 (standing firm) and 5:25 (walking by the Spirit). The imperfect Ἐτρέχετε recalls Paul's concern in 2:2 that his own apostolic race not be in vain, and the same imagery in 1 Cor 9:24–27 and Phil 3:13–14. The verb ἐνέκοψεν (from ἐγκόπτω, "to cut into") was used of cutting up a road to impede an army's advance or of a runner cutting in on a competitor's lane — a vivid image of external interference with a race already underway. The singular subject ("who cut in on you?") hints at a specific leader among the agitators, though Paul will not name him.

The phrase τῇ ἀληθείᾳ μὴ πείθεσθαι — "so as not to be persuaded by / to obey the truth" — uses the articular dative with the infinitive. The ἀλήθεια here is the gospel of grace as Paul has proclaimed it throughout the letter (cf. 2:5, 14: "the truth of the gospel"). The infinitive πείθεσθαι (passive/middle of πείθω) means to be won over by, to follow, to comply with — the Galatians had been persuaded by the truth and were living accordingly; someone has now cut in and re-routed their persuasion toward a different gospel.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:8
ἡ πεισμονὴ οὐκ ἐκ τοῦ καλοῦντος ὑμᾶς [MAIN CLAUSE — elliptical (ἐστίν implied) — "the persuasion is not from the one who calls you"]

The word πεισμονή is a NT hapax — it appears nowhere else in the New Testament, and very rarely in earlier Greek literature. It picks up the root πείθ- from v.7 (ἐπείθεσθαι) in a deliberate wordplay: the "persuasion" that has cut in on their race does not come from τοῦ καλοῦντος ὑμᾶς — the one who called you. The present participle καλοῦντος is almost certainly a reference to God (cf. 1:6, where Paul marvels that the Galatians are "so quickly turning away from the one who called you in the grace of Christ"). The divine call is present-tense and ongoing — God continues to call — making the defection all the more tragic.

The verse is a terse dismissal: whatever the agitators are offering, however persuasive their case may seem, it does not originate from the God who called the Galatians into his grace. The argument has echoes of Paul's earlier statement in 1:8–9, where even an angel from heaven is anathematized for preaching a different gospel. The contrast with "the one calling you" implicitly identifies the agitators' persuasion as coming from a human — or worse — source, without specifying which.

This is a proverbial statement that Paul cites identically in 1 Cor 5:6, suggesting it was a known saying that he applies to different situations. In 1 Corinthians the leaven is sexual immorality tolerating itself within the congregation; here it is false teaching. The point is that even a small doctrinal concession — accepting circumcision as a supplement to faith — will not remain small. The logic of works-righteousness, once admitted, permeates and transforms the entire theological lump. The word ζύμη carries negative connotations in Jewish and early Christian usage (cf. the "leaven of the Pharisees," Mark 8:15; Luke 12:1), though Paul can use it positively in Matt 13:33. Here it is clearly negative: the false teaching is spreading corruption through the Galatian communities. This verse is Paul's explanation of why he cannot be indifferent to the circumcision issue — the stakes are the integrity of the whole gospel.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads ἐὰν, the Byzantine text has ἂν.

The perfect πέποιθα expresses Paul's settled, abiding confidence — not a recent or provisional hope but a firm persuasion grounded in the Lord. The phrase ἐν κυρίῳ qualifies the confidence: it rests not in Paul's optimism about human nature but in his trust in the Lord's faithfulness to his own people. This pastoral confidence is significant for the question of whether 5:4 describes the Galatians themselves: Paul's confidence that they "will think no other way" suggests they have not yet definitively crossed into the law-system he warns against. They are wavering, not apostate.

The singulars ὁ ταράσσων ("the one who is troubling") and ὅστις ἐὰν ᾖ ("whoever he may be") are fascinatingly guarded — Paul identifies the agitator by function but refuses to name him. The phrase ὅστις ἐὰν ᾖ may indicate genuine uncertainty about the leader's identity, or it may be rhetorical universalising — "I don't care who it is, the judgment stands." The noun κρίμα denotes judicial verdict and in Paul usually refers to divine judgment (cf. Rom 2:2–3; 3:8; 13:2). This is a serious eschatological warning directed entirely at the agitator(s), not at the Galatian congregation — a crucial distinction for Free Grace interpretation of the passage.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:11
εἰ περιτομὴν ἔτι κηρύσσω [CONDITION — 1st class; Paul treats it as a real rumour — "if I still preach circumcision"] τί ἔτι διώκομαι; [RHETORICAL Q. — reductio ad absurdum — "why am I still being persecuted?"] ἄρα κατήργηται τὸ σκάνδαλον τοῦ σταυροῦ [IRONIC INFERENCE — perf.: what would follow if the condition were true — "then the stumbling block of the cross has been abolished"]

Verse 11 responds to what appears to have been a specific slander: that Paul himself still preached circumcision when it suited him (perhaps a distortion of his circumcision of Timothy in Acts 16:3, or of his Jewish practice generally). The first-class condition (εἰ + present indicative) treats the premise as live and real for the sake of argument — "if, as they say, I still preach circumcision…" — before demolishing it with the rhetorical question: why then is Paul still being persecuted? If he preached circumcision, he would be in full agreement with the Jewish establishment and would face no persecution. The fact of his ongoing persecution is itself proof that he does not.

The ironic conclusion ἄρα κατήργηται τὸ σκάνδαλον τοῦ σταυροῦ is devastating. The σκάνδαλον τοῦ σταυροῦ — the offence/stumbling block of the cross — is its claim to be the sole and complete basis of right standing before God, requiring nothing from the human side. A circumcision-preaching Paul would have removed this offence by making the cross one element among others in a works-faith synthesis, and the Jewish community would have nothing to object to. The perfect κατήργηται echoes the same verb used in v.4 (κατηργήθητε ἀπὸ Χριστοῦ) — the cross would be rendered inoperative, nullified, abolished as the sole ground of salvation. Paul's point is that a gospel that supplements the cross with human achievement is no gospel at all (cf. 1:6–9).

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:12
ὄφελον καὶ ἀποκόψονται οἱ ἀναστατοῦντες ὑμᾶς [WISH CLAUSE — ὄφελον + future; sharpest rhetoric in the letter — "would that those who unsettle you would cut themselves off!"]

Verse 12 is the most pungently rhetorical sentence in the entire Pauline corpus. The particle ὄφελον introduces an unattainable or strongly desired wish; combined with the future indicative ἀποκόψονται rather than the optative, it expresses a fervent desire rather than a polite hope. The verb ἀποκόπτω means "to cut off" — and in the middle voice, "to cut oneself off." The wordplay with circumcision is unmistakeable: those who are so enthusiastic about cutting (περιτέμνω) should go further and emasculate themselves completely, like the Galli, the castrated priests of Cybele whose cult was well known throughout the Galatian region. Betz provides extensive documentation of the Cybele cult background. Bruce calls this "the most forceful expression of indignation in the letter."

The participle ἀναστατοῦντες (from ἀναστατόω, "to unsettle, to stir up, to turn upside down") shifts the vocabulary from the earlier ταράσσων (v.10) to an even stronger term of social disruption — the same verb used of those who "turned the world upside down" in Acts 17:6. Paul's pastoral outrage is not theatre: the agitators are dismantling the communities he founded and distorting the gospel that gives life. From a Free Grace perspective, v.12 is not the word of a man worried about his converts losing salvation — it is the word of an apostle who sees believers being drawn out of the grace-sphere into a system where Christ profits them nothing, where the cross is offence-free and therefore powerless. The stakes of the circumcision debate are nothing less than the integrity of the gospel itself.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 5:1–12

Passage Summary: Galatians 5:1–12 turns the argument into application. Christ set believers free, so they are not to take the yoke back on; to accept circumcision as a requirement is to become a debtor to the whole law and to cut oneself off from the benefit Christ offers. Over against that stands the positive formula of v. 6 — faith working through love — before Paul returns to the agitators and the judgment they will bear.
