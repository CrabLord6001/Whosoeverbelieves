Introduction to the Epistle to the Galatians

Galatians is Paul's most urgent letter. Writing to congregations he had founded, Paul confronts teachers — commonly called the Judaizers — who were telling his Gentile converts that faith in Christ was not enough: circumcision and Torah observance were required for full standing before God. Paul's response is not a measured essay but a sustained argument under pressure, and it produced the clearest statement in Scripture that righteousness comes by faith apart from works of law.

The letter's date and destination are debated. The South Galatian view locates the recipients in the cities of Acts 13–14 (Antioch of Pisidia, Iconium, Lystra, Derbe) and dates the letter before the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15, making it possibly Paul's earliest surviving letter. The North Galatian view places the recipients among ethnic Galatians farther north and dates the letter later. The exegesis in this series does not depend on resolving that question.

The Greek text throughout this series follows the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with NA28 divergences noted where they occur, and the English is the World English Bible.

Galatians opens without the thanksgiving Paul normally offers. In its place stands a defence of his apostleship (v. 1) and, by v. 6, open astonishment. The salutation is already doing argumentative work: Paul's commission came neither from a human source nor through a human intermediary, and the Christ who commissioned him gave himself to rescue believers out of the present evil age. Verses 6–10 then state the crisis — the Galatians are deserting the One who called them for a gospel that is not another gospel at all — and pronounce the letter's double anathema on anyone who preaches otherwise.

Paul's letters normally open with a bare self-identification; here the salutation is already an argument. ἀπόστολος οὐκ ἀπ’ ἀνθρώπων, οὐδὲ δι’ ἀνθρώπου denies two distinct things with two distinct prepositions. ἀπό denies the ultimate source: no human body commissioned him. διά denies the intermediary: no human hand passed the commission along. The agitators evidently argued that Paul's authority was derivative — received from Jerusalem and therefore subordinate to it. Paul's grammar refuses both halves of that claim before he has written a sentence of argument.

The positive side uses the same διά he has just denied: διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ καὶ θεοῦ πατρός. The single preposition governing both names places the Father and the Son together as the agency of his commissioning, which is a considerable Christological claim made in passing.

The Father is then identified by a single act: τοῦ ἐγείραντος αὐτὸν ἐκ νεκρῶν. The resurrection is not decoration here. An apostle is one sent by a living Lord, and the raising of Jesus is what makes Paul's commission a present reality rather than a memory.

οἱ σὺν ἐμοὶ πάντες ἀδελφοί — "all the brothers with me" — is unusual. Paul normally names his co-senders (Sosthenes, Timothy, Silvanus). The anonymous plural here suggests he wants the weight of a body of witnesses behind the letter rather than the endorsement of particular individuals: he is not alone in what he is about to say.

ταῖς ἐκκλησίαις τῆς Γαλατίας is addressed to a plural of congregations across a region, not to one city church. Whether Γαλατία means the Roman province (so the South Galatian view, making these the churches of Acts 13–14) or the northern ethnic territory is the standing question in the letter's dating; the exegesis here does not turn on it.

What is most striking is what is missing. Every other Pauline letter to a church except this one follows the address with thanksgiving. Here there is none — the omission is itself a rebuke, and the Galatians would have felt it.

The greeting χάρις ὑμῖν καὶ εἰρήνη is Paul's standard fusion of the Greek greeting (χαίρειν, reshaped into χάρις) with the Hebrew shalom. It is formulaic, but in this letter the first word of it is the contested one: grace is precisely what the Galatians are in danger of abandoning (1:6; 5:4).

The source is given once and governs both names: ἀπὸ θεοῦ πατρός, καὶ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ. Grace and peace proceed from Father and Son together.

Verse 4 is the gospel in one relative clause. τοῦ δόντος ἑαυτὸν — "who gave himself" — makes the gift and the giver the same: Christ did not merely provide something, he handed over his own person.

περὶ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν states what the self-giving addressed. The Byzantine text reads περί where NA28 has ὑπέρ; in this construction both carry the sense of acting with respect to, and on behalf of, our sins, and nothing in the theology turns on the choice.

The purpose clause is the striking part: ὅπως ἐξέληται ἡμᾶς ἐκ τοῦ ἐνεστῶτος αἰῶνος πονηροῦ. ἐξαιρέω is a rescue verb — to snatch out, to extract. The deliverance Paul names is not merely forgiveness of past sins but removal from the grip of an entire age, and the perfect participle ἐνεστῶτος describes that age as one which has set in and still stands. The Galatians' drift toward Torah observance is, in these terms, a return into the very age Christ died to pull them out of.

κατὰ τὸ θέλημα τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ πατρὸς ἡμῶν closes the clause by grounding the whole transaction in the Father's will — forestalling any suggestion that the Son's grace and the Father's law stand in tension.

The doxology is brief and conventional, but its placement is not. Paul has just compressed the gospel into v. 4, and the glory he ascribes follows directly from it: the deliverance described is God's achievement, so the glory is God's. The Ἀμήν invites the congregation's assent before the rebuke of v. 6 lands.

Θαυμάζω occupies the slot where Paul's thanksgiving would stand. The effect in a letter read aloud would have been immediate: the congregation waits for "I thank God for you" and hears "I am astonished" instead.

Two details sharpen the charge. οὕτως ταχέως — "so quickly" — says the defection came soon after their founding, which is one of the arguments for an early date. And μετατίθεσθε is present tense: the desertion is in progress, not finished. Paul writes to people who can still be turned back, which is why the letter argues rather than merely condemns.

Most important is the object of the desertion. They are turning ἀπὸ τοῦ καλέσαντος ὑμᾶς — away from a person, the One who called them in grace, not away from a doctrinal position. For Free Grace reading this matters: the issue is not whether they remain saved but whom they are now listening to, and what they are forfeiting by listening.

ἕτερον εὐαγγέλιον uses the adjective for a thing of a different kind, which sets up the correction in v. 7.

Paul immediately qualifies the phrase he has just used. ὃ οὐκ ἔστιν ἄλλο — using the other adjective, for another of the same kind — denies that what the agitators offer belongs in the category at all. There is a different gospel on offer (ἕτερον); there is no second gospel (ἄλλο). The two words do the work of a paragraph.

The agitators are described only by participles: οἱ ταράσσοντες ὑμᾶς, "those troubling you." Paul never names them anywhere in the letter. ταράσσω is used of stirring up water or throwing a crowd into confusion; the word suggests disturbance of a settled state rather than open opposition.

Their aim is μεταστρέψαι τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ Χριστοῦ — to turn the gospel around into its opposite. Paul's judgment is not that they add an optional extra but that the addition reverses the thing itself.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads [ὑμῖν] παρ’, the Byzantine text has ὑμῖν παρ’.

The condition is deliberately extreme. Paul includes himself (ἡμεῖς) and then raises the stakes as far as they can go — ἄγγελος ἐξ οὐρανοῦ. The gospel's authority rests on its content, not on the standing of its messenger, and Paul subordinates his own apostolic authority to it in the same breath in which he has defended that authority.

παρ’ ὃ εὐηγγελισάμεθα — "contrary to what we preached" — makes the measure the message already delivered and received, not a later development or a deeper teaching.

ἀνάθεμα ἔστω draws on the LXX term for what is devoted to God for destruction (Hebrew ḥerem). The third-person imperative is not a wish but a pronouncement. Paul is declaring a verdict on the preacher of such a message, not on the Galatians who have been listening to it — a distinction worth keeping clear.

The repetition is deliberate, and the grammar shifts in a way that sharpens it. Verse 8 used a third-class condition (ἐάν with the subjunctive) — a hypothetical case. Verse 9 uses a first-class condition (εἴ with the indicative), which assumes the case to be real. The hypothetical of v. 8 has become the actual situation in Galatia.

Ὡς προειρήκαμεν is perfect: this is not a new ruling invented for the occasion but a warning Paul had already given and that still stands. The standard in this verse is παρ’ ὃ παρελάβετε — what you received — shifting the measure from Paul's preaching to the Galatians' own reception of it, so they cannot plead ignorance of the content.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads ἔτι, the Byzantine text has γὰρ ἔτι.

The double question answers a charge. Paul has evidently been accused of trimming his message to suit his audience — most plausibly, of dropping circumcision to make the gospel easier for Gentiles. The curses of vv. 8–9 are themselves the refutation: a man-pleaser does not pronounce anathemas.

πείθω here carries the sense of seeking to win someone over or conciliate them, and the alternatives Paul poses — men or God — admit no middle position.

The closing condition is contrary to fact: εἰ … ἤρεσκον … οὐκ ἂν ἤμην, "if I were still pleasing men, I would not be Christ's slave." δοῦλος is the self-designation Paul shares with James and Peter, and it settles the question of whose approval governs him. The word also closes the circle with v. 1: an apostle sent by Christ is a slave belonging to Christ, and neither title was conferred by men.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 1:1–10

Passage Summary: Galatians 1:1–10 is the letter's opening salvo. Paul establishes the divine origin of his apostleship, compresses the gospel into a single relative clause about Christ's self-giving (v. 4), and then, skipping the customary thanksgiving entirely, expresses astonishment that the Galatians are turning to a different gospel — closing with a twice-stated curse on any who would preach one.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

A. Salutation as argument (vv. 1–5). Paul loads the opening with the two claims the letter must establish: that his apostleship is of divine and not human origin (v. 1), and that the gospel is Christ's self-giving to rescue believers out of the present evil age according to the Father's will (v. 4). Neither is argued here; both are asserted, and the body of the letter then defends them in order — the apostleship through 1:11–2:21, the gospel from 3:1 onward.

B. The missing thanksgiving (v. 6). Every Pauline letter to a congregation except this one moves from greeting to thanksgiving. Here Θαυμάζω stands in that slot. The omission is structural, not accidental, and it signals the letter's temperature before a word of argument is made.

C. The double anathema (vv. 8–9). The repetition is not rhetorical excess. The shift from a third-class condition (v. 8, hypothetical) to a first-class condition (v. 9, assumed real) moves the curse from a thought experiment to the actual Galatian situation, and the shift of standard from "what we preached" to "what you received" closes off any appeal to misunderstanding.

II. Key Theological Themes

1. Apostolic authority grounded in the resurrection (v. 1). Paul identifies the Father as the one who raised Jesus from the dead, which is what makes a commission "through Jesus Christ" a present and not a posthumous authority. The defence of his apostleship and the gospel of the risen Christ are a single claim.

2. Deliverance from an age, not merely from guilt (v. 4). ἐξαιρέω with ἐκ τοῦ ἐνεστῶτος αἰῶνος πονηροῦ frames salvation as rescue out of a present order that still stands. This is the apocalyptic frame within which the rest of the letter's arguments about law, flesh and Spirit operate: to go back under law is to re-enter the age Christ died to extract believers from.

3. The gospel as a fixed deposit (vv. 8–9). The measure of any preaching is what was already preached and received. Paul subjects himself and even an angel to that measure, which locates authority in the message rather than in the messenger — including in himself.

4. Grace as the thing being deserted (v. 6). The Galatians are turning from "the One who called you in grace." Paul does not describe their situation as the loss of eternal life but as desertion of a person and of the grace in which the call was issued. From a Free Grace perspective the distinction is important: what is at stake in the letter is their standing in grace, their freedom, and their reward — not the security of the gift they received by faith.

5. Man-pleasing and slavery to Christ (v. 10). Paul sets human approval and the service of Christ as mutually exclusive, and makes his own willingness to pronounce a curse the proof of which master he serves.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

The absence of a thanksgiving in Galatians has been noticed since antiquity and remains one of the clearest pieces of internal evidence for the letter's urgency. The "so quickly" of v. 6 is also a standing datum in the dating debate: those who hold the South Galatian view take it to support a very early date, before the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15, which would make Galatians the earliest of Paul's surviving letters.

Verses 8–9 have carried disproportionate weight in the history of doctrine. They were central to Reformation arguments that the content of the gospel stands above ecclesiastical authority, since Paul places even an apostle and an angel under the judgment of the message already delivered. The passage is equally a caution against using the curse formula casually: Paul pronounces it on those who preach a different gospel, not on those who have been confused by one — the Galatians themselves he addresses throughout as brothers.
