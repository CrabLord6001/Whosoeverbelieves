Introduction to Galatians 2:1–10

Paul's biographical argument reaches its first climax. Fourteen years after the events of chapter 1 he goes up to Jerusalem — by revelation, not by summons — and lays his gospel before the leaders there. Two things happen, and Paul wants both on record. Titus, a Greek, was not compelled to be circumcised, which settles the test case in the open. And the men of repute added nothing to his message; they recognised the grace given him and extended the right hand of fellowship. The passage is Paul's proof that the gospel he preaches was neither corrected nor supplemented by Jerusalem.

Ἔπειτα διὰ δεκατεσσάρων ἐτῶν resumes the itinerary of chapter 1, and the interval carries the argument. Whether the fourteen years run from his conversion or from the visit of 1:18, Paul had been preaching for the better part of two decades before the consultation described here. A man whose gospel required Jerusalem's authorisation does not wait that long to seek it.

The companions are chosen, not incidental. Barnabas was a trusted Jerusalem figure (Acts 4:36–37; 9:27), which gave the delegation standing. συμπαραλαβὼν καὶ Τίτον is the deliberate move: Titus was an uncircumcised Greek, and bringing him turned an abstract question into a case that had to be decided in front of witnesses.

Which visit this is remains debated — the famine visit of Acts 11:30 or the council of Acts 15. Those who date Galatians before the council identify it with the former, which has the advantage of explaining why Paul never cites the council's decree in a letter where it would have settled the argument outright.

ἀνέβην δὲ κατὰ ἀποκάλυψιν is pointed. Paul went up because God directed him, not because Jerusalem summoned him. The clause guards the independence established in chapter 1 even while describing a consultation.

ἀνεθέμην means to lay a matter before someone for consideration — the kind of thing colleagues do. It is not the language of a subordinate submitting work for approval, and the distinction matters for Paul's case.

The phrase τοῖς δοκοῦσιν — "those of repute" — recurs through vv. 2, 6 and 9. It is not contemptuous; Paul accepts that they are held in esteem. But he keeps a slight distance from the estimate, because his argument requires that reputation not be the thing that settles doctrine.

μήπως εἰς κενὸν τρέχω ἢ ἔδραμον has troubled readers who take it as doubt about his gospel. It is better read as concern for the mission: if Jerusalem had repudiated the Gentile work, the result would have been a fractured church and years of labour undone. The worry is practical and ecclesial, not theological.

This is the verse the whole visit turns on. οὐδὲ Τίτος … ἠναγκάσθη περιτμηθῆναι — not even Titus was compelled to be circumcised.

The concessive participle Ἕλλην ὤν sharpens it: he was precisely the sort of person the agitators' programme would require to be circumcised, and he was present in Jerusalem among the leadership, and still no such requirement was imposed.

ἠναγκάσθη is the operative word, and Paul will use the same verb in v. 14 of Peter's conduct at Antioch — "how is it that you compel the Gentiles to Judaize?" What Jerusalem declined to do, Peter's withdrawal at Antioch effectively did. The verbal link binds the two halves of the chapter.

For the Galatians the inference is direct: the apostolic leadership itself did not treat circumcision as necessary for a Gentile believer's standing. The agitators are demanding what Jerusalem declined to demand.

The syntax of v. 4 is broken — it begins with a causal phrase that never reaches a main verb — and the roughness is a fair index of Paul's feeling about the people he is describing.

Two compounds in παρα- fall in consecutive words: παρεισάκτους (smuggled in) and παρεισῆλθον (slipped in alongside). The repetition presses the image of infiltration. ψευδαδέλφους — "false brothers" — is a judgment about their standing, not merely their opinions; Paul distinguishes them sharply from the Jerusalem leaders he has just been consulting.

κατασκοπῆσαι is a military term for reconnaissance. What they came to scout is τὴν ἐλευθερίαν ἡμῶν ἣν ἔχομεν ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ — the freedom believers possess, present tense, as a current holding in Christ.

Their aim, ἵνα ἡμᾶς καταδουλώσωνται, is stated as enslavement. The intensive compound of δουλόω is deliberate. This is the first appearance of the freedom-and-slavery antithesis that will organise chapters 4 and 5, and it is introduced here not as a metaphor for lost salvation but as the loss of a liberty believers presently have.

οὐδὲ πρὸς ὥραν εἴξαμεν τῇ ὑποταγῇ — not for an hour did they yield. The aorist marks a single, decisive refusal, and πρὸς ὥραν rules out even a temporary accommodation for the sake of peace.

The purpose clause explains why a matter that might look like a procedural concession was worth a confrontation: ἵνα ἡ ἀλήθεια τοῦ εὐαγγελίου διαμείνῃ πρὸς ὑμᾶς. Paul stood firm in Jerusalem years earlier so that the gospel would still be intact when it reached Galatia. The second person πρὸς ὑμᾶς lands the point on the present readers: the freedom they are now bargaining away was defended on their behalf before they ever heard of it.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads [ὁ] θεὸς, the Byzantine text has θεὸς.

The Greek here is the most broken in the letter. Paul begins Ἀπὸ δὲ τῶν δοκούντων εἶναί τι — "but from those reputed to be something" — then interrupts himself twice before restarting the sentence with a different subject. Robinson's text marks the interruptions with dashes. The syntax is worth preserving rather than smoothing: a man dictating under pressure sounds like this.

The first parenthesis, ὁποῖοί ποτε ἦσαν οὐδέν μοι διαφέρει, sets aside the leaders' past standing — most naturally their having known Jesus in the flesh — as irrelevant to the question at issue.

The second, πρόσωπον θεὸς ἀνθρώπου οὐ λαμβάνει, renders the Hebrew idiom for showing partiality (lifting the face). Paul grounds his indifference to reputation in the character of God, which is a considerable thing to say about the Jerusalem apostles and is said without hostility.

The restarted main clause is the verdict: ἐμοὶ … οἱ δοκοῦντες οὐδὲν προσανέθεντο. The verb is the same one Paul used in 1:16 of not consulting flesh and blood. The leaders laid no additional requirement on him — no circumcision, no Torah observance, nothing added to the gospel he had been preaching for fourteen years.

πεπίστευμαι τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τῆς ἀκροβυστίας is a perfect passive: Paul has been entrusted, and remains entrusted, with a standing commission. The passive is divine — God did the entrusting, which is why Jerusalem's role could only be recognition.

The genitives τῆς ἀκροβυστίας and τῆς περιτομῆς describe the sphere of each mission, not two different messages. There is one gospel; Peter carries it to Jews and Paul to Gentiles. Reading these as two gospels would undo everything Paul argued in 1:6–9.

A small point of style worth noticing: the Byzantine text here reads Πέτρος, while v. 9 has Κηφᾶς. Paul moves between the Greek and Aramaic names for the same man within three verses.

The verse is a parenthetical ground for v. 7, and its force lies in the repeated verb. ὁ … ἐνεργήσας Πέτρῳ … ἐνήργησεν καὶ ἐμοί — the one who worked in Peter also worked in me. The subject of both is God, unnamed but unmistakable.

ἀποστολήν covers both commissions with a single noun. Paul does not claim a different kind of apostleship from Peter's, nor a lesser one; he claims the same apostleship exercised in a different field. Given that his opponents evidently ranked him below the Jerusalem apostles, the symmetry of the sentence is the argument.

The order of names — James first, then Cephas, then John — probably reflects James's leading role in the Jerusalem church by this date.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 2:1–10

Passage Summary: Galatians 2:1–10 recounts Paul's Jerusalem visit with Barnabas and Titus. Against pressure from "false brothers" smuggled in to spy out the freedom believers have in Christ, Titus was not compelled to be circumcised; and the reputed pillars — James, Cephas and John — added nothing to Paul's gospel but recognised his commission to the Gentiles and gave the right hand of fellowship, asking only that he remember the poor.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

A. The visit and its terms (vv. 1–2). Paul fixes the interval (fourteen years), the companions (Barnabas and Titus), the motive (revelation, not summons), and the manner of the consultation (ἀνεθέμην, laying a matter before colleagues). Every one of these details defends the independence argued in chapter 1 while describing a genuine consultation.

B. The test case (vv. 3–5). Titus is the hinge. An uncircumcised Greek was present in Jerusalem and was not compelled to be circumcised, despite pressure from infiltrators. The syntax breaks down in vv. 4–5 — the sentence never completes — and the roughness registers Paul's feeling about the episode.

C. The outcome (vv. 6–10). Three movements: the leaders added nothing (v. 6, after a doubly interrupted sentence); they recognised the commission God had given (vv. 7–9); they asked only that the poor be remembered (v. 10). The paragraph is constructed to close every avenue by which the agitators might appeal to Jerusalem against Paul.

D. The link forward. ἀναγκάζω in v. 3 — what Jerusalem did not do to Titus — returns in v. 14 as what Peter's withdrawal did do to the Gentiles at Antioch. The two halves of chapter 2 are bound by that verb.

II. Key Theological Themes

1. Recognition, not authorisation (vv. 6–9). The pillars recognised the grace given to Paul (γνόντες τὴν χάριν τὴν δοθεῖσάν μοι) and added nothing (οὐδὲν προσανέθεντο). Apostolic standing in this account is conferred by God and acknowledged by the church, never conferred by the church. This is the theological point on which Paul's freedom to rebuke Peter in v. 11 depends.

2. Freedom as a present possession (v. 4). The false brothers came to spy out "the freedom we have in Christ Jesus" — present tense, a holding already in hand. What was at risk was not the believers' possession of eternal life but their liberty, and the threat is described as enslavement (καταδουλώσωνται). This introduces the freedom/slavery antithesis that governs chapters 4–5.

3. One gospel, two spheres (vv. 7–9). The genitives of v. 7 divide the mission field, not the message, and v. 8 grounds both commissions in the same divine working. Paul is careful here: having insisted in 1:6–9 that there is no second gospel, he cannot now be heard to claim a distinct one for Gentiles.

4. The truth of the gospel is worth a confrontation (v. 5). Paul refuses a temporary, prudential concession — not even for an hour — because the matter at stake was not a custom but the gospel's truth, and because the Galatians would eventually be the beneficiaries of that refusal.

5. Grace that adds nothing. Running through the passage is a single implied argument for the Galatians: the apostles nearest to Jesus, meeting the test case face to face, laid no requirement on a Gentile believer beyond faith in Christ. Those now demanding circumcision are asking for what Jerusalem itself declined to ask.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

Identifying this visit is one of the standing problems of Pauline chronology. The two candidates are the famine-relief visit of Acts 11:27–30 and the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15. The private character of the meeting described here (κατ’ ἰδίαν, v. 2), and Paul's silence about any conciliar decree in a letter where citing one would have ended the argument, are the strongest reasons for identifying it with the earlier visit and dating Galatians before Acts 15.

The passage is also the primary evidence for how the earliest church resolved its first major dispute: not by a ruling from a central authority imposed on a subordinate, but by recognition between apostles of a common gospel entrusted to different fields. Paul reports the Jerusalem leaders with respect and without deference — a combination the letter needs, since his case requires both that he owed them nothing for his gospel and that they found nothing in it to correct.

Verse 10's request to remember the poor had a long afterlife in Paul's ministry. The collection for the saints in Jerusalem occupies substantial space in Romans and the Corinthian correspondence, and Paul seems to have regarded it as a tangible expression of the fellowship pledged by the right hand in v. 9.
