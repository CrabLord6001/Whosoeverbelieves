Introduction to Galatians 4:17–31

Paul exposes the agitators' motive — they court the Galatians in order to be courted in return (vv. 17–18) — and then drops his argument for a moment of raw pastoral feeling: he is in labor again until Christ is formed in them. From v. 21 he turns to the law itself and reads the Hagar and Sarah narrative as an allegory of two covenants, two Jerusalems, and two kinds of sonship. The conclusion is blunt: the children of the free woman do not share an inheritance with the children of the slave.

Paul exposes the agitators' strategy with surgical precision. The verb ζηλόω (used three times in vv.17-18) is rich: it means to be zealous for, to court with devotion, to seek to win someone's exclusive loyalty. The agitators are lavishing attention, flattery, and zealous interest on the Galatians — but Paul reveals the motive: οὐ καλῶς, "not nobly, not from a good place."

The intent is named starkly: ἐκκλεῖσαι ὑμᾶς — "to shut you out." The verb ἐκκλείω means to exclude, to bar from access. The question is: shut out from what? Most likely: to exclude the Galatians from the community of those who are "properly" Jewish-Christian (i.e., circumcised), thereby creating a sense of incompleteness and spiritual anxiety — so that the Galatians will feel they need the agitators' teaching to be fully included.

The purpose clause reveals the payoff the agitators seek: ἵνα αὐτοὺς ζηλοῦτε — "so that you will court them." The flattery creates dependency; the exclusion creates felt need; the felt need drives the Galatians to seek the agitators' approval and instruction. It is a masterclass in manipulative pastoral control. Paul names it plainly so the Galatians can see it for what it is.

Paul is not against zeal or devoted attention per se — καλὸν δὲ ζηλοῦσθαι ἐν καλῷ πάντοτε ("it is good to be zealously courted in a good cause always"). The word καλόν/καλῷ appears twice in the same sentence, forming a play: zeal is fine when it is genuinely καλόν in its content and motive. The agitators' zeal fails this test because their motive is self-serving (v.17).

The implied contrast with the agitators is pointed: the Galatians enjoyed Paul's devoted pastoral attention when he was physically present with them. The agitators' flattery is available in his absence. Paul's concern is that noble zeal — grounded in truth and genuine concern — should mark their relationships πάντοτε ("always"), not only when the one who models it is physically present to enforce it. Authentic gospel community does not depend on the presence of a controlling figure.

This is one of the most emotionally charged verses in the Pauline corpus. The address τέκνα μου ("my children") — neuter plural, the most tender address possible — signals a complete shift of emotional register. Paul speaks not as theologian but as spiritual parent.

The metaphor is startling and deliberate: Paul casts himself as a mother in labor. ὠδίνω is the verb for the agony of childbirth contractions. Paul has used birth-pain language before (Rom 8:22; 1 Cor 4:15; 1 Thess 2:7), but here it is particularly vivid. He originally travailed over them at their conversion — bringing them to new birth through the gospel. Now, because of the agitators' influence, he is in labor a second time (πάλιν). Their drift toward Torah-observance has, in his metaphor, imperiled the spiritual life already birthed in them, requiring a second round of apostolic labor.

The goal of this travail is stated with precision: ἄχρι οὗ μορφωθῇ Χριστὸς ἐν ὑμῖν — "until Christ is formed in you." The verb μορφόω (aorist passive subjunctive — "be formed, take shape") describes a process of interior formation. Μορφή is the shape or essential character of something — Paul prays for Christ's very character and likeness to take shape within the Galatian believers. This is not merely a matter of correct doctrine but of Christological transformation. The imagery suggests gestation: just as a fetus gradually takes human form in the womb, so Christ is to be progressively formed within the believer's inner life through the Spirit (cf. 2:20: "it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me").

The imperfect ἤθελον expresses a present unfulfilled wish — like the English "I wish I were" (contrary-to-fact). Paul longs to be physically present, not merely to deliver a letter. The written word, however powerful, cannot fully substitute for the pastoral presence of a father with his children.

The desire to ἀλλάξαι τὴν φωνήν μου ("change my tone/voice") is revealing. The written letter is necessarily one-toned — sharp rebuke mixed with tender appeal. In person, Paul could modulate: he could soften when he saw receptivity, press harder when he perceived resistance, weep when grief was called for, laugh when joy broke through. The φωνή ("voice, tone") of the letter is locked by necessity in its written form.

The reason for this desire: ὅτι ἀποροῦμαι ἐν ὑμῖν — "because I am at a loss about you." Ἀπορέω (from ἄπορος — "no way through") means to be without resources, to have no idea how to proceed, to be utterly perplexed. Paul does not know what tone to strike. He cannot fully read their situation at a distance. This is not apostolic weakness but pastoral honesty — and it stands in stark contrast to the agitators' confident certainty about what the Galatians need.

Paul pivots sharply from emotional appeal to renewed scriptural argument. The imperative Λέγετέ μοι ("Tell me!") is urgent and almost confrontational. He identifies his audience precisely: οἱ ὑπὸ νόμον θέλοντες εἶναι — "those who want to be under law." The present participle θέλοντες marks this as an ongoing desire, not a done deal — they are still in the process of deciding. Paul is intervening before the decision is irreversible.

The rhetorical question τὸν νόμον οὐκ ἀκούετε ("do you not hear the law?") contains a clever play: νόμος is used twice in the verse with different senses. The first time: "the law" as a legal/covenantal system they want to be under. The second: "the law" as the Pentateuch — the story of Abraham in Genesis. Paul is about to use the Torah itself to argue against Torah-observance as the basis of covenant standing. If they really listened to what Torah says, they would understand his argument. The irony is pointed.

Paul begins his allegorical argument by citing the historical fact from Genesis: Abraham had two sons — Ishmael (by Hagar, the slave woman, Gen 16) and Isaac (by Sarah, the free woman, Gen 21). The formula γέγραπται γὰρ ("for it stands written") anchors the argument in Scripture itself — a fitting move given that Paul is addressing people who want to be "under the law" (v.21), i.e., under the authority of the Torah.

The key categories are established immediately: παιδίσκη ("slave girl, bondwoman") vs. ἐλευθέρα ("free woman"). These terms — slavery and freedom — have been the structural poles of the entire letter (cf. 2:4; 3:28; 4:1-7; 5:1). By grounding the allegory in the contrast between Hagar and Sarah, Paul locates the freedom/slavery antithesis within Abraham's own household. The Torah's story contains within itself the argument for Paul's gospel.

Paul draws the theological distinction that drives the entire allegory. Both sons of Abraham are historical figures — but their origins place them in different categories. κατὰ σάρκα ("according to flesh") describes Ishmael's birth: Abraham and Hagar conceived naturally, through human initiative and physical capacity. Abraham was biologically capable; Hagar was a healthy slave woman. There was nothing miraculous about it — it was a human solution to a human problem (Sarah's apparent infertility).

δι᾽ ἐπαγγελίας ("through promise") describes Isaac's birth: Sarah's womb was "as good as dead" (Rom 4:19), Abraham was nearly a hundred years old, and yet God had promised a son. Isaac's birth required divine intervention — it was the fulfillment of a word that transcended natural possibility. The perfect tense γεγέννηται ("has been born, remains born") underscores the permanent, established character of each birth as a type.

The antithesis κατὰ σάρκα vs. δι᾽ ἐπαγγελίας maps directly onto Paul's foundational contrast throughout the letter: human effort/achievement vs. divine gift/promise. Torah-observance as the basis of covenant standing is a κατὰ σάρκα strategy; justification by faith in Christ's promise is a δι᾽ ἐπαγγελίας reality. The very family of Abraham encodes the gospel in its structure.

Paul names his interpretive method: ἅτινά ἐστιν ἀλληγορούμενα — "which things are being allegorized." The verb ἀλληγορέω (NT hapax) does not necessarily mean what modern interpreters call "allegory" (an extended metaphor where every element has a hidden meaning). Paul uses the word loosely to mean a figural or typological reading — the historical events contain a deeper significance that illuminates present realities. He does not deny the historical reality of Hagar and Sarah; he draws theological meaning from the pattern.

The identification is startling: the two women correspond to δύο διαθῆκαι — two covenants. Hagar corresponds to the Sinai covenant (Mosaic law); Sarah will correspond to the Abrahamic/new covenant (v.26). The Sinai covenant is described with a damning participial phrase: εἰς δουλείαν γεννῶσα — "bearing children into slavery." The covenant given at Sinai produces slaves, not free heirs. This is the logical endpoint of the argument from 3:10-4:7: the law functions as custodian, guardian, and — in this metaphor — slave-mother, producing children who share its own enslaved status.

The identification ἥτις ἐστὶν Ἁγάρ completes the first column of the allegory. Note Paul does not name Sarah explicitly — the structural logic implies her, but he focuses on the positive identification of the "Jerusalem above" (v.26) rather than Sarah the individual. The allegorical structure draws on the Genesis narrative but reframes it: Hagar's story is not primarily about an Egyptian slave woman but about a covenantal type that finds its anti-type in the present-day situation of the Galatians.

This verse is the most geographically and textually complex in the passage. The unusual construction τὸ δὲ Ἁγὰρ (article + indeclinable proper noun) treats "Hagar" as a neuter noun — either "the name Hagar" or "the word/element Hagar." Paul may be noting a linguistic connection: in Arabic (the language of the Nabataean region he knew from his time in Arabia, cf. 1:17), Hagar or a cognate word denoted a rocky mountain or fortress, potentially linking the name etymologically to Sinai's rocky terrain. This is conjectural but fits Paul's multilingual background.

The key word is the NT hapax συστοιχεῖ — "corresponds to, stands in the same column as." The verb comes from military/grammatical usage: στοιχεῖον means an element in a row or column; συστοιχέω means to be aligned in the same column. Paul envisions a two-column table: Hagar / Sarah; Sinai / Jerusalem above; slavery / freedom; present Jerusalem / heavenly Jerusalem. Hagar-Sinai-present Jerusalem occupy one column; Sarah-promise-Jerusalem above occupy the other.

The identification of τῇ νῦν Ἰερουσαλήμ ("the present Jerusalem") with the Hagar/slavery column is Paul's most provocative move in the allegory. Jerusalem — the holy city, the center of Torah observance, the place where the agitators' authority was grounded (cf. 1:17-18; 2:1-10) — is in Paul's reckoning on the wrong side of the allegorical ledger. The earthly Jerusalem, with all its temple, Torah, and institutional religious apparatus, is in the bondage column. Its children (δουλεύει … μετὰ τῶν τέκνων αὐτῆς) share their mother's slavery. This is a direct counter to the Judaizers' appeal to Jerusalem-based authority.

The gospel's reach beyond Israel is the fulfillment of Isaiah's promise.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads Διό, the Byzantine text has Ἄρα.

The chapter closes with one of Paul's characteristically crisp, declarative conclusions. The inferential διό ("therefore") draws the line under the entire allegorical argument. The first-person plural ἐσμεν ("we are") is deliberately inclusive — Paul aligns himself with the Galatians in the free-woman column. Jew and Gentile, apostle and congregation — all who are in Christ are children of the free woman together.

The antithesis is absolute and binary: οὐκ … παιδίσκης τέκνα ἀλλὰ τῆς ἐλευθέρας — "not children of the slave woman but of the free woman." There is no middle position, no partial Torah-observance that keeps one foot in both columns. The allegorical logic demands a choice — Hagar or Sarah, slavery or freedom, law as the governing principle or promise as the governing principle.

Significantly, verse 31 serves as the direct theological gateway to 5:1 — "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery." The freedom declared in 4:31 ("we are children of the free woman") becomes the imperative of 5:1 ("stand firm in this freedom"). Indicative grounds imperative — theological identity calls forth ethical response.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 4:17–31

Passage Summary: Passage Summary: Galatians 4:17–31 completes the personal appeal (vv.17–20) and then introduces the most extended OT exegesis in the letter — the Hagar-Sarah allegory (vv.21–31). Paul uses the Genesis narrative of Abraham's two sons to construct a two-column typological framework: Hagar/Sinai/law/slavery/present Jerusalem vs. Sarah/promise/Spirit/freedom/heavenly Jerusalem. The passage closes with the declaration that believers are children of the free woman, providing the theological foundation for the call to freedom in 5:1.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

I. Exposing the Agitators' Method (vv.17–18). Paul provides a diagnostic of the Judaizers' pastoral strategy: flattery without noble motivation. They use ζηλόω (devoted courting) to create felt need and dependency, with the goal of securing the Galatians' loyalty to themselves. Against this, Paul affirms that devoted zeal is genuinely good — when it serves the truth, not the zeal-er's self-interest.

II. The Mother in Labor (vv.19–20). The birth-pang metaphor of v.19 is the emotional peak of the personal appeal: Paul is in labor a second time, agonizing until Christ is formed in his congregants. His desire to be physically present (v.20) and his frank confession of pastoral perplexity (ἀποροῦμαι) frame the allegory that follows as the product of genuine pastoral desperation — a last resort to reach those who are slipping away.

III. The Allegorical Framework (vv.21–27). Paul challenges Torah-desirers to hear what the Torah itself says (v.21). He identifies the two women of Genesis as two covenants (v.24): Hagar = Sinai covenant = present Jerusalem = slavery; Sarah (implied) = promise covenant = heavenly Jerusalem = freedom. The Isaiah 54:1 quotation (v.27) confirms that the apparently barren, desolate woman (the free Jerusalem) will have far more children than the institutionally established one.

IV. Direct Application and Scriptural Verdict (vv.28–31). The Galatian believers are children of promise like Isaac (v.28). Just as Ishmael persecuted Isaac, so the flesh-born persecute the Spirit-born now (v.29). Scripture's own word is the verdict: cast out the slave woman and her son — the two cannot co-inherit (v.30). Conclusion: we are not children of the slave but of the free woman (v.31).

II. Key Theological Themes

1. Paul's Allegorical Method (v.24). The use of ἀλληγορέω has generated extensive discussion. Paul is not employing Alexandrian allegory (Philo's method of replacing the historical sense with a timeless philosophical meaning). His method is closer to typology: the historical events of Genesis are real and carry their plain meaning, but they also encode a pattern that finds its eschatological fulfillment in the Christ-event and the community of believers. Paul reads Scripture as a forward-pointing narrative whose deepest logic is Christological and eschatological. The Genesis story was always about more than Hagar and Sarah.

2. The Two Jerusalems (vv.25–26). The contrast between the present Jerusalem and the Jerusalem above is theologically revolutionary. For Paul's Jewish contemporaries, Jerusalem was the eschatological center — the place to which the nations would stream in the last days (Isa 2:2-3; Mic 4:1-2). Paul inverts this: the earthly Jerusalem, as long as it is organized around Sinai-Torah as the governing principle of covenantal standing, is on the wrong side of the eschatological divide. It belongs to the old age, the slavery column. The real eschatological Jerusalem is not a city to be captured or rebuilt but a reality already existing in heaven, whose citizenship believers already hold (cf. Phil 3:20).

3. Σάρξ and Πνεῦμα as Covenantal Categories (vv.23, 29). The flesh/Spirit contrast in this passage is not primarily anthropological (inner vs. outer, spiritual vs. material) but covenantal and eschatological. Κατὰ σάρκα describes what operates within the sphere of natural, unaided human capacity — including Torah observance as a religious achievement. Κατὰ πνεῦμα describes what operates within the sphere of divine initiative, promise, and Spirit-power. The two sons represent two economies of relationship with God, not two parts of human nature.

4. Persecution as Typological Confirmation (v.29). Paul's appeal to the Ishmael-Isaac conflict is not merely apologetic — it is interpretive. The fact that the flesh-born persecute the Spirit-born is not accidental but patterned. It confirms that the Galatian church, under pressure from the Judaizers, is in the right line. Persecution by the Sinai-column validates their place in the Isaac-column. This is a remarkable inversion of normal social logic: being pressured and harassed becomes evidence of being the legitimate heir.

5. The Finality of the Verdict: Cast Out (v.30). Paul does not call for a negotiated settlement or a both/and approach. The Scripture's word is expulsion: the slave and her son cannot co-inherit with the free woman's son. The application is not that individual Judaizers are to be expelled from the church (though cf. 5:12!), but that the covenantal system they represent — Torah observance as the basis of standing before God — must be expelled from the Galatians' understanding of their own identity. There is no room for a partial Torah-plus-Christ gospel. The inheritance is undivided and belongs entirely to the children of promise.

6. Freedom as Identity, Not Achievement (v.31). The concluding declaration "we are children of the free woman" is an identity statement, not an ethical imperative. Paul is not calling the Galatians to become free or to earn their freedom; he is reminding them of what they already are. Freedom is their status, their birthright as children of the heavenly Jerusalem. The imperative will come in 5:1 ("stand firm therefore") — but it is grounded in and flows from the indicative of 4:31. Christian ethics in Paul is always indicative-then-imperative: be what you are.

III. Canonical Significance and Historical Reception

The Hagar-Sarah allegory has had an outsized influence in Christian interpretation. Origen (c. 185–253) used it as a paradigm for allegorical biblical interpretation more broadly, arguing that the literal/historical sense corresponds to Hagar while the spiritual/allegorical sense corresponds to Sarah. Augustine engaged it extensively in his anti-Donatist and anti-Pelagian writings, reading the two sons as representing the two cities — the earthly city of self-love and the heavenly city of God-love — a reading that would shape his entire City of God.

Luther found in this passage one of the clearest statements of the law/gospel distinction. The two covenants are not sequential phases of a single plan but fundamentally different principles of relating to God — one through human performance, one through divine promise. Any mixing of the two produces the confusion Paul is combating in Galatia.

In modern scholarship, J. Louis Martyn (Galatians, AB) argues that the allegory is not merely typological but participationist — believers do not merely resemble Isaac but are actually incorporated into the Isaac-line through their union with Christ the Seed. N.T. Wright reads the passage within a larger narrative of Israel's exile and restoration: the present Jerusalem remains in exile (slavery); the church, as the eschatological Israel reconstituted in Christ, is the restored/free Jerusalem of Isaiah's promise. Both readings have exegetical merit and are not mutually exclusive.

The verse ἄχρι οὗ μορφωθῇ Χριστὸς ἐν ὑμῖν (v.19) has been particularly significant for the theology of sanctification. The formation of Christ within believers became central to mystical and pietist traditions (Thomas à Kempis, Imitation of Christ; John Wesley's emphasis on entire sanctification; the Celtic spirituality tradition's Christ in you). Paul's birth-pang metaphor suggests that this formation is a process — ongoing, often painful, never complete in the present age — but genuinely real and the goal of all apostolic ministry.
