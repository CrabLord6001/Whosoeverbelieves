Introduction to Galatians 1:11–24

Paul now argues the claim he asserted in v. 1. His gospel is not a human tradition handed down to him; it came by revelation of Jesus Christ. The proof is biographical, and its force lies in the itinerary: he did not go up to Jerusalem after his call, but into Arabia; his first visit to Jerusalem came three years later and lasted a fortnight; he saw only Peter and James; and the Judean churches knew him by reputation alone. A man whose message depended on Jerusalem's authorisation would have gone there first and stayed longer.

Γνωρίζω introduces a formal declaration — Paul is putting something on record, not making conversation. The vocative ἀδελφοί is worth noticing immediately after the anathemas of vv. 8–9: the curse fell on those preaching another gospel, and the Galatians are still addressed as brothers.

The thesis is stated negatively: the gospel he preached is οὐ … κατὰ ἄνθρωπον, not of human kind or standard. The Byzantine text opens with δέ where NA28 reads γάρ; the difference is between a transition and a ground, and the sense of the paragraph is unaffected.

The denial is precise and twofold. παρέλαβον is the technical term for receiving transmitted tradition — the same verb Paul uses positively in 1 Cor 15:3 of the resurrection tradition. Here he denies it: the gospel did not reach him by that route. οὔτε ἐδιδάχθην adds that he was not catechised into it either.

The positive is δι’ ἀποκαλύψεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ. The genitive may be subjective (a revelation given by Jesus Christ) or objective (a revelation whose content is Jesus Christ); v. 16 — "to reveal his Son in me" — tips the balance toward the objective sense, though both are true of the Damascus road.

The biographical argument begins, and Paul starts with what the Galatians already know by reputation: τὴν ἐμὴν ἀναστροφήν ποτε ἐν τῷ Ἰουδαϊσμῷ. Ἰουδαϊσμός is rare in the NT and denotes the Jewish way of life as a bounded, defined system — the very system the agitators want the Galatians to enter.

The two imperfects are the point: ἐδίωκον and ἐπόρθουν describe sustained action, not a single episode. πορθέω is a strong verb used of sacking a city. With καθ’ ὑπερβολήν — "beyond all measure" — Paul is not minimising his past but making it evidence: a man on that trajectory does not arrive at this gospel by human persuasion.

προέκοπτον, another imperfect, describes a career in progress. Paul was not a marginal figure drifting toward Christianity; he was outpacing πολλοὺς συνηλικιώτας — many of his own contemporaries — within Judaism.

The cause is given in the participle: he was περισσοτέρως ζηλωτὴς … τῶν πατρικῶν μου παραδόσεων, more exceedingly zealous for his ancestral traditions. The genitive is objective — the traditions are the object of the zeal. The irony is deliberate and aimed at the agitators: the one person in this dispute with impeccable credentials in Torah zeal is the one arguing that Torah observance cannot justify.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads [ὁ θεὸς], the Byzantine text has ὁ θεὸς.

The sentence that began in v. 15 does not reach its main verb until v. 16. εὐδόκησεν — "it pleased" — makes God's good pleasure, not Paul's merit or search, the cause of everything that follows.

The two participles are drawn from prophetic call narratives. ὁ ἀφορίσας με ἐκ κοιλίας μητρός μου echoes Isaiah 49:1 and Jeremiah 1:5, where the servant and the prophet are set apart before birth. Paul is placing his own call in that line, which is a considerable claim and exactly the claim his opponents denied.

καλέσας διὰ τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ names the means. The same grace the Galatians are deserting (1:6) is the grace by which Paul was called, and there is a quiet argument in that overlap. The Byzantine text makes ὁ θεός explicit where NA28 brackets it as doubtful; the subject is unmistakable either way.

ἀποκαλύψαι τὸν υἱὸν αὐτοῦ ἐν ἐμοί states the purpose of the setting-apart. The preposition is ἐν, "in me," not simply "to me" — the revelation was not only something Paul saw but something he became, a point borne out by the ἵνα clause: the revealing was in order that he might preach Christ among the Gentiles. Call and commission are one act.

The main verb at last: εὐθέως οὐ προσανεθέμην σαρκὶ καὶ αἵματι. προσανατίθημι means to lay a matter before someone for their verdict. Paul did not submit his gospel for human ratification — not because he despised the Jerusalem apostles, but because a revealed gospel does not require it.

The itinerary now carries the argument. οὐδὲ ἀνῆλθον εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα — he did not go up to Jerusalem, the one move a man seeking authorisation would have made first.

Instead, ἀπῆλθον εἰς Ἀραβίαν. Arabia here is the Nabataean kingdom, away from Judea in the opposite direction. Paul says nothing about what he did there, and speculation that it was a period of solitary reflection goes beyond the text; what matters for the argument is simply that it was not Jerusalem.

The return εἰς Δαμασκόν — back to where the call occurred, not on to the mother church — completes the picture.

Ἔπειτα μετὰ ἔτη τρία establishes the interval. Three years passed before Paul saw Jerusalem at all, by which time his gospel was long since formed and preached.

The verb for the visit is chosen carefully: ἱστορῆσαι Πέτρον. ἱστορέω means to visit in order to get to know someone, to make an acquaintance. It is not a verb of receiving instruction. The fifteen days reinforce it — long enough for conversation and friendship, far too short for an apprenticeship in doctrine.

The Byzantine text reads Πέτρον where NA28 has Κηφᾶν, the Aramaic form. Both name the same man, and Paul uses both forms elsewhere in this very letter (2:7–8 against 2:9, 11, 14).

The argument of 1:11–24 is therefore double-edged, and both edges matter. Paul owed the Jerusalem churches nothing for his gospel — and he was never in conflict with them either. They glorified God in him. The agitators' implied claim, that Paul preaches a diminished gospel at odds with the mother church, is false on both counts.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 1:11–24

Passage Summary: Galatians 1:11–24 defends the divine origin of Paul's gospel by narrating what he did after his call. The movements — away from Jerusalem into Arabia, a brief first visit three years later, then Syria and Cilicia — are selected to show that his message was not received from the Jerusalem apostles, while the closing note that the Judean churches glorified God in him shows he was never at odds with them either.

I. Rhetorical and Structural Architecture

A. Thesis (vv. 11–12). Paul states what he will prove: the gospel he preaches is not κατὰ ἄνθρωπον, received neither by transmitted tradition (παρέλαβον) nor by instruction (ἐδιδάχθην), but through revelation of Jesus Christ.

B. The argument from biography (vv. 13–24). The proof is a sequence of movements, and the sequence is the argument: former life in Judaism (vv. 13–14) → call by God's good pleasure (vv. 15–16a) → not to Jerusalem, but Arabia and Damascus (vv. 16b–17) → Jerusalem at last, three years later, for fifteen days, seeing two men (vv. 18–19) → Syria and Cilicia (v. 21) → unknown by face in Judea (v. 22). Each step widens the distance between Paul's gospel and any human authorisation of it.

C. The oath and the closing report (vv. 20, 24). The oath in v. 20 marks how much weight the itinerary bears, and the report in vv. 23–24 supplies independent corroboration: the Judean churches described Paul's past in his own terms and glorified God for his present. The paragraph thus ends by establishing both his independence from Jerusalem and his harmony with it.

II. Key Theological Themes

1. Revelation over transmission (v. 12). Paul distinguishes two ways a message can be acquired — handed down or revealed — and places his gospel firmly in the second. This does not disparage tradition, which Paul elsewhere receives and passes on gladly (1 Cor 15:3), but it does mean his gospel cannot be corrected by appeal to a human chain of custody.

2. Prophetic call and divine initiative (v. 15). The language of being set apart from the womb and called through grace places Paul in the line of Isaiah's servant and Jeremiah. Everything in the passage flows from εὐδόκησεν — God's good pleasure — and nothing from Paul's merit, which is a working illustration of the grace the letter is defending.

3. Grace as the origin of the apostle as well as the message (vv. 6, 15). Paul was called διὰ τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ, by the same grace the Galatians are deserting. The man making the argument is himself an instance of it.

4. Independence without rivalry (vv. 17–24). Paul is careful to establish distance from Jerusalem without establishing opposition to it. He saw Peter and James; the Judean churches glorified God in him. The point is that his gospel did not come from them, not that it differs from theirs — a distinction chapter 2 will press much further.

III. Historical and Canonical Significance

This passage is the primary source for the chronology of Paul's early years, and it is the main point of contact — and tension — with Acts. The three years before the first Jerusalem visit (v. 18), the Arabian sojourn (v. 17, unmentioned in Acts), and the Syria–Cilicia years (v. 21) have to be set alongside Acts 9:19–30, and harmonising the two accounts is a standing problem in Pauline chronology. The usual solution treats Acts 9 as compressing a period Paul here expands for his own argumentative purposes.

The passage also bears on the question of how apostolic authority was understood in the earliest decades. Paul does not claim to be independent of the apostolic circle in fellowship or in doctrine; he claims that his commission and his message came directly from the risen Christ. That claim, made under oath in v. 20, is the foundation on which the confrontation at Antioch in 2:11–21 becomes possible — only an apostle whose authority is not derived from Jerusalem could withstand Peter to his face.
