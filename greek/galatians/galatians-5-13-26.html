Introduction to Galatians 5:13–26

Freedom is not a licence, and Paul spends this passage saying what it is for. The whole law is fulfilled in loving one's neighbour; the alternative is a congregation biting and devouring itself. The governing instruction is to walk by the Spirit, set out against the opposing pull of the flesh. The two lists that follow — the works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit — describe two ways of living that are already open to the believer, not two ways of obtaining eternal life.

The γάρ links back to v.1: freedom is not a new topic but the continuation and application of the charter just stated. The aorist passive ἐκλήθητε grounds the exhortation in the divine calling — God himself called the Galatians into freedom, and this calling is the basis for what follows. The word ἀφορμή is a military term: a base of operations, a beachhead from which a campaign is launched. The flesh is portrayed not as passive weakness but as an aggressor looking for a foothold. Freedom, if misunderstood as licence, gives the flesh exactly the base it needs to mount its campaign.

The positive command διὰ τῆς ἀγάπης δουλεύετε ἀλλήλοις is deliberately paradoxical: freedom expresses itself as mutual service — a voluntary enslavement to one another through love. This turns the law's external demand into an internal impulse. Paul is anticipating the summary of v.14: the entire law is fulfilled in the love-command. The Galatians do not need to submit to the Mosaic law to be loving — love, operating through the Spirit (cf. 5:22), accomplishes what the law demanded without operating under the law's coercive and condemnatory force. For Free Grace theology, this verse is the pivot of the whole paraenetic section: freedom from law is not freedom from obligation to neighbour but freedom to serve from a transformed heart rather than a legal obligation.

The perfect passive πεπλήρωται is theologically loaded. It does not mean "is summarised" (though that is a secondary implication) but "has been brought to its intended completion." The law finds its telos in love. This echoes Jesus' teaching in Matt 22:37–40 (the double love command) and Rom 13:8–10 (love is the fulfilment of the law), and raises the same question: in what sense does love fulfil the law? The answer Paul implies throughout is that the Spirit produces in the believer what the law demanded but could never produce — genuine love for God and neighbour — so that those who walk by the Spirit (v.16) are not under law (v.18) precisely because the law's purpose is being achieved in them through a higher principle.

The citation is Lev 19:18 LXX verbatim. Paul's choice of the neighbour-love command rather than the God-love command (Deut 6:5) is deliberate: the horizontal dimension of love — service to one another — is exactly what he called for in v.13 (δουλεύετε ἀλλήλοις) and what is being destroyed by the factional strife of v.15. The whole law is not being declared irrelevant — its moral content is being re-grounded in love as its animating principle. For Free Grace exegesis, this verse clarifies that the paraenetic section is not about keeping the Mosaic law as a means of standing before God but about living out the love that the Spirit produces, which happens to fulfil what the law was aiming at all along.

The animal imagery of δάκνετε καὶ κατεσθίετε — biting and devouring — is vivid and suggests a community already in conflict, not merely a theoretical warning. The first-class condition treats it as current reality: the Galatians are in fact already engaged in this destructive behaviour, most likely fanned by the circumcision controversy which has divided the community along Jewish/Gentile lines. The escalation from δάκνω (biting — an initial attack) to κατεσθίω (devouring — complete consumption) and then to ἀναλόω (being destroyed — total annihilation) traces a trajectory from factional squabbling to communal self-destruction.

The verse functions as a reductio ad absurdum of the agitators' programme: if freedom from law leads to licence, and licence to mutual aggression, the community will destroy itself. The alternative — the love-service of v.13 — is not merely more virtuous but more rational: it alone preserves the community. The implicit answer to "why walk by the Spirit?" is partly given here: because the alternative is mutual consumption. For Free Grace exegesis, this verse is important as evidence that Paul's pastoral concern throughout 5:13–26 is communal health and experiential flourishing, not the soteriological status of individual members.

Verse 16 is the programmatic statement of the entire section 5:16–26. The command πνεύματι περιπατεῖτε (present imperative — "keep on walking by the Spirit") introduces the pneumatological solution to the problem stated in vv.13–15: the alternative to both legal obligation and fleshly licence is Spirit-directed living. The dative πνεύματι is most naturally instrumental ("by the Spirit") though a dative of sphere ("in the Spirit") is also possible; both ideas are present — the Spirit is the means of the walk and the environment within which it takes place.

The consequence clause οὐ μὴ τελέσητε uses the strongest possible Greek negation — the double negative οὐ μή with the aorist subjunctive — to state an absolute promise rather than a mere possibility: those who walk by the Spirit will certainly not fulfil the flesh's desire. This is a promise, not another imperative. The relationship between Spirit-walking and flesh-mastery is not one of moral effort supplemented by the Spirit's help; it is one of displacement — where the Spirit governs the walk, the flesh's programme simply cannot complete itself. This is crucial for Free Grace interpretation: Paul is not calling the Galatians to strenuous moral effort but to Spirit-dependence, which produces the very fruit described in vv.22–23.

Textual note: Where NA28 reads γὰρ ἀλλήλοις ἀντίκειται, the Byzantine text has δὲ ἀντίκειται ἀλλήλοις. Where NA28 reads ἐὰν, the Byzantine text has ἂν.

Verse 17 is one of the most debated verses in Paul's ethical teaching. The conflict between σάρξ and πνεῦμα is presented as mutual and ongoing — both parties are active, both are described with the same verb ἐπιθυμέω (to desire, crave), and they are fundamentally opposed. The question is: who is experiencing this conflict? Three main views exist: (1) it describes the pre-conversion state (Augustine's early reading, later abandoned); (2) it describes the experience of the Christian who is not yet fully yielded to the Spirit (a pietist reading); (3) it describes the normal experience of every believer as long as the flesh is present (the mainstream Reformed and Lutheran reading, also favoured by most Free Grace interpreters).

The final clause ἵνα μὴ ἃ ἐὰν θέλητε ταῦτα ποιῆτε is the crux. The ἵνα is most naturally telic or consecutive: the mutual opposition of flesh and Spirit has the result (or purpose) that you cannot simply do whatever you wish. This has often been read as pessimistic — you are paralysed by the conflict. But read positively, it means that the flesh's agenda is being frustrated by the Spirit, and the Spirit's agenda is being frustrated by the flesh. The believer is thus not left to the flesh's mercy: the Spirit is a genuine combatant. The verse grounds the promise of v.16 (the flesh's desire will not be fulfilled) by explaining the mechanism: the Spirit actively opposes the flesh's programme, ensuring it cannot run unchecked. The practical implication is v.18: be led by the Spirit, which tips the balance decisively toward the Spirit's side.

This brief verse is one of the clearest statements in Paul of the relationship between Spirit-leading and law. The passive ἄγεσθε ("you are being led") is significant — the Spirit is the active agent, the believer the one being guided. The image is of the Spirit as a shepherd or guide leading the flock, not of the believer striving to follow. This passivity (in the sense of receptivity) is the opposite of the self-directed effort implied by life under the law. The condition is first-class — it treats Spirit-leading as the actual reality for the Galatians, not a mere hypothesis.

The apodosis οὐκ ἐστὲ ὑπὸ νόμον directly echoes 5:1 (the yoke of slavery) and 4:4–5 (Christ came to redeem those under law). To be led by the Spirit is to have moved into the freedom-sphere described in v.1 — and in that sphere, law is not the governing principle. This does not mean the Spirit-led believer is morally unaccountable, as v.19–21 will make clear; it means they are not operating under the law's system of demand, condemnation, and curse. The law's commands may be fulfilled (v.14) but its coercive power is not the animating force of the Spirit-led life. For Free Grace exegesis, this verse is the pneumatological resolution of the whole law-grace antithesis: the solution to the Galatians' temptation to return to the law is not better moral effort but greater Spirit-dependence.

Paul introduces the vice list with the adjective φανερά — "manifest, obvious." The works of the flesh do not require detection; they are self-evident when present. The list that follows across vv.19–21 contains fifteen or sixteen items (the count varies slightly depending on textual tradition) grouped loosely into four categories: sexual sins (v.19), religious/spiritual sins (v.20a), social/relational sins (vv.20b–21a), and sins of excess (v.21b). The first triad — πορνεία (sexual immorality in general), ἀκαθαρσία (ritual and moral impurity), ἀσέλγεια (licentiousness, shameless excess) — covers the sexual domain from the act itself through its motivating spirit to its public expression. These are listed first, probably because sexual immorality was the most visibly destructive in the Galatian context, but also because these are precisely the sins that a misunderstanding of freedom as licence (v.13) would most readily produce.

The second cluster pivots from sexual to spiritual and social sins. εἰδωλολατρεία and φαρμακεία (idolatry and sorcery) represent the religious dimension of fleshly life — the orientation of the self toward powers other than God. φαρμακεία in the first century encompassed the use of drugs, potions, and magical practices in religious and healing contexts; it is the root of the English "pharmacy" but here carries strongly negative connotations of occult manipulation. The seven social vices that follow — enmities, strifes, jealousies, rages, rivalries, divisions, factions — are striking in their specific relevance to the Galatian situation. The community being torn apart by the circumcision controversy is exhibiting exactly these flesh-works, and Paul's list is partly diagnostic: the agitators' programme has produced fleshly fruit, not spiritual.

The warning at the end of v.21 is the most theologically significant statement in the vice list: βασιλείαν θεοῦ οὐ κληρονομήσουσιν — "will not inherit the kingdom of God." This warning has been read in three main ways. The traditional Reformation reading takes it as a test of genuine regeneration: those who habitually practise these things show by their behaviour that they were never truly saved. The Arminian reading takes it as a warning that genuine believers can lose their salvation through persistent sin. The Free Grace reading, developed extensively by Hodges and Zane Hodges, distinguishes between entering the kingdom (a matter of regeneration by faith) and inheriting the kingdom (a matter of experiential participation in the kingdom's blessings, potentially at the messianic judgment).

Several features of the text support the Free Grace inheritance reading. First, the verb is κληρονομήσουσιν — "will inherit" — not "will enter." Inheritance in Paul is consistently linked to sonship and faithfulness (cf. 4:7; Rom 8:17; 1 Cor 6:9–10), and throughout Galatians the heir-language distinguishes between those who are sons (all believers, 3:26; 4:5–7) and those who will receive the inheritance (those who remain in the promise-sphere and live accordingly). Second, the present participle πράσσοντες (habitually practising) suggests a settled lifestyle pattern, not an isolated sin — but even this does not automatically imply non-regeneracy, since Paul elsewhere addresses genuine believers who engage in flesh-works (1 Cor 5:1–5; 6:9–11). Third, the back-reference καθὼς καὶ προεῖπον ("just as I forewarned you") shows this was a pastoral warning given during Paul's founding visits — addressed to those he was bringing to faith, not to unbelievers.

The singular καρπὸς ("fruit" — not "fruits") stands in deliberate contrast to the plural ἔργα ("works") of the flesh. The works of the flesh are many and fragmented; the fruit of the Spirit is one — a unified, organic whole that grows from a single source. This is not a list of virtues to be cultivated by moral effort but a description of what the Spirit naturally produces in the life of the believer who walks by the Spirit (v.16) and is led by the Spirit (v.18). The agricultural metaphor implies growth over time and dependence on the vine rather than autonomous effort.

ἀγάπη heads the list — as it did in v.14's summary of the whole law and in v.6's description of faith's operation. Love is the primary fruit from which the others flow. The nine qualities are sometimes grouped as three triads: (1) God-ward: love, joy, peace; (2) other-ward: patience, kindness, goodness; (3) self-ward: faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. This is a helpful heuristic but not explicitly indicated by Paul's grammar. The seventh item, πίστις, likely means "faithfulness" or "reliability" here rather than "faith" in the saving sense — it is a quality of character produced by the Spirit, the trustworthiness that sustains relationships, though the semantic range of the word encompasses both.

The phrase κατὰ τῶν τοιούτων οὐκ ἔστιν νόμος — "against such things there is no law" — is one of Paul's most concise and rhetorically satisfying conclusions. The construction κατά + genitive means "against" in the sense of bringing a charge or levying a prohibition. No law stands opposed to love, joy, peace, gentleness, and self-control. The statement is partly polemical and partly celebratory: polemically, it answers the agitators' implied claim that law is necessary for moral order — in fact, the Spirit-produced life exceeds what law can demand or achieve; celebratorily, it declares the Spirit-led believer to be beyond law's jurisdiction, not because they are lawless but because they have been brought into a sphere where law's work is already done and more.

The final fruit ἐγκράτεια (self-control) is particularly apt as the conclusion of the list in this context: it is precisely the quality that answers the objection of v.13 (freedom as licence for the flesh). Self-control produced by the Spirit is not the white-knuckled effort of the person who is under law and failing to keep it; it is the natural restraint of a person in whom the Spirit has settled and is governing. The list as a whole describes what it looks like to "not fulfil the desire of the flesh" (v.16) — not through prohibition and penalty but through the positive displacement of fleshly impulses by Spirit-produced qualities.

The phrase οἱ τοῦ Χριστοῦ — "those of Christ," those who belong to Christ — defines the subject as all believers, not a spiritual elite. The aorist ἐσταύρωσαν points to a definitive past event — the crucifixion of the flesh is something that happened, not something that is still being accomplished. This echoes the language of 2:19–20 ("I have been crucified with Christ") and 6:14 ("the world has been crucified to me and I to the world"). The crucifixion of the flesh is not a moral achievement but a positional reality — it occurred when the believer was united with Christ in his death.

This verse does not mean the flesh no longer exerts influence — v.17 makes clear that the conflict continues. Rather, it means the flesh's authority has been terminated: its passions and desires no longer have legal claim over those who belong to Christ. The practical implication is that believers are not fighting to achieve the flesh's defeat; they are fighting to enforce a defeat that has already occurred. This is the indicative foundation (the flesh has been crucified) for the subsequent imperatives of vv.25–26 (walk and live by the Spirit). For Free Grace exegesis, this verse is the ontological ground of the ethical programme: the flesh's crucifixion is not the reward for good behaviour but the presupposition of it.

Sentence Diagram — Galatians 5:25

Verse 25 is one of Paul's sharpest indicative-to-imperative pivots. The verb ζῶμεν ("we live") refers to the new life given by the Spirit at regeneration — the believer already lives by the Spirit in the ontological sense (cf. 2:20; Rom 8:10–11). The hortatory subjunctive στοιχῶμεν calls for the practical alignment of daily conduct with that ontological reality. The verb στοιχέω is distinct from περιπατέω (walk, v.16): it means to march in line, to keep in step, to follow in a straight row — a military or procession metaphor implying ordered, disciplined movement in concert with others.

The first-class condition treats Spirit-life as the already-given reality ("since we live by the Spirit") and the hortatory subjunctive turns that reality into a claim on daily conduct: let the walk match the life. This is the fundamental structure of Pauline ethics throughout — not "try harder to become what God wants" but "become in your conduct what you already are by grace." For Free Grace theology, this verse is the programmatic statement of the sanctification theology implied throughout the section: the Spirit who gives life also directs the walk, and the call to walk by the Spirit is a call to cooperative alignment with what the Spirit is already doing in and for the believer.

The final verse of the chapter functions as a hinge between the Spirit-flesh contrast of vv.16–25 and the community-ethics of 6:1–5. The adjective κενόδοξοι — "vainglorious, conceited, empty of real glory" — is a compound: κενός (empty) + δόξα (glory). It describes the person who pursues glory that has no substance — reputation built on comparative diminishment of others. The two participles unpack the two social expressions of vanity: προκαλούμενοι (provoking, challenging — the behaviour of those who feel superior) and φθονοῦντες (envying — the behaviour of those who feel inferior). Together they describe the full spectrum of the pride-driven community: those on top provoke, those on the bottom envy, and the whole structure is held together by the empty glory that neither side actually possesses.

This is a precise diagnosis of what has gone wrong in Galatia. The circumcision controversy has created exactly this dynamic: the circumcised party challenging the uncircumcised (provoking), the uncircumcised resenting the circumcised (envying). Paul's application of the Spirit-walk principle to this specific social pathology shows that his ethical concern is not abstract but diagnostically targeted. The remedy is neither law nor licence but the fruit of the Spirit — love that serves rather than challenges, and joy and peace that have no need of empty glory because the believer's true glory is in the cross (6:14). For Free Grace theology, v.26 makes clear that the paraenetic section is primarily concerned with communal flourishing in the present age, not with conditions for eschatological salvation.

Overall Exegesis: Galatians 5:13–26

Passage Summary: Galatians 5:13–26 defines what Christian freedom is for. It is not an opening for the flesh but the ground of serving one another in love, since the whole law is fulfilled in loving one's neighbour. The governing instruction is to walk by the Spirit, and the two lists that follow — the works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit — describe two ways of living open to the believer, not two ways of obtaining eternal life.
