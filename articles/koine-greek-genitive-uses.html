Koine Greek Genitive Uses

Thirty ways Greek can mean “of” — and how to tell which one Paul meant.

Jonathan Crow 10/7/26

I have published a polished version of my Greek notes from year one of my seminary courses. Chapter 2 outlines the five cases of Greek nouns. In English the noun doesn’t change based on whether it is the subject or object of the sentence. However, Greek changes the ending part of the word based on its purpose in the sentence.

The genitive case shows the specific relationship to another noun, usually translated with “of” between them. In English we typically think of possession or close association, which is mostly true in Koine Greek — like the “house of Simon and Andrew” (τὴν οἰκίαν Σίμωνος καὶ Ἀνδρέου) in Mark 1:29.

While a simple “of” works for most translations, in year two of seminary Greek, you will learn there are actually around 30 different possible uses of the genitive that connote all kinds of nuances. Knowing the context and the function of the head noun, what the phrase actually describes, helps interpreters decide which best fits the individual meanings in the text. The genitive phrase usually acts as an adjective modifying the head noun in some way — though other times acts as verbal phrases, adverbial phrases, ablatival senses, or in special uses when no other case is expected.

In this article, I am going to explain a few genitive uses from each category with some examples followed by an exegetical exercise as an illustration.1 Keep in mind: I am only highlighting some to illustrate the complexity of translation choices. I plan to upload a chart on the Greek page as a quick reference on all of them. The tests are general rules to help guide you to the most likely use, though the context and other factors should be weighed too.

Adjectival Genitives

First, genitive phrases can function as adjectives that act as some kind of description of the head noun.

Attributive Genitive

The attributive genitive describes an attribute or innate quality of the head noun. In other words, this genitive works like an adjectival phrase, often carrying more emphasis than a simple adjective would.

Identification Test: If the genitive noun can be turned into an adjective modifying the head noun, then it is most likely an attributive genitive.

Clear examples include “the judge of unrighteousness”, that is, an unrighteous judge (ὁ κριτὴς τῆς ἀδικίας, Luke 18:6). Others include spirit of gentleness or “gentle spirit” (πνεύματι πρᾳότητος, Galatians 6:1) and body of sin or “sinful body” (σῶμα τῆς ἁμαρτίας, Romans 6:6).

Genitive of Content

Very simply, the genitive of content specifies the contents related to the head noun, whether in another noun or verbal phrase.

Identification Test: If the word it modifies is a noun, try replacing of with “full of” or “containing”. In English, we might say a bucket of balls, meaning a bucket full of balls. However, if the modified word is a verb, then use “full with”, as in a “day of shopping” describing a day full of shopping.

Think of John 2:7, where it is understood as “jars full with water” (γεμίσατε τὰς ὑδρίας ὕδατος) because it describes the imperative command Jesus gives. For nouns, John 21:8 is a good example, the disciples dragging a net “full of fish” (τὸ δίκτυον τῶν ἰχθύων).

Genitive of Apposition (Epexegetical / Definition)

The substantive in the genitive case refers to the same thing as its head noun, though it may not be an exact equivalence. Typically, the genitive states a specific example that is part of the larger category. This is especially true when the head noun is ambiguous or metaphorical.

This use differs slightly from the simple apposition genitive, that instead does not name a specific example but gives a different designation or relation of the one named — like “God, the Father”.

Identification Test: Most genitives of apposition are translated with of. However, if the of can be safely replaced with “which is”, “that is”, “namely”, or in the case of personal nouns “who is” and the same sense remains, it is likely a genitive of apposition.

Good examples include the “land of Egypt” (τὴν γῆν Αἰγύπτου, Acts 7:11), “the sign of circumcision” (σημεῖον ἔλαβεν περιτομῆς, Romans 4:11), and the “feast of Unleavened Bread” (ἡ ἑορτὴ τῶν ἀζύμων, Luke 22:1). For a metaphorical use, consider “the temple of His body” (τοῦ ναοῦ τοῦ σώματος αὐτοῦ) in John 2:21 is certainly understood as “the temple, that is His body.”

Genitive of Destination or Purpose

The genitive of destination or purpose, as the category implies, indicates the head noun’s direction or the recipient of it. This use is somewhat rare in the New Testament.

Identification Test: If the “of” can be safely replaced with “for” or “to” and retain the same sense, it is likely a genitive of destination. Though that may not necessarily describe the intended purpose, “destined for”, “towards”, or “with the purpose of” may convey what’s not accidental or by chance.

1 Corinthians 1:18 is an interesting example, since “the word of the cross” (Ὅ λόγος γὰρ ὁ τοῦ σταυροῦ) can mean both the message which proceeds out of cross or the message which leads to the cross, a distinction between destination and purpose. Then again, it might also refer to the message which concerns the cross, a genitive of reference or respect.

Less controversial is Romans 8:36, quoting Psalm 44:22, “...we are accounted as sheep for the slaughter” (πρόβατα σφαγῆς).

Ablatival Genitives

Unlike adjectival genitives that describe the head noun, ablatival genitives function by indicating their origin, source, or point of reference to something else. Instead of using “of” as a default translation choice, they use prepositional phrases like “from”, “out of”, “than” (in comparisons). The genitive word stands in for the prepositions themselves.

Genitive of Source

The source genitive use answers the question of where the head noun originates or derives from. It’s a relatively rare category in Koine Greek.

Identification Test: If the standard “of” can be replaced or paraphrased as “out of”, “dependent on”, or “sourced in” with the same sense, it likely is a genitive of source.

In John 1:29, the genitive phrase “Lamb of God” (ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ) tells us the source of the Lamb, which falls under the genitive of source. The text may well mirror Genesis 22:8. Other interpreters view it as a simple possessive genitive, as the “Lamb who belongs to God”. From the context though, John narrates that the Word became incarnate, taking on flesh, being begotten, eternally sent, from the Father (John 1:14) — fitting a genitive of source.

Another example of a source might include Romans 1:17, interpreted as “righteousness that comes from God by faith”, though the phrase δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ is heavily disputed. It could also describe a possessive (“God’s righteousness”), a kind of attributive genitive (“divine righteousness”), or a subjective genitive (“the righteousness God performs”). The disagreement centers around how the genitive noun relates back to its head noun, in this case God, whether it provides a sense of quality, status, or names an action.

Genitive of Separation

The genitive of separation tells a point of departure from a verb or sometimes the head noun. It is a rare usage in Koine Greek, as by this time the idea of separation was made explicit with ἀπό (away, from) or ἐκ (out of) as independent prepositions or verbal prefixes.

Identification Test: These genitive uses only make sense by supplying words like “away from”, “out of”, or “from” and typically rely on a verb or verbal phrase.

Acts 15:29 is a good, clear example with several genitives in a row, “...that you abstain from things sacrificed to idols (εἰδωλοθύτων), from blood (αἱματος), from things strangled (πνικτοῦ), and from sexual immorality (πορνείας)...”

Verbal Genitives

Verbal genitives are technically in the adjectival category but under their own umbrella by Daniel Wallace because they are critical and often confusing. Unlike all other descriptive genitives, verbal uses must include a verb in the head noun as a cognate, typically a transitive verbal idea (requiring a direct object).

In plain English, “The love of a mother” includes a mother containing the noun love, that functions with an inherent action — which may best follow under the next group, the subjective genitive.

Subjective Genitive

The subjective genitive functions with the subject including a verbal idea implicit in the head noun, or what is better called the “verbal noun”. Many nouns carry a verbal double meaning, like witness, fear, anger, love, and hope. Some nouns from English don’t carry over a verbal sense while they do in Greek, like king (βασιλεῦς having the same root form as the verb “I rule” βασιλεύω).

Identification Test: Attempt to convert the verbal noun related to the genitive into a verbal form and the genitive into its subject. If it works with the same sense, it likely fits as a subjective genitive. “The love of God...” in Romans 5:5 could be cleanly worded as “because God loved us...”

“The coming of the Lord”, usually parousia (παρουσία) is both an event and inherently the action of Him arriving. Thus, Matthew 24:27 and James 5:8 each use a subjective genitive. Think also of Luke 1:35, “... power of the Most High...” (δύναμις ὑψίστου) as this use may well be translated “the Most High will powerfully act.”

Objective Genitive

As the opposite reading from the subjective, the objective genitive functions as the direct object of the verbal idea implicit in the head noun.

Identification Test: Attempt to convert the verbal noun which the genitive relates into the verbal form with the genitive as its direct object. “Love of God” would then be “my love for God”, rather than “God’s love for me” as the subjective use.

There are many clear examples of the objective use, like Matthew 12:31 (blasphemy of the Holy Spirit) and Romans 3:25 (demonstration of His righteousness).

One famously disputed text is “faith of Jesus Christ” (πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ) in Galatians 2:16. In the KJV, as a wooden translation, that phrasing is correct but does not make clear the genitive type. If viewed as a subjective use, it would read “the faithfulness of Jesus Christ,” which is reflected in the NET Bible and held by some scholars, like N.T. Wright.

On the other hand, in an objective sense it should be read “faith in Jesus Christ,” where Jesus is the object of our faith (ESV, WEB, NIV, etc...). Paul seems to deliberately repeat himself later in the same verse, “we believed in Christ Jesus.” Believers are justified by a faith directed towards Jesus, believing in His promise. Also, striking parallels across Pauline literature help show consistency, such as Philippians 3:9.

Plenary Genitive

If the text allows for both the subjective and objective uses to read together without contradiction, it is possible the Biblical author intended each simultaneously as a double meaning. Thus, it would have a full or plenary genitive use. For the identification tests, both key words and verbal rephrasing apply.

The Book of Revelation begins “This is the Revelation of Jesus Christ...” (Revelation 1:1) Does it then mean the revelation concerning Christ or the revelation which comes from Christ? Most directly, Jesus dictates what John should record, but also that dictation is clearly about Jesus Himself. Both senses are likely intended.

Adverbial Genitives

Finally, the genitive may act similar to an adverb, often as a prepositional phrase. In such cases, they would relate more to the main verb or an adjective rather than any noun.

Wallace lists eight adverbial uses in the New Testament. Some relate to idioms about price or value (“two-hundred denarii worth of bread”, John 6:7), a kind of time reference (“I fast twice a week”, Luke 18:12), or indicate the place within which the verb acts (“... even death on a cross.” Philippians 2:8).

Example Exegesis: The Hearing of Faith (Galatians 3:2 & 5)

ἐξ ἔργων νόμου, ἢ ἐξ ἀκοῆς πίστεως

The Greek genitive phrase ἀκοῆς πίστεως in Galatians 3:2 and 3:5, meaning woodenly “hearing of faith,” has a number of valid translation choices. Sometimes, ἀκούω denotes literally perceiving sound or a voice (Acts 9:7). Other times, it may also have the sense of listening with attention, to pay heed, or to understand in believing (John 5:25). In the context of Galatians 3, Paul certainly means a response of faith to a message.

To determine which genitive use best fits, try fitting the key test words to substitute the “of” and evaluate how it flows with the context. In no particular order, here are my thoughts:

The genitive of content would be the hearing containing faith. The KJV leaves this reading possible, though that use deals with what is materially or metaphorically a portion of the head noun. The message of the gospel is not itself composed of faith.

If it were a genitive of apposition (or epexegetical), Paul would be asking if they received the Spirit by a hearing, which itself is faith. By saying the same thing twice for no purpose, he would be creating a strange tautology. Further, Paul builds a parallel between this hearing of faith with the “works of the law.” They should have a similar sense to retain the thematic grouping. Works are not themselves the legal system of Moses.

Perhaps a genitive of source? It would then read, “hearing which comes by faith.” This seems to align with Romans 10:16-17, only, when examined closely, is actually giving the opposite construction (“faith out of hearing” ἡ πίστις ἐξ ἀκοῆς). Again, the parallelism also fails. The Law merely presents the truth of God’s righteous standards, but does not by itself empower anyone to fulfill its requirements (i.e. originate works).

Wallace states the genitive of destination, here the sense of “hearing destined for faith”, is rare and typically restricted to modifying head nouns related to movement. He does actually give Galatians 2:7 as an example (“gospel to the uncircumcised”), but for 3:2 and 3:5 it doesn’t fit.

The objective genitive, however, is a strong candidate. In this use, the hearing can be converted into a verb with the genitive word faith as the direct object, creating “the faith you heard.” This idea has an anchor in Galatians 1:23, Paul being unknown to the Judean churches, who only heard the faith he now preaches. The NET Bible offers a dynamic equivalence with the objective genitive force in translating, “... by believing what you heard?” Their note explains faith is impersonal. The person exercising the response is the focus.

Lastly, the attributive genitive would also fit appropriately, where the hearing has the quality or is characterized by faith (“hearing with faith” ESV / NASB). This might be called the “faithful listening” and functions as a single adjectival phrase. It might pair well with “law-characterized works” and has the appeal of flowing well in English.

Personally, I am settling on the objective genitive, since it keeps the sense of faith as believing — setting up the Biblical language of Abraham, whom God accredited righteousness because he believed the promise. He simply took God at His word by being fully convinced (Romans 4:21).

Conclusion

Translating the New Testament is difficult in that it involves some necessary exegesis. Interlinears won’t always answer the challenge of determining genitive uses. I wanted to write most of the Wallace chapter in my own words to understand and identify his categories by sight reading.

Hopefully, I’ve given you a good reference to print and share around your church community. If you have any confusion (I sure did!), feel free to leave a comment and I’ll try to clear it up.

God bless!
